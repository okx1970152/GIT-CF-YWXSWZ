## Chapter Overview
This chapter transitions from perilous survival to a moment of strange, delicate wonder. Xiao Chen stumbles upon evidence that Zhao Lin’er has also entered the World of Immortality, shifting the dynamic from hunter to potential hunted. Yet the chapter’s heart lies in his encounter with a celestial horse—a jade-like foal that drinks moonlight and dances above the treetops. The tonal contrast between the grim implications of Zhao Lin’er’s presence and the serene, almost sacred beauty of the little horse deepens the novel’s atmospheric richness. It is a chapter of portent and grace, where the protagonist’s healing body mirrors the dawning of a new, more mysterious phase in his journey.

## Key Plot Points
Xiao Chen finds a white gauze scarf embroidered with a phoenix, unmistakably Zhao Lin’er’s. He deduces that she must have been swept into the World of Immortality alongside him during Lan Nuo’s void-breaking ascent. This realization reframes his situation: the royal status that once protected her is meaningless here, but she remains a formidable fighter. For now, he prioritizes recovery, knowing a confrontation is inevitable. Over the following days, Xiao Chen practices cultivation under moonlight, his silent healing punctuated by the arrival of a miniature celestial horse. The foal is no ordinary beast—it is carved from what seems like mutton-fat jade, has a single translucent horn, and can siphon moonlight essence from the air. It visits nightly, sharing the spiritual energy Xiao Chen gathers, growing bolder with each appearance. Xiao Chen exercises remarkable restraint, neither chasing nor capturing it, understanding that such divine creatures must be won through patience. By the chapter’s end, his wounds are fully healed, and a new presence approaches through the forest, setting up the next confrontation.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
The celestial horse (天马) is a classic motif in Chinese mythology, often depicted as a winged or horned steed that can traverse the heavens. The jade horn specifically evokes associations with the qilin or the White Deer, both omens of wisdom and spiritual power. The description of the foal as “carved from mutton-fat jade” ties into traditional Chinese connoisseurship of fine jade, suggesting a creature of supreme purity and rarity. Cultivators in the World of Immortality absorb moonlight essence (月华) as part of their practice, believing that the moon holds a refined yin energy that nourishes the soul and body. The fact that the little horse instinctively shares this cultivation space hints at its innate connection to the Dao. Xiao Chen’s decision not to capture the horse reflects a deeper Xianxia principle: true mastery over spiritual beasts comes not from force but from virtue and mutual affinity.

## Reading Guide
This chapter plants several seeds for the arc ahead. Zhao Lin’er’s confirmed presence on the island promises a reckoning the moment Xiao Chen regains full strength—and he has just recovered. The celestial foal, meanwhile, is clearly not a mere animal; its intelligence and spiritual sensitivity suggest it may become a significant ally or even a test of character. Xiao Chen’s patient approach is a hallmark of his growth—he is learning that the world of immortality rewards wisdom over aggression. The approaching figure at the chapter’s end, sensed by the horse before it flees, could be friend or foe. Readers should note the careful build-up: the scarf, the horse, and the healed body all converge toward a pivotal moment. The atmosphere remains laden with the novel’s signature blend of mythic wonder and lurking danger.
