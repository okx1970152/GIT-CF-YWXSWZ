## Chapter Overview
Li Huowang gets a firsthand taste of imperial desperation as the young Emperor of Great Liang, Ji Lin, practically begs him for help in a succession war he’s clearly losing. After turning down the doomed plea with vague politeness, Li Huowang uses his Zuowandao disguise skills to slip out of the city with Bai Lingmiao and Li Sui—only to have his morning snack interrupted by the friendly, unignorable presence of his “Big Boss” Zuowandao superior.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang is summoned to the palace, where a towering, bronze-masked guard confiscates his weapons at the gate.
- The young Emperor Ji Lin reveals his desperate situation: his brothers are moving against him, he has no trustworthy officials, and he’s been trying to recruit anyone he can, including the Supervisory Heavenly Office.
- Ji Lin offers Li Huowang titles and wealth in exchange for help, but his approach is amateurish and impulsive—he threatens beheading one moment, then begs with tears the next.
- Li Huowang internally dismisses the emperor’s chances and refuses the offer with a non-committal “let me think about it,” seeing it as a sure way to get crushed in the succession bloodbath.
- After leaving, Li Huowang notices an old eunuch circling a name on his palm, confirming the emperor is mass-recruiting and likely grasping at straws.
- The next morning, Li Huowang uses his disguise skills (as a former Zuowandao Red Center) to swap faces with a beggar and exits the city with his group.
- At a street stall, Li Huowang buys six fried-dough-stick-and-rice-cake sandwiches—but then spits out a die and addresses the plump vendor as “Big Boss,” revealing the stall owner is a Zuowandao operative.
- The “Big Boss” cheerfully asks for a “little favor,” confirming the Zuowandao have found him even through his disguise.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Eight-Carrier Palanquin (八抬大轿):** In imperial China, the number of carriers for a sedan chair reflected the occupant’s rank. An eight-carrier palanquin was the highest honor a civilian could receive, typically reserved for top officials or those on direct imperial business. Li Huowang getting one is a subtle sign that the emperor is treating him as a VIP—but it also makes him painfully visible to spies.

**Zuowandao Disguise (坐忘道障眼法):** The “face-hanging” trick Li Huowang uses—attaching his own face to a beggar and leaving him at home—is consistent with Zuowandao illusion methods. In earlier lore, these tricksters rely on cognitive misdirection rather than physical transformation: the beggar doesn’t actually *become* Li Huowang, but anyone watching the house *perceives* him there. The die Li Huowang spits out at the stall is a Zuowandao calling card—the four-pip face of a die is a known symbol of the Red Center.

**Fried Dough Stick with Rice Cake (油条包麻糍):** A classic Chinese street breakfast from the Jiangxi region. A freshly fried *youtiao* (crunchy, savory) is folded around a slab of *maci* (soft, chewy glutinous rice cake coated in black sesame and sugar). The contrast between savory and sweet, crunchy and soft, is a beloved comfort food—making it a quietly heartwarming detail that stands in stark contrast to the looming political horror and Zuowandao scheming.

## Reading Guide
Get ready, fellow Daoists—Li Huowang’s morning just went from “brutal palace politics” to “friendly Zuowandao drive-by.” The young emperor’s desperation is painfully transparent: he has no allies, no strategy, and no idea that the one person he’s begging is currently wearing a fake face and planning his escape. But the real gut punch comes at the breakfast stall. That cheerful, fat vendor with the mung-bean eyes? He’s *die-spitting* familiar with Li Huowang’s past. And the way he asks for a “little favor” with a big smile? That’s not a request—it’s a leash. The Zuowandao have found him, and they’re not letting go.
