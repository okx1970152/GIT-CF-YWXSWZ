## Chapter Overview
Get ready, weary travelers—because the road into Later Shu just got a whole lot crazier. This chapter is the perfect breather-before-the-storm: we cross the border, dodge mysterious highwaymen using slick gangland code talk, and stumble into a frontier town where the folk horror is served *live and in person*. The Nuo opera scene is pure uncanny valley—masks that grind their wooden teeth, bodies that move like they've forgotten human anatomy, and a soundtrack that could double as a haunting. It's a masterclass in building unease through cultural dissonance.

## Key Plot Points
- The group successfully passes through the Siqi border checkpoint, bribing officials with silver.
- While camping in the barren Gobi, Li Huowang detects an unknown presence in the darkness and uses professional "code talk" (spring canon) to identify and de-escalate the encounter with a potential bandit or scout.
- At dawn, the mysterious figure has vanished without a trace.
- The next day, the group arrives at the first town in Later Shu—a rugged, dusty frontier settlement.
- A public Nuo opera performance draws the crowd's attention, and Li Huowang observes the dancers with growing unease—their movements are unnatural, and their masks produce actual wooden fangs that seem to grow from the wood itself.
- Lü Zhuangyuan, a professional opera performer himself, openly dismisses the "opera" as trash, revealing a performer's rivalry across regional traditions.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Nuo Opera (傩戏)—Ancient Chinese Exorcism as Performance:** This isn't your average folk theater. Nuo opera is one of China's oldest living ritual performance traditions, tracing back to shamanistic exorcisms during the Shang and Zhou dynasties. The word "Nuo" (傩) itself means "to drive away plague demons." Performers wear terrifying wooden masks—deliberately made more frightening than the demons they're meant to scare—and their movements are deliberately jerky, inhuman, and alienating. That's the point: you're supposed to feel *wrong* watching it. The masks aren't just props; they're considered vessels for spirits, ancient ghosts, or even the gods of plague. In a world already saturated with supernatural corruption, seeing a "pure" Nuo performance should still make your skin crawl, because the tradition itself was always meant to put you on edge.

**The "Teeth-Manipulation" Skill (耍牙):** This is a real, highly specialized folk skill originating from Ninghai Pingdiao opera (宁海平调). Performers train for years to manipulate large, curved boar's tusks in their mouths—up to eight at a time—using only their tongue and cheek muscles. The teeth slide in and out, click together, and can even rotate independently. In this chapter, the detail of *sixteen* teeth is a deliberate exaggeration for horror effect, pushing a real folk skill into the uncanny.

**Spring Canon (春典)—The Code Talk of the Jianghu:** Li Huowang's exchange with the mysterious figure isn't random shouting. "Spring canon" (chūn diǎn) is the secret jargon of China's traveling underworld—a covert language shared by bandits, wanderers, martial artists, and itinerant performers. It's how they identify friend from foe without drawing weapons. The phrases Li Huowang uses—"the spring opens," "what's your vine," "a jeweled pagoda tower"—are all ritualized greetings that establish rank, affiliation, and intent without ever saying "please don't rob us." Getting this right in a world of constant danger is a survival skill as important as any martial art.

## Reading Guide
Two things stand out in this deceptively quiet chapter. First, Li Huowang's handling of the night encounter shows he's no longer just reacting to threats—he's *managing* them. He doesn't panic, doesn't wake everyone screaming. He plays the underworld's own game, and it works. That's a huge leap from the lost, paranoid kid we met in the cave.

Second, the Nuo opera. Pay close attention to how the *discomfort* is described: "you couldn't imagine a human body making those moves." That's not poetic license—that's the author telling you *something wants to come through this performance*. In many folk traditions, ritual dance isn't about entertaining humans; it's about inviting or appeasing spirits. The fact that the fangs *actually appear to grow from the wood* is a dead giveaway that this isn't just theater.

Also: Lü Zhuangyuan's disdain is a subtle class marker. Nuo opera is considered "crude" and "folk" compared to formal court opera traditions. His professional snobbery is a lovely bit of characterization—he's a low-status performer himself, but he still has his own hierarchy. Everyone needs someone to look down on, even in the Dao-Twisted World.
