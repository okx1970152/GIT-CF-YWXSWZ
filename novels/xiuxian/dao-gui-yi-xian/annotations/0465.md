## Chapter Overview

Get ready, folks, because this chapter is a real tear-jerker wrapped in body horror. After the chaos of the Heavenly Calamity, a blind Li Huowang must confront the monstrous new form his daughter Li Sui has taken. She’s merged with Mantou the dog, creating a flesh-twisted, tentacled, dog-faced creature. But where anyone else would see a monster, Li Huowang sees his child. This is not a chapter about fighting or scheming; it’s about acceptance in a world that has twisted the very definition of family. We also get a brief, brilliantly mundane glimpse of life in Niuxin Village with the perpetually grumpy Tutor Wu, proving that even in the shadow of cosmic horror, people still have to do the dishes.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang, now blind, uses sound and touch to identify Li Sui’s new form. The process is a slow, detailed, and deeply unsettling reveal of the creature she has become.
- Li Sui explains what happened: she was playing with Mantou, fell asleep, and when she woke up, the dog was gone. She has assumed Mantou’s body, merging her tentacled form with the dog’s flesh and bones. Her dialogue is simple, literal, and emotionally direct—she’s still a child.
- Li Huowang, after a long and painful pause, accepts her. He decides that she is now a girl (because Mantou was female) and declares her his and Bai Lingmiao’s daughter.
- Bai Lingmiao is hesitant, but the Second Spirit acts first, reaching out to touch Li Sui’s head in a gesture of acceptance.
- The chapter closes with a sharp tonal shift to Tutor Wu in Niuxin Village. He is woken from a nap by his wife and expects to be waited on hand and foot. She refuses, telling him to eat his dinner and wash his own dishes. His outraged proclamation that a “scholar” cannot step foot in a kitchen leaves him arguing with an empty room.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Black Tai Sui’s Body-Snatching Ability:** This chapter provides a visceral demonstration of a key Black Tai Sui ability: the power to “take over” another living body. This is not spirit possession in the Western sense, but a physical merging and subsumption of the host. The body is literally rearranged, with tentacles re-sculpting the flesh from the inside. This is a form of corrupt alchemical transformation, not a magical spell.

**The “Heavenly Calamity” Aftermath:** The chapter’s opening shows the immediate consequence of large-scale supernatural events. The “rules” have been bent; blindness, physical transformation, and the blurring of identity are not casualties, but the new normal. This reinforces the novel’s core theme that reality is a fragile, pliable thing.

**The Scholar’s Pride (Scholar-Bureaucrat Culture):** Tutor Wu’s scene is a microcosm of traditional Confucian social hierarchy. As a “scholar-gentry,” he belongs to the class that is supposed to wield the brush, not the knife or the mop. His wife’s blunt refusal to serve him is a small act of rebellion against these rigid societal codes. His complaint, “How can a scholar step foot in a kitchen,” is a classic historical trope that underscores the deep chasm between intellectual and manual labor.

**Body Horror vs. Emotional Beat:** This chapter is a masterclass in contrasting external horror with internal warmth. The detailed description of Li Sui’s form—the skinned dog face, the exposed bone, the bulging tentacles—is pure grotesque. Yet, through Li Huowang’s tactile exploration and his calm, accepting voice, the narrative reframes the monster as a lost child seeking comfort. This is the Dao-Twisted World’s unique emotional signature: finding a beating heart inside a rotting corpse.

### Heavens Beyond Heaven

A term for the higher layers of cosmic reality in the Dao-Twisted World; direct exposure can cause physical possession before any mental breakdown.

### Scholar-Gentry (读书人)

The educated elite class in traditional Chinese society, highly respected and exempt from manual labor; Confucian ideals held that scholars should not engage in physical work like farming or cooking.

## Reading Guide

If you were worried this was just going to be non-stop action after the Heavenly Calamity, this chapter is a beautiful, gut-wrenching reset. The translation needs to treat Li Sui’s appearance with horrific detail, but Li Huowang’s dialogue must remain soft, exhausted, and paternal. Her voice, by contrast, should be that of a child who doesn’t understand why she’s scary. The final scene with Tutor Wu is pure comic relief, but don’t play it for over-the-top slapstick. His indignation is genuine and stuffy, which makes his wife’s dismissal all the more satisfying. The contrast between these two halves of the chapter—the profound, shattering acceptance of a monster-daughter and the petty squabble over washing dishes—is the real jewel here. It tells us that life, in all its strange and mundane forms, stubbornly continues.
