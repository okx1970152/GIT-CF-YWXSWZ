## Chapter Overview

Dear readers, strap in—because nothing says “relaxing visit to the capital” like immediately assaulting a face-changing performer out of pure trauma reflex. Li Huowang’s brief stop in Shangjing is supposed to be a simple errand: cash in a favor with the Chief Recorder and find a way to save Bai Lingmiao. But as always in the Dao-Twisted World, “simple” doesn’t exist. The chapter pivots from a tense inn scene to a bizarre home visit, then descends into the sweat-soaked, killer-saturated bowels of a maximum-security prison. Every interaction hums with unspoken debt, theatrical goodwill, and the creeping sense that Li Huowang is being maneuvered—even when the maneuvering looks like generosity.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang nearly assaults a face-changing performer at the inn after being triggered by the act of rapid mask-switching, which reminds him of the Zuowandao’s ability to flip faces.
- Bai Lingmiao questions his extreme reaction; Li Huowang calms down and explains that Shangjing is under the Supervisory Heavenly Office and thus theoretically safe from Zuowandao infiltration.
- After moving to a quieter inn, Li Huowang goes alone at night to the address on the Chief Recorder’s letter.
- The Chief Recorder lives in a modest but carefully kept courtyard house—a stark contrast to his high rank, which immediately raises Li Huowang’s suspicions.
- The Recorder is effusively warm, practically forcing a house deed and keys on Li Huowang as repayment for the earlier favor.
- They go by sedan chair to yet another prison—the Recorder seems structurally tied to these facilities—where Li Huowang meets a barracks-full of hardened Military practitioners (兵家), all of them looking like variations of Peng Longteng.
- The prison is triple-gated, with carvings of the mythical beast *bi'an*, and the air is so thick with killing intent it’s physically oppressive.
- A senior gaoler, Captain Cao, interrupts their conversation to light incense for the Prison God, revealing a sandstone shrine embedded in the wall with a kindly old deity flanked by two vicious demons.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Face-Changing (变脸 / *biaolian*)** is a signature technique of Sichuan opera, where performers use brilliantly colored silk masks and sleight-of-hand to swap faces in the blink of an eye—sometimes dozens of times in a single performance. It’s a dazzling, joyful art form, which makes Li Huowang’s violent reaction all the more telling. In a world where the Zuowandao literally peel and swap identities, even an innocent cultural performance becomes a trauma landmine.

**狴犴 (Bì’àn)** is the seventh son of the Dragon King in Chinese mythology. He looks like a tiger and is said to love litigation and justice—which is why his image is traditionally carved on prison gates and courtroom doors. In the Dao-Twisted World, this mythical guardian marks the threshold between the mundane and the abyss: once you pass under the *bi'an*, you are entering the territory of inescapable judgment.

**狱神 (Prison God / *Yushen*)** is a lesser-known but widely worshipped deity in traditional Chinese folk religion. Each prison had its own shrine, and gaolers would burn incense to him before starting their shifts, petitioning for order and protection. Here, the shrine's placement—halfway up a wall, with a serene old man seated between two snarling little demons—is a perfect visual metaphor for the novel’s moral landscape: compassion trapped between two horrors.

### Two-Layer Heart-Turbid Trap (一生一死心浊)

A containment paradox where a dead Heart-Turbid conceals a living one, and the living one is hidden inside the dead; victims are trapped in a fabricated karmic obstacle layer, unable to perceive the true world or threat.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in *misdirected generosity*. The Chief Recorder is being so aggressively helpful—giving away his retirement home, pre-arranging sedan chairs, practically dragging Li Huowang into a prison full of blades—that it screams *transaction*. Pay attention to every detail of the courtyard house: the single well, the jujube tree, the phrase “just right for a family of three.” The Recorder is either a lonely old man trying to buy company, or he’s clearing his assets for a reason far darker than retirement. Also, the comparison of the gaolers to “a room full of Peng Longtengs” isn’t just color—it’s a warning. These are men who live and breathe killing intent, and whatever is locked up deeper in this prison must be something so terrible that only a den of human weapons can hold it. Watch that *bi'an* gate—it might not just be keeping something in, but keeping Li Huowang from seeing the truth too soon.
