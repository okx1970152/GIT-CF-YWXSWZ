## Chapter Overview
Get ready, fellow Daoists—this chapter is a study in aftermath. The cult assault on Niuxin Village is over, but the real battle has just begun. Instead of a bloody climax, we get something far more unsettling: the quiet, stinking wake of violence. Bodies are laid out, nerves are frayed, and the survivors are left to process what just happened. The horror here is not the clash of swords but the hollowed-out feeling in your gut when the adrenaline fades and you realize the people you shared meals with are now under white cloth. But the chapter’s true dirty trick is its interrogation scene—a masterclass in psychological terror that feels disturbingly *familiar*. Who needs knives when you have a coarse rope and a good imagination?

## Key Plot Points
- The cultists flee into the night after their leader's death, leaving Niuxin Village in a daze of relief, nausea, and delayed grief.
- Twelve bodies are laid out in the Bai family courtyard. The dead include two of Lü Zhuangyuan's newest apprentices, one of whom was his most promising.
- Chun Xiaoman collapses from Spirit Invocation exhaustion but recovers enough to refute the theory that the attackers were ordinary bandits, pointing out their fanatical chanting of "Yú'er Shén."
- Gouwa drags in a horribly disfigured cultist (crippled by his own "divine needle") for interrogation.
- Chun Xiaoman's attempt at a brutal interrogation fails when the cultist welcomes death, believing it will let him join his god and be reborn as an emperor.
- Gouwa steps in, mimicking Li Huowang's methods. He lays out a set of butcher's knives… then ignores them completely. Instead, he picks up a coarse hemp rope and *describes* what he will do with it in slow, lurid detail.
- The cultist breaks down completely before any actual torture is applied, confessing everything and begging for death.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Yú'er Shén (于儿神)** – This creature is lifted straight from the *Classic of Mountains and Seas (Shanhaijing)*, one of the foundational texts of Chinese mythology. The original description is a deity with a human head and two snake-like bodies. In the Dao-Twisted World, it's been repurposed into the patron god of a death-cult that promises its followers reincarnation as emperors—a chilling perversion of folk-religious salvation.

**Spirit Invocation (神打)** – A real folk martial practice (often associated with Southern Chinese martial arts and the Boxer Rebellion), where practitioners enter a trance state to gain superhuman toughness. The catch? The "invited masters" leave you utterly drained. Chun Xiaoman's collapse is not just a dramatic beat; it's a faithful execution of the rule: bodies have to pay for borrowed power.

**The Power of Description over Violence** – Gouwa's interrogation is a brilliant subversion of the "torture scene" trope. He doesn't need to inflict pain; he only needs to make the victim *anticipate* it. The rough hemp rope, the grinding motion, the specific mention of "between the legs"—this is psychological warfare dressed as a butcher's craft. The chapter's Chinese title, *怕*, means "fear." And that's the real weapon here: not the blade, but the shadow the blade casts.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a quiet gut-punch. There's no big monster fight, no Li Huowang doing something spectacular, and yet it feels heavier than a dozen action scenes. Pay attention to how the group dynamics shift without Li Huowang present: Chun Xiaoman is angry and impulsive, Lü Zhuangyuan is grieving and pragmatic, and Gouwa—the comic relief—is the one who steps up with the most effective (and most disturbing) solution. The fact that everyone is *unsettled* by how well Gouwa imitates Li Huowang is a powerful moment of dramatic irony: they're seeing Li Huowang's methods through fresh eyes, and they don't like what they see. This chapter also quietly reinforces a core theme of the Dao-Twisted World: cruelty is a skill, and sometimes the most terrifying thing isn't the monster under the bed, but the man standing over you with a rope he hasn't even used yet.
