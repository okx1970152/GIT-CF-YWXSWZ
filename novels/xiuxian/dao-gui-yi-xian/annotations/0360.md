## Chapter Overview

This chapter is a frantic, four-way supernatural brawl where a newly revealed horror, the **Heart-Turbid (心浊)**, uses its terrifying ability to “hide” people, memories, and objects from reality itself. Li Huowang, Hong Da, and the Chief Recorder are ambushed by an opponent that can erase their allies from existence, and even from their minds. The stakes escalate when Li Huowang realizes that Bai Lingmiao and the Second Spirit are among those being hidden. The conflict crescendos into a brutal, cooperative gambit where the cost of victory leaves a chilling aftertaste, hinting at a deeper corruption taking root.

## Key Plot Points

- **The Revelation:** Li Huowang discovers text carved on his own burned arm that warns of a “Heart-Turbid.” The Chief Recorder suddenly remembers that this was the reason he summoned them in the first place.
- **The Erasure Mechanic:** The Heart-Turbid’s ability is defined. It doesn’t just turn people invisible; it hides their very existence and the memories others have of them. The Chief Recorder’s abacus confirms three people are missing, including Tuoba Danqing and Liu Zongyuan.
- **Li Huowang’s Counter:** To fight the memory theft, Li Huowang carves warnings directly into his own flesh. He pulls the cotton from his ears to use his supernatural hearing to track the invisible enemy.
- **The Second Spirit’s Plot:** It’s revealed that the Second Spirit (Er Shen) never left them earlier. She has secretly brought Bai Lingmiao back to help, making them targets of the Heart-Turbid.
- **The Final Gambit:** After a chaotic back-and-forth where the Heart-Turbid uses hidden people as living projectiles, Li Huowang and the women coordinate a trap. The Second Spirit boosts Bai Lingmiao, who is forced to grab the tainted Purple-Tasseled Sword and kill the Heart-Turbid with it.
- **The Chilling End:** The chapter ends not with relief, but with Bai Lingmiao and the Second Spirit laughing uncontrollably after Bai Lingmiao touches the cursed sword, a clear sign of corruption.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Heart-Turbid (心浊) as a Mirror to the Heart-Element (心素):** This chapter finally gives us the “Heart-Turbid” as a defined counterpart to Li Huowang’s own “Heart-Element” constitution. While a Heart-Element makes fictions real (a “positive” corruption of reality), the Heart-Turbid *hides* real things, erasing them from existence and memory. In Daoist cosmology, *su* (素) represents the raw, undifferentiated potential of the universe, while *zhuo* (浊) represents the heavy, settling, obscuring dregs. The novel brilliantly pairs these concepts as two sides of a single cosmic coin: one makes you believe in lies, the other makes you forget the truth. Together, they are a terrifying philosophical weapon against any stable perception of the world.

**The Purple-Tasseled Sword’s Corruption:** The sword is described as a “military path” weapon, saturated with *sha qi* (killing intent). In xianxia, such weapons have a will of their own and can corrupt the wielder's mind, feeding their aggression and bloodlust. Bai Lingmiao, a gentle spirit-medium, touching this weapon was a desperate act. The fact that she and the Second Spirit are now laughing uncontrollably suggests that the sword’s malevolent essence is now interacting with her spirit, which could have dangerous consequences. This hints at a future struggle where part of the group might need to fight or reclaim her.

### Knife-Pawnbroker (赊刀人)

A folk figure from Chinese legend who loans knives and later collects the borrower’s luck or fortune; the rusty blades on Hong Da’s robe carry a supernatural debt.

### Bang Bing Jue (帮兵决)

A formal, rhythmic incantation used by *chuma* spirit-mediums in northeastern China to summon spirits; it is a ritual chant, not a folk song.

## Reading Guide

Okay, this chapter is a masterclass in tension. The Heart-Turbid is a *horrifying* concept because it attacks the very foundation of the story: the unreliable narrator. Li Huowang’s solution—carving notes into his own flesh to fight memory loss—is peak *Dao Gui Yi Xian*: desperate, self-destructive, and brutally pragmatic. But the real gut-punch is the ending. We get a *victory*, but it’s paid for with Bai Lingmiao touching the cursed sword. That laugh isn’t a laugh of joy; it’s the sound of something breaking, or something new being born. The story is setting up a major conflict: Li Huowang’s growing power is matched by the horrifying price those around him pay. Pay close attention to the “ceramic horses” mention in the next segment—it’s a huge clue about the White Lotus’s deeper plans.

## Deeper Lore: The “Skinning Knife Pawnbroker” (赊刀人)

The rusty knives hanging on Hong Da’s robe are not just decoration. They are a calling card. The “She Dao Ren” (赊刀人) or “Knife-Pawnbroker” is a figure from Chinese folk legend. They would loan out knives, predicting that a certain event would happen (like a famine or a war). When the prediction came true, they would return to collect their payment, which was often not money but a piece of the borrower’s luck, fortune, or lifespan. In this novel, Hong Da’s entire body is covered in these knives, implying he has made countless terrible deals. Each rusty blade is a debt owed to him, or a piece of his own soul he’s had to cut off. He’s a walking, breathing collection of supernatural debts, a perfect fit for the grim world of the Office.
