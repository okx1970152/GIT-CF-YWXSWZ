## Chapter Overview
This chapter is a masterclass in genre blending—a slice-of-life *jianghu* hustle, a folk exorcism performance, and a supernatural horror reveal all in one tight package. We follow the Lü Family Troupe as they stumble into a seemingly routine “demon-expelling” job for a wealthy Zhang family. But when Lü Xiucai spots an object—a copper-coin sword identical to Li Huowang’s—the stakes shift from performance booking to a covert investigation. Get ready for a tense, shadowy pursuit in a rich man’s courtyard that ends with a smirk-worthy punchline: the Daoist is *raising* the ghost, and worst of all, he’s just discovered a new prize.

## Key Plot Points
- Lü Zhuangyuan’s troupe arrives in a small village where a Daoist is performing a public exorcism on a possessed man.
- The exorcism works: black smoke exits the man’s mouth, he recovers, and the crowd is awestruck. The head of the household hires the Lü Family Troupe to perform at the celebratory feast.
- During the night performance, Lü Xiucai is distracted by the Daoist’s back-tacked copper-coin sword, which he believes is identical to Li Huowang’s.
- Gouwa and Lü Xiucai debate whether to approach the Daoist directly, but hesitate due to the risk of danger.
- After the feast, the Daoist takes the family head to a back courtyard. The two infiltrators follow.
- The Daoist uses a compass and dark magic to “discover” an heirloom clay bodhisattva as the possessed object.
- He then stages a fake attack using black smoke that enters a black gown, which then animates and blocks their escape route.
- The Daoist realises he has been observed, and the two must flee. Gouwa uses the rotten-wood *ruyi* to strike the animated gown, buying them time.
- The chapter ends with the Daoist’s delighted discovery: “It’s the tongue of a Heart-Element!”

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
The “Celestial Master” aesthetic is a classic trope in Chinese folk horror. The yellow robe, black crown, and ritual chanting (here quoting a spirit-calming verse from Daoist inner cultivation) are stock-in-trade for *fangshi* sorcerers and itinerant priests. But the twist is textbook Dao-Twisted World: the “disgraced exorcist who secretly feeds the monster” trope is a time-honoured reversal in Chinese ghost lore. The **inverted clay bodhisattva** is a particularly nasty detail—it is almost certainly a cursed container for the spirit, deliberately placed to frame the family’s heirloom.  
Also notable: Lü Zhuangyuan’s pragmatic “wait and see if the man dies, then we get the job” mentality is a brutally authentic glimpse into the operating logic of itinerant performers in the Dao-Twisted World’s cruel social economy. The copper-coin sword reappearance is a *dongjie* (dong read as “thread” continuity clue) connecting this roaming side story back to Li Huowang’s main arc.

## Reading Guide
This is an excellent “breather chapter” that still raises the hair on your neck. The pacing is a textbook “slow burn to a fast finish.” The domestic scene of haggling for a performance by lantern light feels like a genuine glimpse into folk theatrical life, making the betrayal of the Daoist—and the sudden danger—hit harder. Watch for the second structural payoff: the copper-coin sword first appeared in Li Huowang’s hands as a powerful exorcism tool. Now it’s been recognized by the world he left behind. The chapter’s final line—a cold, almost businesslike acknowledgment that the Daoist now knows there’s a Heart-Element in the area—is the perfect low-volume alarm bell.

## Clarifying the Line “To Twist the Gown”
The closing image of the “inverted clay bodhisattva” has a subtle but grim connotation. In Chinese folk religion, a *daozuo pusa* (倒坐菩萨) is a figure seated backward, “facing the wall.” It is associated with the idea of being “cut off from grace,” a sacred object reversed into a heretical container. The Daoist storing the poisoned smoke-spirit inside such an object and then framing the household heirloom is another layer of his calculated, professional malice.
