## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang wakes up from his brush with death in the Dao-Twisted World, only to find his body inexplicably healed. His desperate need for certainty collides with Dr. Li's maddening suggestion from "reality": if he can send things *to* the hallucination, what does that say about which world is real? The chapter pivots on a dark, intimate revelation—Bai Lingmiao confesses she secretly became a *chuma* medium and used her spirit to save him. But just as Li Huowang feels his footing return, he learns that the world around him is rotting in a stranger, quieter way: the entire village has forgotten who they are.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang wakes on a wooden bed in the Dao-Twisted World to find his fatal abdominal wound neatly bandaged and nearly healed, a recovery his own crude alchemy could never achieve.
- He is tormented by Dr. Li's parting question: if he can hallucinate objects *into* reality, the converse test (sending a real object *into* the hallucination) might shatter his certainty.
- Bai Lingmiao reveals to him with shame that she has become a *chuma* (spirit-medium) disciple; she secretly summoned a family spirit, the "Old Lady of the Bai Family," to heal him at the cost of nearly all his yang-life pills.
- Li Huowang's first reaction is not anger but profound relief—her confession buoys him because it means *this* side is real, re-solidifying his identity against the encroaching doubt planted by the modern world.
- Chun Xiaoman enters to deliver a disturbing update: all of Wujia Village's inhabitants have left, and more unsettlingly, they had been losing their identities, forgetting each other, as if their past bonds had been erased.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Chu Ma (出马) Spirit Mediumship**: This is a folk tradition, especially alive in Northeast China, where a human becomes the "mount" or vehicle for a powerful spirit—often a fox, weasel, snake, or other animal immortal. The process is violent and involuntary; the chosen person typically suffers a life-threatening illness or madness that only resolves when they "mount the horse" and accept the spirit. Bai Lingmiao's confession that she became a *chu ma* disciple *without* Li Huowang's knowledge is a huge character reveal—she has taken on a body-changing, reality-bending burden to save him, and it makes her far more than the "helpless girl" she appeared to be.

**The "Yang Life" (阳寿) Transaction**: In Chinese folk belief, one's life expectancy is a quantifiable resource. Spirits and deities can take it as payment. Li Huowang's yang-life pills, abstracted from his own lifespan, become a universal currency that spirits happily accept. Bai Lingmiao's spirit taking "all but one" means the cost of Li Huowang's complete recovery was almost all his stored longevity—a price so high it borders on extortion.

**Loss of Collective Memory**: The Wujia villagers forgetting each other—even sons forgetting their father—echoes a distinctly Dao-Twisted horror. It's not a curse that kills or maims; it *edits* identity. In a world where "cultivating the false into reality" is a working cosmic law, a collective belief that "you are not family" can become truer than blood. The rotting trees suggest a supernatural leaching, a sign that the village's fate is tied to the land's vitality, a concept resonant with *feng shui* and local earth-god worship in Chinese folk religion.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is about the unbearable weight of proof. Li Huowang has been given a terrible tool—a logical test that could either confirm or destroy his reality—and he chooses *not to use it*. His desperate laughter of relief when Bai Lingmiao reveals her secret is not joy; it is the sound of a man catching a rope before he falls back into the abyss. He needs *this* side to be real so badly that any evidence, even a confession of hidden magical healing, feels like salvation.

But the chapter ends with a detail that will haunt you: the Wujia villagers are disappearing not from violence, but from a slow, impersonal erosion of identity. The world isn't attacking them. It's forgetting them. And that, in the Dao-Twisted World, is a far more terrifying fate.
