## Chapter Overview
After surviving the nightmare of his own corpse and a sea of blood, Li Huowang finally has a moment of fragile peace—and promptly uses it to tell Bai Lingmiao the truth about his origins. This chapter is a quiet, tense breather where the real drama isn’t a monster attack but a confession. Li Huowang lays out his impossible past, Bai Lingmiao makes her stance heartbreakingly clear, and our poor, paranoid protagonist has to confront the fact that his survival tool (The Thousand Greats Record) is also a slow-acting poison. Plus, there’s a mysterious side effect from Bashe’s gaze that’s speeding up his recovery—but in this world, a “benefit” always feels like it’s just setting up the next horror.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang wakes from a terrifying dream of his own dead body, checking for a jade pendant that isn’t there; he calms himself by repeating “it was just a dream.”
- In the dark, before dawn, he finally opens up to Bai Lingmiao about being from another world, his delusional disorder, and the source of his agony; the confession is a rare moment of vulnerability.
- Bai Lingmiao, demonstrating her “soft on the outside, hard on the inside” nature, rejects the fantastical details and cuts straight to the chase: the red bamboo slip, *The Thousand Greats Record*, is the real source of the danger, and she begs him to throw it away, fearing it will kill him—and leave her alone.
- Li Huowang internally admits the Record is a double-edged sword, but decides he cannot discard it until he finds a replacement; his past encounters with Qingfeng Temple, Zhengde Temple, and Anci Nunnery have convinced him all local sects are equally twisted.
- He discovers his stomach wound has healed far faster than expected, attributing the change to the final glance from Bashe; he is cautiously optimistic but deeply uneasy about what this “benefit” really means.
- The chapter closes with a domestic scene at dinner: Li Huowang finds the food unbearably salty, but Bai Lingmiao tastes the same dish and insists it’s normal, leaving a subtle hint that his sensory perception might be shifting.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The "Benefit" That Feels Like a Trap:** In traditional xianxia, a protagonist's body gaining accelerated healing is a classic power-up—usually a sign of a breakthrough or a blessing from a divine technique. Here, the cause is the lingering gaze of Bashe, a primordial, monstrous deity. This subverts the genre trope: Li Huowang isn't being gifted a cultivation boon; his body is being *altered* by an entity he doesn't understand, making the reader feel the same mix of relief and dread he does.

**The Thousand Greats Record (《大千录》):** The red bamboo slip is not a standard cultivation manual. It is an artifact of a "suffering-cult," trading extreme physical and mental agony for supernatural power. This reflects a key theme of *Dao Gui Yi Xian*: nothing comes without a personal, visceral cost. Li Huowang’s internal debate about keeping it versus discarding it is the novel’s central dilemma personified—survival versus humanity.

**Bai Lingmiao's "Soft Shell":** Bai Lingmiao’s character aligns with a classic archetype in Chinese fiction: the outwardly gentle woman who possesses an unyielding core. Her refusal to be distracted by the grand, cosmic details of Huowang’s story, focusing instead on his immediate, bloody wound, shows her pragmatism and deep emotional loyalty. In a world of cosmic horror, her grounded concern feels like a lifeline.

## Reading Guide
This is a fantastic "character development" chapter in a novel that’s usually all about "cosmic dread." If you’ve been waiting for Bai Lingmiao to step up from a background presence, this is her moment—she doesn't lecture, she doesn't faint, she just holds his ruined hand and says, "If you die, what happens to me?" It's devastating. Also, pay close attention to the last scene where their tastes disagree. It’s a small, eerie detail that suggests Li Huowang’s perception of the world is subtly changing, which in this universe is far more ominous than a big monster. Get ready for the next leg of the journey—the group is heading back to Siqi, and nothing good ever waits on the road in the Dao-Twisted World.

## Deep Lore: The "Body Change" Trope
In Chinese folk religion and xianxia, a cultivator's body undergoing physical changes is a sign of progress—a stronger dantian, denser bones, sharper senses. Li Huowang’s accelerated healing fits this template superficially, but the *source* is the key difference. It’s not the result of disciplined meditation or consuming a sacred pill; it’s the passive side-effect of being noticed by a god of suffering. This is the novel's signature move: taking a familiar, welcome genre convention and twisting it into something that feels deeply wrong. His body is becoming better at surviving, but it’s also becoming *theirs*.
