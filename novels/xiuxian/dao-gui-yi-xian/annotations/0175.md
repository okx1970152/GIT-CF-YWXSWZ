## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, because Chapter 175 isn’t just a clash—it’s a spiritual demolition derby. Danyangzi finally reaches the grand gate he’s been chasing his whole life, only to find the nameplate reads something very different from “Southern Heavenly Gate.” The world he built on faith and gore crumbles in an instant. But before he can even process the existential crisis, his own body betrays him. Li Huowang’s head has replaced one of his three, and the student has become the master’s worst torment. This chapter delivers a masterclass in psychological warfare: no spells, no chants, just two madmen tearing at each other’s souls while the real truth peels back like rotten skin. And that final charge into the White Jade Capital? Pure, desperate defiance.

## Key Plot Points
- Danyangzi reads the jade plaque: it says “Mao Gate” (牦之门), not the Southern Heavenly Gate (南天门) he expected. The revelation that there is no Immortal Realm shatters him.
- Li Huowang’s head replaces one of Danyangzi’s three. He appears not as a separate entity but as an integrated part of the fusion, mocking him from within.
- Danyangzi tries to cut Li Huowang down but is physically restrained by his own body—Li Huowang even slaps him, using the shared physiology as a weapon.
- Both sets of hallucinations converge. Li Huowang’s bewilderment (迷惘) and Danyangzi’s victims begin to manifest as writhing, seaweed-like forms around them.
- Li Huowang weaponizes the Heart-Element’s burden: even if Danyangzi kills him, the torment may transfer permanently to the pursuer.
- The jade plaque vanishes, replaced by a kaleidoscopic vision of the White Jade Capital. The “immortals” inside are revealed to be a bodhisattva with a halo made of white eyes.
- Danyangzi’s instinct screams danger and attempts to pull back, but Li Huowang  reverses the tug-of-war at the last second, dragging them both into the bodhisattva’s face.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
The **Southern Heavenly Gate (南天门)** is the iconic main entrance to Heaven in Chinese mythology, especially the Daoist celestial bureaucracy. It’s the gate all cultivators dream of crossing to achieve immortality. Changing it to **Mao Gate (牦之门)** is a devastating subversion—牦 (Mao) primarily means “yak” or “coarse wool” in classical Chinese, carrying connotations of the animalistic and the base. It suggests the path Danyangzi walked leads not to transcendence but to a beastly parody of divinity.

This chapter also plays directly with the **Mao Gate / Nantian Men** rebus from earlier in the arc—where Li Huowang’s reading of a misaligned gate name was dismissed as madness. Now it’s confirmed as the truth. The novel weaponizes the folk motif of “wrong gate, wrong destination,” a common horror in Chinese ghost stories where a traveler enters a familiar door only to find themselves in a demon’s lair.

The **White Jade Capital (白玉京)** is a key term. In Daoist poetry, most famously Li Bai’s line “天上白玉京，十二楼五城” (The White Jade Capital in heaven, with twelve towers and five cities), it represents the ultimate celestial destination. Here, its beauty is a trap: a halo of grasping white eyes, not radiant saints. The novel corrupts the most sacred image in Daoist cosmology into a horror show.

On the alchemical side, the **Three Corpses (三尸/San Shi)** are central to the internal conflict. Traditional Daoist cultivation holds that three parasitic demons reside in the human body—in the head, chest, and belly—spurring lust, greed, and delusion. Cutting them off is a prerequisite for immortality. Danyangzi claims to have severed them, yet Li Huowang’s intrusion into his body proves otherwise. The “cutting” was incomplete, and now the parasites have returned in a new, far more personal form.

## Reading Guide
This is one of those rare chapters where the battle is entirely internal—no swords drawn in the physical world, but a full-scale war in the spiritual one. What makes it so gripping is the role reversal. Danyangzi, a man who has spent centuries brutalizing and exploiting others, is suddenly the one whose worldview shatters. Li Huowang, the perpetual victim, takes the driver’s seat and shows he has learned the game. His taunts are not random cruelty; they are precise strikes at Danyangzi’s greatest weakness: the terror of having sacrificed everything for a lie.

Pay special attention to the hallucination-deluge. Both Li Huowang’s and Danyangzi’s mental debris—dead disciples, betrayed allies, grotesque memories—flood the Heaven-Outside-Heaven space. The novel makes no distinction between whose ghost is whose; in this fused consciousness, suffering is shared and weaponized. The climax, where Li Huowang reverses direction and drags them into the White Jade Capital, is a stunning act of spiteful rebirth. He doesn’t kill Danyangzi; he *invests* him in a eternal nightmare. And for a man like Li Huowang, that counts as a win.

## Deep Dive: The Real Meaning of the Mao Gate
It’s worth unpacking the **Mao Gate** a bit more, because this is where the novel’s folk-horror DNA really shines. In Chinese rural tradition, gates and doors are heavily ritualized—the wrong threshold can lead to spiritual catastrophe. “Nantian Men” is a known, bureaucratic address in the celestial map. “Mao Gate” is not. It sounds like a mistake, like a sign a trickster spirit painted in the middle of the night. This is the Zuowandao’s territory: the path one thinks leads to salvation actually ends in a slaughterhouse.

Furthermore, “牦” (Mao) shares phonetic and graphic elements with “毛” (hair/rough), lending the gate an animalistic, unrefined quality. It’s the backdoor of the universe, a cattle-gate to the gods’ larder. Danyangzi’s final, horrified recognition that the immortals he sought are just residents of a cosmic butcher-shop is the most devastating moment in his character arc—and Li Huowang, laughing on the inside the entire time, makes sure he pays the admission fee.
