## Chapter Overview
Hold on to your guanmao hats, fellow Daoists, because the Zuowandao just rolled into town with a full deck—and they’re not here for a friendly game of mahjong. After last chapter’s philosophical bombshells, we’re yanked straight back into the grimy, high-stakes underworld of Shangjing, where Li Huowang is playing interrogator with a captured North Wind. Thanks to Zhuge Yuan’s absurdly overpowered almanac, the liar can’t lie, but as Li Huowang knows all too well, a Zuowandao can deceive you with nothing but truth. The chapter crackles with tension, dry humor, and the cold pragmatism of a man who has been burned by tricks one too many times. By the end, he’s off to the Supervisory Heavenly Office with a wild warning, and we’re left wondering: what is the Liang Emperor really doing, and what does a full Zuowandao mobilization mean for everyone who thought they were safe?

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang, with Zhuge Yuan’s almanac enforcing veracity, interrogates the captured North Wind. The Zuowandao member confirms three others of the same name have entered Shangjing, and lists the entire batch: three Eights, two Nines, four North Winds, and—unnervingly—two Red Centers.
- North Wind reveals the Zuowandao were sent by “the Dice Boss” to investigate the Liang Emperor’s secret activities, confirming that the capital is the center of a brewing storm involving both the imperial court and the supernatural sect.
- Li Huowang deduces that the Zuowandao’s information is all true but likely phrased or structured to mislead, demonstrating his seasoned paranoia.
- Using the Heart-Turbid’s corpse stored in the landscape painting, Zhuge Yuan erases the North Wind’s memories of Great Liang, transforming him back into a harmless old woman who is guided safely out of the compound.
- Li Huowang breaks his promise to aid Zhuge Yuan, citing the extreme urgency of the Zuowandao threat, and rushes to the Supervisory Heavenly Office to sound the alarm, dramatically announcing that the entire Zuowandao has come to Shangjing.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Zuowandao Rank Structure aka The Mahjong Hierarchy**—This is one of the most *deliciously* unhinged organizational systems in xianxia. The Zuowandao doesn’t use boring titles like “Elder” or “Sect Leader”; they name their ranks after mahjong tiles. A “North Wind” (北风) is roughly mid-tier; “Eight Bamboos” (八条) and “Nine Bamboos” (九条) are higher; and a “Red Center” (红中) is top-tier elite, a warning label that makes Li Huowang instantly tense up. The “Dice” (骰子) is their leader, a title that screams “I control probability, fate, and your cognitive stability.” This ties beautifully into their core philosophy: life is a game they play to win, and everyone else is just a tile on the board.

**The Almanac as a Weapon**—Zhuge Yuan’s “old almanac” is more than a quaint antique. Traditional Chinese almanacs (黄历/lunar calendar) dictate auspicious and taboo actions for each day. An “old almanac” that can *enforce* a taboo—like “No Lying”—is a terrifying exorcism tool disguised as a reference book. It’s a wonderfully xianxia concept: turn an everyday cultural object into a divine-level constraint artifact. The Zuowandao, masters of deception, are helpless against its cold, printed authority.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in “truth as a weapon.” Li Huowang knows the North Wind isn’t lying, but he knows *that doesn’t matter*. The Zuowandao can weave a perfect net of true statements that still lead you straight into a trap: a lie by omission, a lie by context, a lie by the way the truth is arranged. It’s the kind of intellectual horror that makes this novel so special. The enemy doesn’t need to deceive you with falsehoods—they can ruin you with facts. Watch how Li Huowang treats everything he hears with surgical suspicion even *after* confirming it’s true. And note the fascinating character beat at the end: Zhuge Yuan is so good-natured he helps the amnesiac old lady shuffle out the door, while Li Huowang is already sprinting across rooftops to sound an alarm. Heroism comes in very different shapes in this world.

## Deeper Dive: The Heart-Turbid’s Last Job
The scene where Zhuge Yuan uses the corpse of the Heart-Turbid in his painting to erase memories is a subtle but massive flex of worldbuilding. The Heart-Turbid’s power was “hiding” or “erasing” concepts. Here, Zhuge Yuan uses its dead body as a tool to surgically carve memories from a living person’s brain. This isn’t just a convenient plot mechanism—it’s a brutal demonstration that these supernatural faculties don’t stop being dangerous even after their owner is dead. In the Dao-Twisted World, a corpse is still a loaded gun. The fact that the North Wind can be reverted to an innocent old lady, with no memory of the Zuowandao or Great Liang, also raises chilling questions: How many of the “normal” people we meet are former supernatural operatives who have been “retired” by a similar erasure?
