## Chapter Overview

Alright, folks, strap in. This chapter is a full-throttle brawl with zero brakes. Li Huowang is cornered, outnumbered, and out-talked by a Zuowandao trickster who's flipped the entire script—suddenly *he's* the monster, and the mob of grieving, furious Lai family bandits is buying every single lie. What follows is messy, brutal, and deeply satisfying carnage as Li Huowang and his crew fight for their lives. But the real punch? It lands in the final moments, with a gambit so desperate and so visceral it redefines what "paying the price" means in this world. Get ready for body horror, tactical fury, and that signature *Dao Guai Yi Xian* blend of folk-horror violence.

## Key Plot Points

- Erbing publicly denounces Li Huowang as Danyangzi, pinning every atrocity—mercury flayings, buried-alive victims—on him. The Lai family mob, already grieving, believes her without question.
- Li Huowang tries to tell the truth, but Erbing’s earlier framing is airtight. No one listens.
- The bandits charge the wagon. Li Huowang spots Bai Lingmiao grazed by a barbed crossbow bolt—her injury ignites his fury.
- He leaps into the fray, sword in hand. His newly learned technique sharpens fast on living targets. He cuts down an old man, a youth, and others, all while screaming that he's innocent.
- Erbing cloaks her presence, avoiding Li Huowang’s supernatural perception. She reveals herself briefly with a taunting smile before hiding again.
- Li Huowang uses *The Thousand Greats Record*: pulling his own molars and detonating them as shrapnel, clearing a path.
- Gao Zhijian and Chun Xiaoman arrive as backup. Bai Lingmiao’s spirit-veiled Second Spirit enters the fight with ferocious bites.
- Erbing, cornered, lashes out: a charmed die flies into Gao Zhijian’s temple. He falls.
- Erbing begins a domino-tile incantation, the tiles softening into the earth—but Li Huowang’s severed arm bursts from the ground, clutching the tiles, and drives into her abdomen, crushing her spine.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Nuo Masks & Mob Justice.** The Lai family members wear Nuo opera masks, which in the Dao-Twisted World double as ritual armor and identity concealment. Historically, Nuo masks are part of an ancient Chinese exorcism tradition—performers don these exaggerated wooden faces to frighten away plague demons. Here, the masks serve the opposite purpose: they turn the wearers into a vengeful, faceless mob, stripping away individual identity in favor of collective fury.

**The Thousand Greats Record (《大千录》).** Li Huowang’s go-to survival grimoire. Each page contains a self-mutilation ritual that trades agony for supernatural effect. In this chapter, he uses it to detonate his own molars as fragmentation grenades. This is not a spell—it’s a transaction. Pay in bone and blood, get a tactical edge. No mana. No cooldown. Just pain and reward.

**Domino Tiles as Ritual Tools.** Erbing uses *pai jiu* (Chinese dominoes) as foci for her incantations. In Chinese folk magic and gambling culture, domino tiles are associated with chance, fate, and hidden patterns. Erbing’s chant (“seven-eight-no-nine…”) is a corruption of a traditional dice or domino saying—the kind you’d hear in a smoky gambling den. Here, it’s weaponized. The tiles become mud, burrowing into the earth to set up some unseen trap. It never completes.

### Bang Bing Jue (帮兵决)

A traditional northeastern Chinese spirit-summoning verse; its rapid, urgent rhythm belongs to the chuma medium tradition. Referenced as the "spirit-summoning verses" during Bai Lingmiao's appearance.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is Li Huowang at his most unhinged, but also his most *humanly relatable*. He’s fighting not for glory or power—he’s fighting because someone he cares about got hurt. That crossbow graze on Bai Lingmiao’s shoulder is the emotional trigger. Everything after is pure, unfiltered protective rage. Notice how he yells the truth through gritted teeth, even as he cuts down the old man whose grandson he did *not* kill. That tragedy of mistaken identity—the bandits are grieving real losses, and Erbing fed them a convenient scapegoat—makes this fight feel messier than a simple good-vs-evil clash.

Also: the Gao Zhijian moment hurts. Hard. Just when Li Huowang’s crew starts to feel like they’re gaining momentum, Erbing snipes the strongest fighter with one charmed die. And Li Huowang’s response? He severs his own arm—and uses it as a guided missile. That final, gruesome image is pure *Dao Guai Yi Xian*: a victory that costs you a piece of yourself, literally and irreversibly. Pay attention to how the severed arm “clenched like forged steel.” The body itself becomes a weapon, even in pieces. That’s the kind of horror-meets-tactics this novel lives for.
