## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang is back in the modern world, and it feels *wrong*. Riding a public bus like a normal person, he calls Yang Na under the flimsy cover of a delivery driver just to make sure she’s safe. It’s a quiet, heartbreaking moment of restraint—he *wants* to talk to her, but he knows his presence is a threat. That fragile peace shatters when a familiar face spots him: Old Liu, a fellow mental patient from the hospital. Li Huowang’s attempt at a low-profile trip to the dock turns into a violent detour as he hunts down a weapon and silences the witness. The chapter ends with Li Huowang standing before the sprawling dock, facing the question of how to breach the fortress where Xu Shou waits.

## Key Plot Points

1. **The Phone Call**: Li Huowang calls Yang Na disguised as a courier. He confirms she’s safe and hiding from him on purpose, then blocks her number to prevent further contact.
2. **Recognition**: Old Liu, the patient whose dentures Li Huowang once punched out, spots him on the bus and panics, fleeing at the next stop.
3. **Interrogation and Restraint**: Li Huowang chases Old Liu down, threatens him with a saw, and drags him back to his empty apartment.
4. **Weapon Selection**: Instead of leaving immediately, Li Huowang raids Old Liu’s kitchen for a proper weapon, settling on a long boning knife.
5. **The Destination**: Li Huowang arrives at the dock. The chapter ends with him staring at the stacked shipping containers, contemplating how to infiltrate Xu Shou’s territory.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter operates almost entirely in the “modern reality” track, so the xianxia vocabulary is sparse. However, one key element deserves attention: **Li Huowang’s weapon swap**. In the Dao-Twisted World, his primary weapon was often a ritual or self-mutilating tool—saws, hooks, needles that draw blood and pay a price. Here, he picks up a **boning knife** from a civilian kitchen. The shift is deliberate: he is operating under modern-world rules, where *utility* trumps *ritual cost*. This is a classic sign of Li Huowang’s dual-consciousness: he knows he can’t use his insane monastery tricks anymore, so he scavenges like any desperate criminal.

Also worth noting is the **`Roosters and dogs ascend to heaven`** idiom, which Lyu Xiucai weaponized in earlier chapters. Li Huowang doesn’t say it here, but the theme of *contempt for dependent beneficiaries* runs under his actions—he refuses to let Old Liu be a loose end that brings the police down on him.

### Roosters and dogs ascend to heaven

A Chinese idiom meaning that when someone rises to power, even their lowliest dependents get dragged up with them. Lyu Xiucai twisted it into contempt for sharing success with family.

## Reading Guide

This is a transitional chapter, and it reads like a ticking clock. Li Huowang is completely alone, scavenging for tools and handling threats the *normal* way—no sorcery, no self-mutilation, just raw human desperation. The phone call with Yang Na is the emotional core: he can’t even let himself hear her voice properly. The encounter with Old Liu is almost darkly comedic—a terrified old man being taped to his own toilet—but it underlines how far Li Huowang has fallen from “normal people” rules. Notice how he corrects Old Liu: *“How many times do I have to tell you? I’m not crazy!”* That line isn’t just frustration; it’s a man trying to claw back his own identity. The dock at the end is a great visual cliffhanger—a concrete barrier that reminds us Li Huowang is up against logistical reality, not just supernatural horrors.
