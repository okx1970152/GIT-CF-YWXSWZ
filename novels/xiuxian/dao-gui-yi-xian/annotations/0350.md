## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow Daoists—we’re heading straight into trauma central. This chapter pulls back the dusty veil on Bai Lingmiao’s childhood memories, but instead of warm fuzzies, we get the kind of family secret that makes your stomach drop into your boots. The Bai family shrine wasn’t built on prayers and piety—it was built on people. Literally. Hundreds of bodies, packed like bricks, with painted lotuses on their hands. And the worst part? There’s no one left to yell at. Li Huowang already turned them all to ash. So now Bai Lingmiao has to do the only thing she can: bury the dead, bow to every grave, and sit with the horrifying realization that the people who raised her were monsters. And yet… she still can’t forgive the man who killed them. Oof.

## Key Plot Points

- Bai Lingmiao relives a childhood memory: her grandmother comforting her on the spot where a terrifying entity scared her. The trigger: she finally remembers what that entity was.
- She digs into the hard-packed earth of the main hall, and others join her. Gouwa expects treasure; instead, they find a buried woman with stitched-shut eyes.
- More bodies are uncovered: adults, children, all arranged like bricks along the hall’s foundations. Every hand bears a painted lotus. This was no random massacre—it was a structural foundation made of human corpses.
- Gouwa’s horrified slip of the tongue (“What kind of monster…”) reminds everyone that this is the Bai family shrine. The culprits are Bai Lingmiao’s own parents—already dead by Li Huowang’s fire.
- Bai Lingmiao breaks down. She cries until she has no tears left, then forces herself to take responsibility. She organizes the burial of every corpse, kowtowing and offering incense at each grave.
- Lü Zhuangyuan offers a grim, paternal theory: her parents knew they were inviting divine punishment, so they kept her in the dark and planned to marry her off, hoping to shield her from their sins.
- In the quiet aftermath, Bai Lingmiao admits to Chun Xiaoman: even knowing what her family did, she still cannot forgive Li Huowang for killing them. The chapter ends on that knife-twist.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Human Pillars (人柱)** — This is not a made-up horror gimmick. The tradition of burying living people (or their corpses) as ritual foundations has deep roots in ancient Chinese construction mythology. The most famous example is the legend of the **Ganjiang and Moye** swords, where human sacrifice was used to forge powerful weapons. Historically, some large-scale projects (walls, bridges, temples) were rumored to have “human foundations” to appease earth spirits. In the Dao-Twisted World, this folk belief is literalized: the Bai family didn’t just build a shrine; they built a vessel for power on a mesh of suffering.

**The Lotus and the White Lotus Society** — The lotus painted on each victim’s hand isn’t just a decoration; it’s a signature. In Chinese folk religion and secret society culture, the **White Lotus Society (白莲教, Báilián Jiào)** was a syncretic, millenarian tradition often centered around the **Unborn Venerable Mother (Wusheng Laomu)** . Its members used lotus imagery to mark their belonging. Here, the lotus doesn’t mean blessing—it means the dead were offerings, branded for a purpose Bai Lingmiao is only beginning to suspect.

### Unborn Venerable Mother (无生老母, Wusheng Laomu)

A high goddess in White Lotus cosmology, seen as the creator and ultimate savior who will call her children back to the "Native Land of True Emptiness." Her presence in the Bai family suggests their violence had a cosmic purpose.

## Reading Guide

If you’ve been following the story for Bai Lingmiao’s softness, this chapter is your wake-up call. Her family wasn’t just “morally gray”—they were full-on horror-movie villains who used living people as concrete. And the genius of this chapter is that it doesn’t let her (or us) deflect the pain onto a living target. Li Huowang already played executioner. She’s left with the clean-up duty and the crushing question: *how do you reconcile love for the people who raised you with the fact that they were monsters?*  

Lü Zhuangyuan’s explanation is painfully real. Some parents *do* shield their children from the worst of themselves—not out of kindness, but out of a twisted sense of “protection.” Bai Lingmiao was kept pure *so she could be sold off clean.* That detail stings more than any gore.

And that final line—*I still can’t forgive him*—is a gut punch. She knows the logic. She knows Li Huowang was probably right to do it. But knowing doesn’t heal the orphan’s hole in her chest. This is character work at its finest.
