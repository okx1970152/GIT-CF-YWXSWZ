## Chapter Overview
Li Huowang digs himself deeper into the Ao-Jing Sect's cave, securing a personal chamber and a traumatized servant named Yingzi. But he's not here to settle in—he's scheming. After his bold pledge to Shou San to find "extreme agony" on his own, our favorite paranoid Heart-Element quickly realizes he's hit a dead end... or has he? Watch as Li Huowang pivots from desperate compliance to active intel-gathering, proving once again that the best way to survive a cult is to recruit your own spy from within.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang refuses Shou San's implicit offer to use Yingzi as a tool for self-harm, declaring he would rather die than become like Danyangzi.
- Shou San punishes a snickering cultist (Yan Liu) by fusing his features and limbs into a living "maggot," reinforcing the sect's terrifying control over pain and healing.
- Li Huowang is given a fully equipped cave chamber with a latrine, his sword, and Mantou the dog.
- Li Huowang comforts Yingzi, learning she is the sole survivor of a town massacre, and successfully recruits her as an informant by offering her a place in his group.
- Li Huowang begins to gather intelligence on the Ao-Jing Sect, learning of their dual-origin mythology (Gu-Spirit and Bashe) and their heretical healing practices.
- Yingzi reveals she has been with the sect for four years and has witnessed them "treat" and cure people.
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang probing for knowledge of any other Ao-Jing techniques beyond the *Thousand Greats Record*, signaling his intent to find a backdoor solution.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
The naming convention of the Ao-Jing Sect is a fascinating blend. Their names (手叁 Shou San, 眼陆 Yan Liu, 耳玖 Er Jiu) are composed of a "body organ" character (hand, eye, ear) plus a number. This isn't just decorative—it likely reflects an internal hierarchy based on the specific Bodily Field (身田) they cultivate, with the organ representing their specialized "sensory vessel" for receiving or channeling pain/energy. In Daoist body-cultivation, different organs are tied to different energetic functions; here, the Ao-Jing Sect seems to have weaponized this into a literal caste system.

The chapter also drops a juicy lore bomb: the Ao-Jing Sect is a merger of two faiths—one worshipping the Gu-Spirit (牯神) and one worshipping Bashe (巴虺). The "Gu-Spirit" is depicted as a charred skull full of envy, having stolen a portion of Bashe's power. This mythology feels raw, unpolished, and deeply *functional*—the sect's power isn't derived from a benevolent deity but from a parasite that learned to play the host.

## Reading Guide
Li Huowang's "recruitment pitch" to Yingzi is a masterclass in his post-Zhengde Temple growth. Notice how he doesn't command or bully—he actively *listens* first, validates her trauma, then presents her with a transaction that offers genuine escape. This is the same man who was catatonic just a few chapters ago, folks! His character development is running in fast-forward, fueled by paranoia and a ferocious will to not repeat past mistakes.

Also, take a moment to appreciate the contrast between Shou San's cold logic (blending a man into a maggot for laughing out of turn) and his casual admission that they are *expert healers*. The Ao-Jing Sect’s power is horrifying because it is disciplined, precise, and utterly amoral. They can shape flesh like clay—and they will, whether to punish or to cure. This is the kind of "supernatural skill" that makes you very, very careful about what you ask for.
