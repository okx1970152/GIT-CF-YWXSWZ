## Chapter Overview

Our ragged band of wanderers finally makes it to the capital, and the culture shock hits like a faceful of cold reality. Lü Zhuangyuan’s dream of buying a theater gets laughed into the gutter by local rich men who treat his life savings like pocket change, forcing the old actor to confront just how far down the social ladder he really is. Meanwhile, Gouwa unveils his brilliant plan for finding Li Huowang in a city of a million people: bribing beggars. It’s a chapter of simmering resentment, class humiliation, and the quiet desperation of people who know they don’t belong here—but have nowhere else to go.

## Key Plot Points

- The capital’s sheer scale and wealth are established through small, crushing details: market goods that would be rare anywhere else are utterly commonplace, and even the cheapest inn is extortionate.
- Lü Zhuangyuan overhears local merchants and squires discussing theater properties and makes a pitiful attempt to join the conversation; he is ridiculed for his lack of money and status as a “low-class actor,” and returns to the table in silence to finish his noodles.
- Lü Xiucai’s simmering rage at his father boils over after the humiliation; he threatens to go back and kill the “old bastard,” but his older brother Lü Juren physically restrains him from starting a fight inside the inn.
- Lü Xiucai declares himself a “man with divine powers now” and disavows the acting profession entirely, claiming the insults were for his family, not for him—a fragile, self-serving distinction that reveals his growing delusion of superiority.
- Gouwa calms him down and takes him on a tour of the capital’s tavern and inn doorways, where he uses a string of copper coins to recruit beggars as informants, dangling the reward for anyone who can lead him to a red-robed Daoist and a white-skinned woman.
- One beggar recalls that “Little Pomelo” delivered a letter for someone matching that description six days ago, but Little Pomelo never returned from the south side of the city—an ominous detail suggesting that Li Huowang’s presence has already disturbed the local underworld.
- Gouwa sends the beggars out to search, then turns back with smug satisfaction, leaving Lü Xiucai half-convinced but deeply uneasy.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Nine Classes (九流) and Social Mobility**: This chapter is a brutal reminder that in the Dao-Twisted World, your profession determines your place in the cosmic hierarchy, and that place is nearly impossible to escape. The “low-class actor” (*下九流*) insult isn’t just a slur—it’s a legal and social reality codified in traditional Chinese class systems. Actors, prostitutes, and entertainers occupied the absolute bottom tier, barred from owning certain properties and subject to casual contempt from merchants who were themselves only one rung higher. Lü Zhuangyuan’s dream of buying a theater in the capital isn’t just ambitious—it’s laughable precisely *because* the system is designed to keep him out. This isn’t merely prejudice; it’s structural violence dressed in polite laughter.

**Beggars as Intelligence Networks**: Using beggars as spies and informants is a classic trope in Chinese martial arts and Jianghu fiction. In a society where the poor are invisible to the wealthy, beggars can move through streets, alleys, and back doors without drawing attention. A string of copper coins—a pittance to the rich—is a fortune to them, making them the most cost-effective and far-reaching intelligence network available to outsiders like Gouwa. This tactic appears in countless wuxia stories, from Louis Cha’s *Demi-Gods and Semi-Devils* to underdog heroes in modern web novels. Gouwa may be lazy and sly, but he knows how the world’s forgotten people work.

**The Copper Coin Sword (铜钱剑)**: Lü Xiucai is still repairing his half-finished copper coin sword by threading coins onto a hemp-bound hilt. In Daoist ritual, a sword made of 108 old coins strung on red cord is a powerful exorcism tool, used to cut through evil spirits, negative energy, and demonic influence. Each coin represents a unit of yang energy (from circulation and human touch), and the red cord represents life force. That Lü Xiucai is painstakingly repairing his—even while griping about his father—suggests that despite his bratty demeanor, he takes his cultivation seriously enough to maintain his gear in the middle of a city street.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a palate cleanser and a positioning move—no epic battles, no cosmic horror, just the quiet, grinding misery of class warfare inside a noodle shop. But don’t let the mundane setting fool you. The real tension here isn’t between Lü Zhuangyuan and the merchant who laughed at him; it’s between Lü Xiucai’s growing sense of superiority (he has *divine powers* now!) and the cold reality that those powers didn’t change his family’s social standing one bit. He can kill his father if he wants to, but that won’t make the city let him in. Keep an eye on that resentment—it’s a fuse, and Li Huowang might be the one who lights it next time these two paths cross. Also, Gouwa using beggars as an impromptu spy network is the smartest thing anyone in this troupe has done all arc. Respect the hustle.
