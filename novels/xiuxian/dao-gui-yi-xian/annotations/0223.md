## Chapter Overview
Alright, fellow cult-traveler, buckle up. Chapter 223, “Baolu,” is a tense, quiet-fire chapter that does what *Dao Gui Yi Xian* does best: it lures you into a character moment, then drop-kicks you into a reality-check. Li Huowang finally confronts Sun Baolu about the human bones, and what follows isn’t a fight—it’s a clash of worldviews. We get a full, chilling exposition of the Qingqiu cosmology and the Heavenly Eternal, a glimpse into Sun Baolu’s hidden trauma, and a closing panel that is *so* perfectly ominous it feels like a jump scare delivered through cultural dissonance. Get ready for a lore dump that actually *hurts*, a character almost breaking, and the slow, creeping realization that in this world, *normal* is just another word for *something you haven't learned to fear yet*.

## Key Plot Points
- **The Bone Confrontation**: Li Huowang corners Sun Baolu about the “horse bone” lie. Sun Baolu, cornered, is forced to give a real answer: Qingqiu sky burial is a normalized practice where the dead are fed to vultures and their bones are worn as trophies of courage and strength. It’s not malice; it’s a cultural constant.
- **The Heavenly Eternal’s Cosmology**: Sun Baolu reveals the core of his faith. The Eternal Sky is the *shadow* of a god called the Heavenly Eternal, cast by two candles. The sun is one candle, the moon is the god’s navel. This is a desperate, animistic view of a universe held in a deity’s hands, in stark contrast to the fragmented, suffering reality Li Huowang has witnessed.
- **The Lie of a Better World**: Li Huowang’s internal monologue falters. He has seen the insane, agonizing truth behind all these worldviews. But he *forces* himself to consider the unbearable alternative: maybe his insanity is the lie, and the beautiful lies of others are the truth. It’s a devastating moment of self-doubt.
- **Baolu’s Hidden Secret**: Li Huowang pushes one step too far. He asks why Sun Baolu keeps his clothes wrapped so tightly. The reaction is violent—not physically, but emotionally. Sun Ba禄 threatens suicide if pushed further, revealing a trauma he *cannot* share, not out of malice, but out of shame.
- **The Closing Frame**: The chapter ends not with an answer, but with a question. Li Huowang stops at the tent entrance, because a light on the street is “too scattered.” Sun Baolu’s baffled reply? “That’s a lion dance. You don’t have those where you’re from?” The horror of the mundane has arrived.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
Let’s unpack the **Heavenly Eternal** (*Chang Sheng Tian*, 长生天). This is not a random name. It’s a direct reference to the supreme sky deity worshipped by the Mongol tribes of the steppe, also known as *Möngke Tengri*. Throughout history, different cultures on the fringes of China—like the Mongols, the Xiongnu, and the northern nomads—worshipped a vast, impersonal sky-father who was the source of all life and power. Sun Baolu’s description—the sun as a candle, the sky as a shadow—is a terrifyingly poetic, animistic reduction of that old faith. In the context of this novel, it’s yet another *pure, organized, beautiful* lie that the broken people of this world cling to, directly contradicting the hellish, screaming reality of the White Jade Capital and the Great Nuo.

And then there’s the **Lion Dance** (*Wu Shi*, 舞狮). In our world, it’s a festive, celebratory performance for Lunar New Year and grand openings, meant to scare away evil spirits with noise and martial movement. But in the Dao-Twisted World? Nothing is just festive. When an ancient exorcistic tradition appears in a town *at night*, and the only person who doesn’t see it as a threat is a local who grew up with it… you know the author is setting up a double-barreled shotgun of a scene for the next chapter. *Trust your instincts, Li Huowang. That thing probably isn’t supposed to be here without a crowd watching it.*

## Reading Guide
This is one of those chapters where the *absence* of violence does all the heavy lifting. Pay attention to the psychological trap Li Huowang sets for himself. He has irrefutable, cosmic-level proof that the universe is a broken, screaming asylum. But his own isolation is so profound that he considers *doubt* a viable survival strategy. “Maybe the pretty lie is better than my ugly truth.” That is the *real* horror of the Heart-Element: you can never trust your own perceptions, so every divine cosmology you hear becomes a potential home you are forbidden to enter.

Also, keep an eye on Sun Baolu. The boy is carrying something that makes him react to a simple question about his clothing with a suicide threat. In a world of flesh buds and gut-churning alchemy, a purely *psychological* scar hidden under a wrapped coat is a rare, deeply human kind of terror. And finally, brace yourself for the lion dance. In the economy of *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, a chapter that ends with “oh, that’s not a monster, it’s just a folk tradition” is a chapter that is *lying to you*.
