## Chapter Overview

Get ready, because Li Huowang finally gets to throw down with a Zuowandao—and not just any impersonator, but one who’s been masquerading as the Great Qi Emperor himself. This isn’t a quiet infiltration or a test of wits; it’s a full-frontal, fire-and-teeth assault that pays off chapters of tension. Zhuge Yuan steps up with a brush and almanac that rewrite the rules on the spot, and the chapter ends on a classic Zuowandao twist: the enemy you thought you cornered was just a burning shadow puppet all along. It’s action-packed, lore-heavy, and leaves you questioning how deep the deception goes.

## Key Plot Points

- The Great Qi Emperor arrives in an absurdly oversized, top-heavy chariot that looks like a “bloated stillborn infant,” setting an immediate tone of grotesque spectacle.
- Li Huowang instantly deduces the emperor is a Zuowandao impostor by the unnatural way the “child” looks at people—like seeing through flesh to bone.
- Zhuge Yuan unveils a critical ability: his old almanac and wolf-hair brush can impose restrictions on reality, writing taboos that physically distort the disguised Zuowandao members.
- Li Huowang goes full berserker mode: he rips out his own teeth, sets himself on fire, and uses the Black Tai Sui's tentacle to deliver a pain-sharing attack that catches the fleeing enemy.
- The final reveal—only a shadow puppet lies burning inside the false emperor—confirms the Zuowandao's escape, leaving Li Huowang’s victory hollow and the mystery intact.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

Zhuge Yuan’s trick with the yellowed almanac is a joy to break down. In traditional Chinese culture, the *huangli* (皇历 or 黄历, “imperial almanac”) is a calendar that specifies which days are auspicious for which activities—a practical guide for everything from weddings to breaking ground on a building. What Zhuge Yuan does here is weaponize that system. He writes a specific date and imposes taboos against lying and face-changing, effectively forcing the *heavens* (or the narrative itself) to enforce those rules on his enemies. It’s a beautiful example of how the novel takes a folk-level, mundane belief system and turns it into a real, binding supernatural law. The “taboo” system here mirrors real almanac conventions, where each day has both auspicious (*yi*, 宜) and inauspicious (*ji*, 忌) entries. Zhuge Yuan is essentially hacking fate using calendar logic.

### Seven apertures (七窍)

The seven openings in the human head (eyes, ears, nostrils, mouth); in Daoist and folk belief, bleeding from these is a sign of extreme qi disturbance or supernatural backlash.

## Reading Guide

Li Huowang’s opening move—yanking out his own teeth—isn’t just shock value; it’s a direct callback to his time under Danyangzi, where he learned to weaponize self-mutilation. The fact that he does it *joyfully* (“You’re finally here!”) is the real character beat here: he’s no longer a victim of body horror, but its active, grinning practitioner. Keep an eye on how the Zuowandao’s escape—leaving only a puppet—connects to earlier lore about “shadow puppets” and illusions. This chapter doesn’t just advance the fight; it crystallizes the philosophical divide between “cultivating the true” and “cultivating the false.” Li Huowang burns everything down with raw, painful reality; the Zuowandao slips away with a clever, fake-out veneer. The game isn’t over, and the next move belongs to the deceivers.
