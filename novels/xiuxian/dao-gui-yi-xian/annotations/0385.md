## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow Daoists—the fallout from the Purple-Tasseled Sword is *real* and it’s hitting the group like a freight train. Bai Lingmiao, once the soft-spoken anchor of the group, walks into the New Year’s Eve dinner holding a wine glass like she owns the place, and proceeds to drop the bombshell that she touched Li Huowang’s corrupted blade. The chapter splits into two stark halves: one half is a character study of just how badly the sword has rewired Bai Lingmiao’s personality—she’s cheerful, assertive, and frankly a little scary in her new lack of inhibition—and the other half follows Li Huowang’s grim, silent journey toward the coast. The two storylines don’t intersect, but they echo each other in a deeply unsettling way.

## Key Plot Points

- Bai Lingmiao shocks everyone at the New Year’s Eve feast by acting with bold, uninhibited confidence; she toasts the villagers and casually admits she touched Li Huowang’s *military sword*, causing immediate panic.
- Chun Xiaoman confronts Bai Lingmiao privately, blaming herself for not watching her closely; Bai Lingmiao dismisses her guilt with startling pragmatism, saying she feels “a lot more comfortable” now.
- Chun Xiaoman accidentally reveals she has been hiding the existence of a hidden martial or cultivation text from the Bai family ancestral hall; Bai Lingmiao immediately zeroes in on this.
- Bai Lingmiao rushes to the Bai family ancestral hall, finds a second hidden chamber within the first, and discovers a library of White Lotus texts stored in pristine condition.
- While Bai Lingmiao is ecstatic and begins cataloging her inheritance, the Second Spirit (Er Shen) breaks down in miserable sobs in a corner, revealing that Bai Lingmiao’s personality shift has left the gentle side of her soul displaced and grieving.
- Li Huowang arrives at a coastal town, having borrowed three extra horses from courier stations under his Supervisory Heavenly Office credentials; the journey is narrated with cold, pragmatic efficiency.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The *White Lotus Society* (白莲教) that Bai Lingmiao is descended from isn’t just a generic “cult.” Historically, the White Lotus was a shifting network of secret religious societies that emerged during the Yuan dynasty, blending Buddhism, Manichaeism, and folk salvationism. By the Ming and Qing periods, they were infamous for millenarian uprisings centered on the *Unborn Venerable Mother* (无生老母), who promised to lead her children back to the “Native Land of True Emptiness.” The hidden library Bai Lingmiao discovers—jade lotus lamps, cowhide-wrapped scriptures, moisture-absorbing sand—is a dead ringer for how these underground societies actually preserved their forbidden texts. That she now has access to a whole tradition’s worth of techniques, both righteous and horrific, sets up a major fork in her character path.

The *military sword* (兵家剑) continues to be a cursed artifact of immense narrative weight. In Chinese martial and Daoist tradition, weapons used in warfare accumulate *sha qi* (killing intent) over time, making them dangerous to ordinary people. The Purple-Tasseled Sword’s effect on Bai Lingmiao—rewiring her personality rather than just hurting her—takes this concept to its horrifying extreme: the blade’s accumulated violence doesn’t just *taint* her, it *replaces* parts of her with an echo of its own nature.

### Military sword (兵家剑)

A sword forged for warfare and saturated with killing intent (sha qi); prolonged contact can corrode or rewrite the wielder’s personality. Bai Lingmiao’s change is the latest example.

### Unborn Venerable Mother (无生老母)

A salvific mother deity in White Lotus belief; she promises to call her children back to the “Native Land of True Emptiness” (真空家乡).

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in tonal whiplash, and it’s *glorious*. Bai Lingmiao’s transformation is genuinely unnerving—not because she’s become evil, but because she’s become *confident*. She’s laughing, she’s swearing, she’s calling her past self a pushover. The real horror isn’t that she’s possessed; it’s that she’s having a great time. Chun Xiaoman’s grief and the Second Spirit’s sobbing in the corner are the emotional anchors that remind us something precious has been lost. Pay close attention to Bai Lingmiao’s line about Li Huowang: “I’m not Li Huowang.” She’s drawing a clear line between her own chosen corruption and his suffering—but given what we know about how the sword works, the reader has to wonder how much of that choice is really hers.

Li Huowang’s section is a quiet, bloody coda—four galloping horses, a skinless face stung by sea salt, and an arrival at a town that promises nothing but more work. The contrast couldn’t be sharper: Bai Lingmiao is in a library, drunk on possibility; Li Huowang is on a windswept hill, already calculating his next move. The chapter ends not with a bang, but with the damp, salty inhalation of a man who has no time to process anything other than the next task.
