## Chapter Overview

Alright, fellow travellers, buckle up. Chapter 104 is a masterclass in tonal whiplash. We begin with Li Huowang coming home to a surprise wedding setup, proceed through actual warmth and intimacy (yes, *that* kind), and then, in the final moments, have the floor drop out from under us with one of the most viscerally unsettling identity-twists the novel has delivered so far. It's a chapter about trust, union, and the horrifying speed at which both can be poisoned. Get ready for a wedding night you won't forget—and not for the reasons you'd expect.

## Key Plot Points

Li Huowang returns to his room to find it decked out like a traditional bridal chamber, with Bai Lingmiao waiting for him in full red wedding regalia. After a brief moment of hesitation, he accepts the situation. They exchange vows on their own terms, and the chapter transitions into an intimate, consensual consummation of their union. In the aftermath, Li Huowang shares a confidence with his new wife: his physical senses—taste, touch, pain—have all been dramatically heightened, a side effect of Bashe's power. This leads to a moment of quiet tenderness where Bai Lingmiao tests the theory by biting him, then soothing the wound.

But the hammer drops in the final paragraphs. A knock at the door. A familiar voice. Bai Lingmiao's voice, calling from outside, claiming she woke up in the woodshed. In that frozen instant, Li Huowang realizes the "Bai Lingmiao" in his bed is an impostor. The creature slips away with a giggle. When he lights the candle, he's alone. And the bite wound on his finger is not a human mark—it's the gnash of a beast.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The opening of this chapter leans heavily into Chinese traditional wedding symbolism: red candles, red double-happiness (囍) characters, red bedclothes, and the red bridal veil for the bride. Red in Chinese culture symbolizes joy, prosperity, and warding off evil spirits—making it deeply ironic here as it becomes the backdrop for horror.

Bai Lingmiao's line, "A married chicken follows the rooster; a married dog follows the dog" (嫁鸡随鸡嫁狗随狗), is a classical Chinese proverb about marital fidelity. It means a wife should follow her husband's fate, for better or worse, regardless of circumstances. It's both sweet and tragic, given the context of the world they live in.

The ritual of asking the groom to blow out the candle before the bride removes her veil is a folk tradition tied to modesty and the gradual unveiling of intimacy. Li Huowang "sweeping his sleeve" to extinguish the flame adds a theatrical, almost performative quality to the act.

The heightened senses Li Huowang describes—touch, smell, taste, and pain—echo the mechanics of certain Daoist body-refinement or "ascetic cultivation" paths, where extreme sensory feedback is linked to spiritual progress or sacrificial power.

## Reading Guide

This chapter stands as one of the most emotionally complex in the novel's early arc. The first half is almost tender: Li Huowang, for all his trauma and paranoia, allows himself a moment of genuine connection. Bai Lingmiao's quiet determination ("I will gladly spend my life with you") shows her evolution from a timid observer into someone capable of asserting her own choices. Pay attention to how their dynamic shifts after the intimacy—Li Huowang shares secrets about Bashe he likely wouldn't have before.

That makes the ending hit all the harder. The impostor's giggle ("Heeheehee, husband~") is not just a jump scare—it's a violation of the one safe space Li Huowang just created. The beast-bite on his finger serves as physical proof that the entity was real, and that its intimacy was a trap. The question left hanging is devastating: *Did Li Huowang just marry a monster? Or was the real Bai Lingmiao locked in the woodshed the whole time?* This chapter weaponizes emotional vulnerability against the reader, and it's one of the cruelest yet most effective ambushes in the story so far.
