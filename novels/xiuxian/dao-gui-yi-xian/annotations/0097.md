## Chapter Overview
Get ready, fellow survivors. Chapter 97 is not a power-up—it’s a total breakdown. Fresh off his near-death, Li Huowang doesn’t just win a fight; he becomes a walking catastrophe. We watch our battered protagonist weaponize his own agony to borrow from Bashe, the distant, indifferent entity of pain. The cost? His body, his mind, and every shred of his humanity. And just when you think the dust has settled, the author yanks us back to the ICU, where the ultimate antagonist isn’t a five-headed ghost but a calm, smiling psychiatrist asking the one question Li Huowang can’t answer: Which world is real?

## Key Plot Points
- Still impaled by his own barbed awl, Li Huowang suffers both the physical agony of his spilled organs and the emotional torment of seeing a copy of himself with red eyes.
- He takes a desperate gamble. Sacrificing his dual extreme pain—physical and emotional—he borrows a sliver of power from Bashe, transforming his agony into a weapon of despair.
- Everything around him begins to bend, break, and die. He bisects the surrounding bamboo and monsters with a casual slash.
- Li Huowang senses the fleeing Layue Shiba and, in a horrifying twist, **grants it the ability to feel pain**—an entity that had none. Overwhelmed, it commits suicide.
- With his body utterly destroyed, Li Huowang slips into a surreal, fatalistic peace. He is neither happy to be alive nor afraid to die.
- He blacks out and awakens in the “hallucination” world of the ICU, cuffed to a bed.
- Dr. Li Xiangguo tricks him by mentioning Yang Na’s new boyfriend, catching him off guard. The doctor doesn’t scold him—he asks a disarmingly simple, devastating question: which of these two worlds feels *normal*?

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
- **Bashe (巴虺)**: Bashe is an ancient Chinese mythical python said to swallow elephants and take three years to digest them. In *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, it has been repurposed as a high-dimensional entity (a Siming) that governs the domain of “Pain.” Unlike many cultivation systems where power comes from purity or enlightenment, Li Huowang’s power here comes from a horribly literal transaction: trading agony for supernatural force. Bashe doesn’t even *notice* him—he is just stealing scraps from the god of suffering.
- **Layue Shiba (腊月十八)**: A traditional Chinese folk demon that resets its appearance, powers, and weaknesses every year on the 18th day of the 12th lunar month. Its weakness here isn’t a magic sword or a spell—it’s being forced to feel pain for the first time. The horror is psychological: an entity that *couldn’t* be hurt is suddenly made vulnerable by a man who has become a vessel for the god of pain.
- **The Thousand Greats Record (《大千录》)**: This red bamboo slip is not a spellbook in the typical sense. It is a litany of suffering. Li Huowang drives a spike back into it to trigger the connection with Bashe. This artifact represents a “suffering-cult,” a twisted path of cultivation where agony is the currency and self-mutilation is the ritual.
- **The ICU Trap**: The transition from the Dao-Twisted world to the hospital is jarring. The author uses a classic technique in psychological horror: the “return to reality” is not a relief—it is a trap. The doctor’s question isn’t hostile, but it’s the most dangerous weapon Li Huowang faces. It asks him, and the reader, to judge which world is “abnormal.”

## Reading Guide
Alright, here’s the gut-punch of this chapter. For 97 chapters, Li Huowang has been screaming about how real the other world is. And now, the author pulls the rug out from under us. The victory over Layue Shiba is horrific, triumphant, and completely insane. It feels like a climax. But what does Li Huowang wake up to? A hospital bed and a doctor who asks a question that doesn’t have an edge.

This is the core of *Dao Gui Yi Xian*. The monster isn’t the one with five heads. The monster is the question: “Which world is normal?” Because if you lean into the first one, you’re crazy. If you lean into the second, you abandon everyone you love. The pain is the only thing that feels true.

Pay attention to the phrase “I want to feel like other people.” Li Huowang’s deepest desire isn’t power, wealth, or enlightenment. It’s normalcy. Connection. A life where he doesn’t have to impale himself to save his friends. That’s the tragedy. He is a boy who just wants to heal, but the only way he can act is through self-destruction.

## Deeper Dive: The Logic of Borrowed Power
The way Li Huowang gains power in this chapter is a masterclass in the novel’s unique system. He doesn’t *ask* Bashe for help. Bashe wouldn’t hear him. Instead, he uses the *method*—the ritual of the Thousand Greats Record—to force a reaction. He doesn’t become Bashe’s champion; he becomes a leaky vessel. The “power of despair” isn’t a spell he casts; it is the *overflow* of his own suffering being radiated outwards.

This reframes his “power-up” as a leaky, dangerous phenomenon. He isn’t in control. He is a disaster zone happening to a person. It’s terrifying because it’s not a skill he can practice. It’s a state he falls into when the pain threshold is crossed. This is the anti-thesis of a traditional xianxia breakthrough.
