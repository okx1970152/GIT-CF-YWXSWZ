## Chapter Overview
Alright, fellow travelers, we've finally made it to a proper city! After the grime and terror of the road, Yinling City presents itself as a bustling, expensive, and deeply cynical metropolis. Li Huowang's group checks into a nickel-and-diming inn, gets their first hot meal in ages, and climbs a high restaurant to take in the view. And what a view it is. The centerpiece is the breathtaking Linglong Pagoda—a tower made entirely of porcelain. But as our weary Daoist soon learns, this city's crowning jewel is a gilded pleasure house. This chapter trades jump-scares for cultural whiplash, contrasting the brutal poverty of a single beggar with the obscene wealth of a city that turns everything, even art, into a very specific transaction. It's a deep dive into the social hypocrisy of the Dao-Twisted World, showing that the cruellest monsters here sometimes wear human faces and charge for water.

## Key Plot Points
- The group finally arrives in Yinling City and immediately feels the sting of its bureaucratic, money-grubbing regulations (fines for ox dung, paying for water at the inn).
- Li Huowang decides to splurge on a good meal for everyone, a rare moment of comfort and morale-boosting leadership.
- From the top floor of a restaurant, they get a panoramic view of the city, dominated by the stunning, but ultimately profane, Linglong Pagoda.
- A local scholar proudly reveals that the "cultivation" inside the pagoda is a euphemism for high-class prostitution, where wealthy patrons can even commission tattoos on the courtesans.
- Li Huowang's attention is drawn away from the spectacle of the pagoda to a single, broken old beggar on the ground, whose face is tattooed with the same fine porcelain patterns now ruined by age.
- The chapter ends with the group enjoying their lavish meal and Bai Lingmiao pointing out floating lanterns beginning to light up the night sky.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Beauty Paper (美人纸)** and **Porcelain Prostitution**: Li Huowang's wry remark about "Beauty Paper" is a callback to a truly horrific historical practice in feudal China, where wealthy nobles would use a beautiful woman as a living, reusable piece of toilet paper. The Linglong Pagoda is the city's ultimate expression of this twisted philosophy: aestheticizing and commodifying the female body as if it were a beautiful object. The description of the courtesans—their bodies like celadon statues, their flesh feeling like jade—blurs the line between human and object. The name "Linglong" itself (玲珑) means "exquisitely carved" or "ingeniously intricate," usually referring to fine art, which adds a layer of dark irony to the pagoda's purpose. This is a brilliant use of *folk-horror* by the author, replacing supernatural dread with the horror of systemic, casual dehumanization.

**The Symbolism of Porcelain**: In Chinese culture, fine porcelain is a highly valued art form. It is beautiful, delicate, and expensive. A single crack can render it worthless. The beggar with the shattered藏 blue tattoos on his wrinkled face is a direct, tragic parallel to the pagoda. He was likely once a part of that world, a vessel for pleasure, now discarded and broken once his "glaze" was ruined. Li Huowang noticing this small detail, while everyone else stares at the spectacle, highlights his core character: he always looks at the price paid, not just the show.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in using *setting as satire*. The "horror" here is purely social and economic, and it's arguably more unnerving than a monster because it feels real. The fine for ox dung isn't just a joke; it's the first taste of how this city commodifies *everything*, even the most basic bodily functions. This sets the stage for the Linglong Pagoda, where even beauty and intimacy are just products to be bought and customized ("I heard someone got a turtle tattooed on his girl's face").

Pay close attention to Li Huowang's mood. He's not scared, he's disgusted. Tired. His reaction isn't panic but a weary, cynical detachment. He's seen enough to recognize the pattern. His glance at the broken old beggar is the real gut-punch of the chapter, a quiet moment of pathos that speaks louder than the tawdry gossip about the pagoda. It's a reminder that in this world, the most valuable objects are people, and when they break, they're thrown away. Get ready, because the lights in the sky are a beautiful sight, but in the Dao-Twisted World, beauty is always a trap.
