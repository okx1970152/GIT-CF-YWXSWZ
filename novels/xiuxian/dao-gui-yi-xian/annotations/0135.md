## Chapter Overview

Hold onto your hats (and maybe a tissue), fellow Daoists, because this chapter is a raw, quiet gut-punch after the storm. We find Li Huowang in a state of profound, broken vulnerability—sitting in the dirt, eating rocks while hallucinating a conversation with his mother from the modern world. It’s not a jump-scare horror; it’s the slow, aching horror of a mind that has been pulverized and is trying to piece itself back together with delusions of maternal care. When his group finds him, the chapter pivots from grotesque spectacle to a touching, almost domestic scene of reunion and tentative trust-building. The real meat, however, is in Li Huowang’s new resolve: he’s done being a passive victim. He’s ready to map this twisted world, understand its rules, and become an active player.

## Key Plot Points

- The chapter opens with Li Huowang in a dissociated state, chewing on a rock and having a one-sided, loving conversation with his mother, who he hallucinates is feeding him. His dog Mantou whines nearby, the only witness to his breakdown.
- Bai Lingmiao is the first to reach him, overwhelmed with pity and sorrow. She stops herself from rushing in, recognizing he is in a dangerous fugue state. The group prepares to restrain him.
- When he regains consciousness, Li Huowang is held tightly by Bai Lingmiao. He experiences a foreign, unfamiliar feeling: joy. He downplays his ordeal, saying only that he "figured some things out" and solved some problems.
- Around the campfire, Li Huowang quietly thanks the group. Lü Zhuangyuan, trying to act normal, asks with forced cheerfulness what evil spirit caused him to eat rocks.
- Gouwa accidentally blurts out a horrifying reference to Li Huowang's past habit of crawling into people's bellies. Zhao Wu and others try to cover it up, but the damage is done, revealing the troupe's fear.
- Li Huowang cuts through the pretense. He calmly tells Lü Zhuangyuan that he *has* done terrible things but wasn't in control. He offers the troupe an out: they can leave without hard feelings, or stay under his protection. Lü quickly backpedals and affirms his loyalty.
- Li Huowang declares they will leave Siqi tomorrow. His primary goal is to get his people home safely, shedding the weight of responsibility.
- Bai Lingmiao relays a message from the nun at Anci Nunnery: Li Huowang must return in person, not just send a letter. She has something to tell him face-to-face.
- Intrigued and cautious, Li Huowang agrees to the detour. He sees it as a perfect opportunity to gather intelligence and finally "map" the factions, territories, and taboos of this nightmarish world.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The "Eating Stone" Madness:** In classical Chinese ghost stories and folklore, madness is often depicted as demonic possession or being "haunted by gu (蛊)." An afflicted person might eat inedible objects like charcoal, nails, or dirt. Li Huowang eating a stone while hallucinating his mother is a visceral realization of this trope. It’s not just a random act of insanity; it’s a classic symptom of deep spiritual corruption, interpreted through the lens of his modern medical condition. The rock is both a symbol of his *literal* inability to digest reality and a perverse act of "eating" the earth itself to ground himself.

**The "Face" of Leadership:** Li Huowang's offer to let the group leave is a masterclass in *mianzi* (face/面子) in a survival context. By openly admitting his past "rotten deeds" but refusing to make excuses, he seizes the moral high ground. He doesn't beg them to stay or threaten them; he gives them a dignified "good parting" (好聚好散), which actually makes it *harder* for them to leave. In this brutal world, a leader who takes responsibility and offers a clear choice is rare. Lü Zhuangyuan's frantic backtracking isn't just fear of the supernatural; it's social and survival pragmatism—you don't abandon a powerful, honest (even if terrifying) leader.

### Mianzi (面子)

A Chinese social concept of "face," encompassing reputation, dignity, and social standing. Managing face—both one's own and others'—is crucial for navigating relationships, conflict, and leadership, especially in high-stakes settings.

## Reading Guide

**The Echo of the Mother:** The chapter's most powerful image is the hallucinated conversation. It weaponizes the one thing Li Huowang cannot escape—love. The horror isn't the monster; it's his mother's gentle voice that his own mind created to soothe him while he destroyed his mouth. This sets up a core emotional conflict: the modern world’s "cure" (maternal love) is now indistinguishable from the Dao-Twisted world's "curse" (a comforting lie). Is his mind protecting him or trapping him deeper in the delusion? The ambiguity is the point.

**Li Huowang the Cartographer:** Note the shift in his vocabulary. He's no longer just surviving; he's thinking about "factions," "territories," "taboos," and "mapping" the world. This is a new phase of his arc. He is transitioning from a frantic, reactive victim to a calculating strategist. The detour to Anci Nunnery isn't a side quest—it's his first deliberate act of intelligence-gathering. He's treating this hellscape like a game board he finally has time to study. This pragmatic curiosity marks a dangerous but necessary evolution in his character.
