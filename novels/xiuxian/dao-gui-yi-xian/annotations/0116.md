## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang walks a lonesome road, hunting for an elusive cult. But what does he find? Ominous hell-beasts scheming to tear him apart? Ancient horrors clawing their way through the dark? Nope. Just a mangy, tick-ridden, starving stray dog that wouldn’t take “Get lost” for an answer. *Obviously*, this is where his biggest threat this chapter comes from: himself. A single conversational slip to his hallucinated parents, and the man who survived Danyangzi and a Flesh Buddha is now unconsciously cuddling a mongrel in a creek. This chapter is a quiet, gut-punching reminder that Li Huowang’s worst enemy isn’t the monsters in the woods—it’s the thin shred of paper he has between himself and his own fractured mind.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang travels alone toward the mountains seeking the Ao-Jing Sect, following directions from Anci Nunnery. He reflects on the unsettling silence of solitary travel.
- A starving stray dog approaches him, drawn by the smell of food. Li Huowang is immediately suspicious and tries to drive it away using the bronze Daoist bell.
- While verbally “seeing” his parents in a hallucination, Li Huowang drops his guard. The dog steals a mantou.
- A critical red flag: Li Huowang calmly tells his hallucinated mother that “the other side” is an illusion and that he will “cooperate with the doctors.” This suggests his rational self is still fighting, but the boundary between worlds is dangerously thin.
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang snapping out of his hallucination to find himself holding the dog in the middle of a creek, the animal licking his chin. His final observation—that he “really can’t be alone”—is delivered with weary acceptance.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Lone Wanderer Trope:** In traditional xianxia, a solitary journey into the mountains is often a rite of passage—a time for quiet reflection, meditative breakthroughs, and gathering rare herbs. Li Huowang’s version is a bitter inversion of this. His solitude doesn’t bring enlightenment; it brings a complete lack of oversight, allowing his psychosis to run unchecked until he loses hours of time holding a random dog.

**The Stray Dog as Folk-Horror Foreshadowing:** In Chinese rural folklore, stray animals—especially dogs appearing at night in desolate locations—can be portents or even shape-shifting spirits sent to test or trick travelers. Li Huowang’s initial impulse to drive it away with a consecrated Daoist bell isn’t just paranoia; it’s the correct survival instinct. The tragedy is that his own brain overrides that instinct with a false sense of security.

**The Full Moon:** In Chinese cultural symbolism, the full moon is associated with reunion, family, and homesickness. Li Huowang’s psychotic break is triggered by this exact image—the perfect round moon pulling him into a delusion where his parents are visiting him. The moon is a symbol of comfort, but for him, it’s a trap door into unreality.

## Reading Guide

*Warning: this chapter doesn’t have a fight scene. It has something far worse: a man having a perfectly normal conversation with nobody.*

This is a textbook example of *Dao Gui Yi Xian’s* unique brand of psychological horror. The monster isn’t a Flesh Buddha or a Wandering Lord. The monster is the silent, creeping moment when Li Huowang’s smile turns genuine and he starts chatting with the moon as if it’s his mother. The shift from “I just want to walk in peace” to “what happened to the last three hours of my life” is delivered so smoothly you almost miss it until he’s standing in a creek holding a stray dog like he’s just come home from a long day at work.

Pay close attention to the dog. It’s genuinely just a hungry animal, but Li Huowang’s world has taught us—and him—that nothing is ever just an animal. The tension between the dog’s perfectly mundane behavior and Li Huowang’s hyper-vigilance is the entire point. And the final punchline? His admission that he needs people around to keep him grounded. Not magic. Not power. Just *witnesses* to keep the world from sliding off its axis. Tough read, but essential for anyone following Li Huowang’s slow erosion.
