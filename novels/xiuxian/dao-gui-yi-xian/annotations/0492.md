## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this chapter trades visceral horror for something almost more painful: quiet, human heartbreak. No cosmic entities, no twisted rituals, no Danyangzi. Just Gao Zhijian and Chun Xiaoman sitting across a bowl of soy-sauce fried rice. And the one thing she can’t give him back. Meanwhile, the village’s slow recovery is interrupted by the oldest alarm in the book—bandits at the gate. But the real wound isn’t the attack; it’s the conversation that happens before it.

## Key Plot Points
1. **Gao Zhijian’s identity crisis**: His memories are stitched together from contradictory patches—imperial palace lives alongside bizarre deaths and mundane survival. He suspects he might be “mad” like Li Huowang, or that his past is a fabrication, but he *chooses* to stay in Niuxin Village and be “Gao Zhijian” because this life—simple, warm, shared—feels *real* to him.
2. **Militia training**: Gao Zhijian trains a dozen villagers with a practiced stick, firm enough to sting but not injure. The soldiers stay for white-flour buns and a hundred coppers a month—a grim sign of the village’s rising prosperity and the desperation of the surrounding world.
3. **Chun Xiaoman’s rejection**: She stops studying her incantations long enough to eat the meal Gao Zhijian brings her—and then gently, firmly, tells him she has vowed never to marry. She is a *self-combed woman*, a real historical choice. She values him as a brother, but that’s all.
4. **Gao Zhijian’s silent pain**: He says nothing, but the empty bowl cracks in his grip. His heartbreak is shown, not told—a quiet, devastating character beat for a man of few words.
5. **Chun Xiaoman’s resolve**: She throws herself back into studying possessed-medium arts with obsessive discipline. She refuses to ever be powerless again.
6. **Cliffhanger**: Lü Xiucai bursts in shouting that bandits are killing people in the village. Several are already dead.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Self-combed women (zishu nü, 自梳女)** were a real historical phenomenon in late imperial and Republican-era China, particularly in the Pearl River Delta region. These were women who made a binding vow never to marry, usually by performing a ritual hair-combing ceremony. They lived independently, often in sisterhoods, and supported themselves through labor. In the Dao-Twisted World, Chun Xiaoman’s declaration is not just personal preference—it’s a social identity with real historical weight, and one that explicitly rejects the “marry the hero” trope common in xianxia.

**“Sincerity can break stone” (精诚所至金石为开)** is a classical Chinese proverb from the *Zhuangzi*, later popularized as a common idiom. It means that unwavering dedication can overcome even the hardest obstacles. Chun Xiaoman uses it here in a low-key, almost self-mocking way—she knows she’s not talented, but she’s betting everything on grit.

**Militia training with a teaching stick** (usually a light bamboo rod or switch) is an old Chinese pedagogical practice, common in martial arts and classical education alike. The trainer uses it to correct posture instantly, relying on sharp but harmless pain—a tactile feedback loop rather than verbal instruction.

**Soy-sauce fried rice** is a simple, hearty comfort food in Chinese cuisine, especially in working-class and rural contexts. It’s oily, salty, and calorically dense—perfect for manual laborers. The fact that Li Huowang’s village can afford lard, soy sauce, and sugar (in the fried egg) is a subtle sign of relative prosperity in a starving world.

## Reading Guide
If you came here for action horror, this chapter will feel slow. But it’s a *needed* slow. The novel’s most terrifying beats hit harder when you remember that these are people with *feelings*—not just trauma machines. Gao Zhijian choosing to be “Gao Zhijian” over an emperor’s past is a quietly heroic act of self-definition. And Chun Xiaoman’s rejection isn’t cruel—it’s honest, early, and born from a vow made long before she met any of them. She is carving out a self she can control, and she will not let romance undo that. The bandit attack is the classic xianxia drama hook, but the emotional punch of this chapter is the cracked bowl. Pay attention to who gets the last line, and what it says about priorities.

## Deeper Dive: The “Self-Combed Woman” and Female Agency in Chinese History
The *zishu nü* (自梳女) tradition emerged as a complex social response to patriarchal marriage pressures. In the Guangdong region, women working in the silk industry gained enough economic independence to refuse marriage altogether. The “hair-combing ceremony” was a formal, binding rite—once performed, the woman could never marry, and breaking the vow was considered a grave social sin.

In xianxia and wuxia fiction, independent female characters are common, but Chun Xiaoman’s specific identity as a *zishu nü* is a rare and grounding detail. It implies a pre-existing vow, not a reaction to trauma. Her refusal of Gao Zhijian isn’t about him—it’s about *her* prior commitment to a life she chose long before the story began. This adds a layer of dignity and autonomy that modern “strong female character” writing often misses.
