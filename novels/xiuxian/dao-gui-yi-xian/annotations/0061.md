## Chapter Overview

Picture this, fellow readers: a smoky earthen house, a pounding drum, a chanting medium, and a silent bride in a red veil whose head keeps bulging with *things* that aren't quite animal faces. Yes, it's spirit-dancing season again, and our boy Li Huowang is learning that "paying for a cheap exorcist" comes with its own set of horrifying risks. This chapter isn't about big fights or cosmic revelations—it's a masterclass in atmospheric folk horror. Li Zhi, the gluttonous, perpetually grinning spirit-dancer, performs a full ritual to expel a spirit from a possessed woman, and Li Huowang gets a front-row seat to observe the mechanics of this world's supernatural commerce. But the real kicker comes at the end: a secret whisper from the silent Second Spirit that suggests not everything is as it seems. Get ready for a chapter that's less about explosions and more about the slow, creeping dread of something *looking back at you* from a clay doll's eyes.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Zhi performs a full summoning ritual, chanting the "summoning chant" as he dances with the Er Shen around a possessed woman.
- The woman's possession escalates violently: she attacks Li Huowang with superhuman strength, biting his sword and clawing at his face.
- Li Zhi regains control by throwing the Er Shen's red veil onto the woman—the animal faces on the veil writhe and burrow into her mouth, expelling the possessing entity.
- After the ritual, the woman is left exhausted but alive; Li Zhi nonchalantly explains he "zoned out" and nearly lost control, blaming the low pay.
- Li Zhi sells the family a "Protecting Immortal"—a clay figurine wrapped in red string—and instructs them on its worship, charging a total of one hundred coppers.
- Li Huowang examines the Protecting Immortal and sees it *twitch*, a detail that unnerves him.
- The Er Shen, still veiled, whispers "Don't look…" to Li Huowang from behind, breaking her silence in a moment of apparent secrecy.
- When Li Huowang tries to press her for more information, she drops the veil and follows Li Zhi without another word.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is a goldmine of authentic Chinese folk religion. The **spirit-dance (跳大神 / tiao dashen)** is a real, historically grounded practice in Northeast China, where a medium (the Lead Spirit) uses drumming, chanting, and dancing to invite spirits or immortals to possess a helper (the Second Spirit / Er Shen) or the afflicted person. The summoning chant here, with its vivid imagery of "head touching the rafters" and "summoning Huang Tianba," mimics genuine ritual texts.

The **Er Shen (二神)** is the crucial second performer in the ritual. While the Lead Spirit chants and directs, the Er Shen acts as the physical vessel for the spirit—hence the veiled face, the unnatural stillness, and the ominous animal-head formations described under the veil. In this chapter, we learn the Er Shen has her *own* will, capable of whispering warnings independent of Li Zhi's control—a detail that carries heavy implications.

The **Protecting Immortal (保家仙 / baojia xian)** is another authentic folk concept: a household guardian spirit, often enshrined as a small clay or wooden figurine on a high shelf or beam, believed to protect the home from harm. Annual worship and incense on specific days were required to maintain its favor. Li Zhi's casual commodification of this sacred object—charging for it like any other goods—fits the novel's theme of institutional corruption and hollow piety.

Also notable: the complaint about low pay. In traditional Chinese folk practice, spirit-dancers were often poorly compensated, and the work—dangerous, exhausting, and spiritually taxing—was seen as a thankless profession. Li Zhi's grumbling isn't just character color; it's a realistic detail that grounds the supernatural in economic reality.

## Reading Guide

This chapter rewards close reading, especially for the **Er Shen**'s secret interaction with Li Huowang. Her whisper—"Don't look…"—is directed at him, not at the possessed woman or Li Zhi. It's a voluntary, independent act of communication behind her master's back. This opens up a huge question: is the Er Shen a victim trapped in this ritual? A being with her own agenda? Or is she warning Li Huowang about something specific about the Protecting Immortal's power? The fact that she *chooses* to speak at all, and then retreats back into silence when Li Zhi calls, suggests a hidden layer of the world's supernatural politics that Li Huowang has only just begun to scratch.

Also, pay attention to Li Huowang's observational mode in this chapter. He's not just surviving—he's *studying*. He notes the mechanics of the ritual, the price of the exorcism, the physical toll on the possessed woman, and the strange "payment" that the immortal takes (something intangible, possibly spiritual). This shift from reactive panic to systematic investigation marks his growth as a character in the Dao-Twisted World. The gap between Li Zhi's careless, money-grubbing exterior and the genuine power he wields is exactly the kind of dissonance this novel loves.

And that final image—the Er Shen lifting her veil just a crack, whispering a warning, then dropping it back down—is pure horror craftsmanship. It's not a jump scare; it's the quiet horror of a silent figure suddenly showing she has her own voice.
