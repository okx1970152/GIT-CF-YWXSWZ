## Chapter Overview

“Acting realistically” means real, innocent people died to sell the scene. That’s the takeaway Li Huowang gets when he meets a member of the Supervisory Heavenly Office named Hong Da—a “Creditor of Blades.” This chapter is a masterclass in how *Dao-Twisted World* horror works when it’s not a monster but a **bureaucracy**: cold, pragmatic, and utterly willing to burn civilians as set dressing to counter a Zuowandao rumor. Li Huowang watches, processes, and calibrates. He’s not appalled; he’s *informed*. And then he tries to buy himself a ticket into that same brutal system.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang realizes the beggar’s extreme method (killing innocent people to make the anti-Zuowandao rumor believable) is a deliberate tactic. No ghost was necessary—just a terrifying counter-rumor backed by real corpses.
- The beggar introduces himself as Hong Da, a “Creditor of Blades” (she dao ren). He offers Li Huowang a blade on credit; Li Huowang touches it, then refuses.
- When Hong Da tries to leave, Li Huowang blocks him and negotiates either a share of the reward or entry into the Supervisory Heavenly Office.
- Hong Da initially suggests registering with the Recorder of Portraits at the Ministry of Rites, but Li Huowang refuses to enter a government stronghold. They settle on a one-way contact: if Hong Da’s superiors agree, they will find Li Huowang.
- After leaving Pi County, Hong Da meets with a second person wearing a ceramic child’s-head doll. Their debate about whether Li Huowang is a Zuowandao plant reveals both caution and opportunity: Hong Da’s final assessment is to “use him and watch him.”

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

### The Creditor of Blades (赊刀人 / She Dao Ren)

In Chinese folklore and xianxia literature, the *she dao ren* occupies a unique, eerie niche. They are itinerant merchants who would “lend” household knives or scissors on credit, but with a twist: they would only demand payment after a specific prophecy came true (*“I will collect when rice falls below this price”*). The uncanny accuracy of these predictions turned the *she dao ren* from peddlers into omens. In this novel, Hong Da turns the archetype on its head—not a wandering prophet of prices, but a roving blade of the Supervisory Heavenly Office, whose “loaned” blades carry obligations that sound more like pacts with a celestial enforcer than a simple sale. The rust is not decay; it’s *age*, and possibly *killing intent* that outlasts the steel.

### Supervisory Heavenly Office: Agents vs. Bureaucracy

The Office doesn’t just send cultivated experts; it dispatches specialized “cut-outs” with unique folk-magic backgrounds. Hong Da’s Creditor-of-Blades identity means his supernatural power might be tied to *debt* itself: blades he loans out may become extensions of his will, and the rust may mark previous victims. The fact that his companion (the second agent) wears a ceramic child’s-head doll and uses a different skill set suggests the Office deploys a broad array of half-folk, half-monstrous operators, not a standardized army of immortals.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is all about *calibration*. Watch how Li Huowang doesn’t get emotional—he gets *practical*. He’s not disgusted by Hong Da’s methods; he files them as data. His refusal of the blade is a masterclass in instinct: he feels the threat of the *debt* more than the promise of a weapon. And his last-minute negotiation for entry into the Heavenly Office shows he’s already thinking in *institutional* terms—he wants a network, not just a weapon. The real delight is the paranoid conversation between Hong Da and his partner: every line Hong Da gives, the partner parrots back with a logical recursion (“What if they guessed we’d think that?”). This is how the Zuowandao have infected *everyone*—even the hunters can’t trust their own hunches, and they’ve learned to be suspicious of their own suspicion.
