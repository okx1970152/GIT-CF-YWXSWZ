## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, fellow travelers, because after a few chapters of (relative) calm, the tension in Li Huowang's rag-tag group finally boils over. And the source? Not a Flesh Buddha, not a Zuowandao trickster, but the simmering resentment of a frustrated teenager. Lyu Xiucai, the scholar who desperately wants to escape his "mortal" fate, finally makes a move, and it's exactly as stupid and dangerous as you'd expect. This chapter is a masterclass in quiet horror—the kind that doesn't come from a monster, but from the ugly, all-too-human emotions of envy, ambition, and the desperate need to be special. Li Huowang's response is brutal, pragmatic, and heartbreakingly honest. Get ready for a character study wrapped in noodles and bruised egos.

## Key Plot Points
The chapter opens with a group quietly recovering. Li Huowang gifts Chun Xiaoman the sword of the late Changming, a symbolic passing of the torch. Yang Xiaohai's cooking skills are on full display, providing a moment of domestic warmth. But this peace is fragile. Lü Zhuangyuan's bruised face reveals his failed attempt to steal techniques from another troupe, a petty act that foreshadows the main conflict. The real storm comes that night. Lyu Xiucai, driven by envy and a twisted sense of entitlement, steals Li Huowang's Purple-Tassel Sword, overwhelmed by its killing intent but deluded into believing he has gained a "supernatural ability." He confronts his father and Li Huowang, only to be swiftly disarmed and humiliated. The aftermath is the most disturbing part: Lyu Xiucai is hung from a tree and brutally whipped by his father, screaming death threats at his own family. The cycle of resentment and violence is laid bare.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The "Scholar" (秀才, Xiucai)**: Lyu Xiucai's name and title are crucial. In imperial China, a *xiucai* was the lowest level of successful candidate in the civil service examinations. It was a title that granted some social status but often came with the bitter reality of being an educated man with no real power or wealth. Lyu Xiucai embodies this frustrated class—too smart and proud to be a common laborer, but not successful enough to escape poverty. His desire for Li Huowang's "supernatural power" is a twisted version of the traditional scholar's ambition for advancement through merit. He doesn't want to *study* to become powerful; he wants the shortcut, the cheat code.
**Knife-Cut Noodles (刀削面, Dao Xiao Mian)**: A classic dish from Shanxi province, known for its method of preparation: a chef holds a block of dough and uses a sharp knife to slice it directly into a pot of boiling water. Yang Xiaohai's skill with this technique is a neat parallel to his own lack of swordsmanship—he can't use a sword in battle, but he can slice noodles with expert precision. It's a small, human detail against a backdrop of horror.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a crucial turning point for the group's internal dynamics. Li Huowang's refusal to teach his "ability" isn't about selfishness; it's a grim, hard-won piece of wisdom delivered in the most painful way possible: "If I had a choice, I'd rather be a normal person like you." This is a key moment for his character, showing the immense burden he carries. The real horror here isn't supernatural. It's the ugliness of Lyu Xiucai's ambition, the desperation of a father trying to beat sense into his son, and the chilling prophecy of violence that ends the chapter. "*Otherwise, I'll kill every one of you!!*" — this isn't just a bratty tantrum. In the Dao-Twisted World, where resentment and desire can literally reshape reality, words like this have weight. Keep an eye on Scholar Lyu. Ambition is a poison that often finds its own cure in this world.

## The Weight of a Title
The term "*Xiucai*" isn't just a name; it's a social straitjacket. For a deep dive into how the imperial examination system created a whole class of brilliant, bitter, and often dangerous men, check out the history of the *Keju* (科举) system. In the Dao-Twisted World, this historical resentment is supercharged. Lyu Xiucai isn't just poor; he's a *symbol* of thwarted ambition in a world where that ambition can literally mutate you into a monster. His cry of "I don't want to be a commoner!" isn't just teenage angst. It's the cry of a whole class of men who were promised glory through study but were handed only bitterness.
