## Chapter Overview

Ready yourself, fellow cultivators, because this chapter is less about sword fights and more about the quiet, devastating battles of the heart. The dust has settled following Li Huowang’s departure, and we zoom in on the fallout in Niuxin Village. The central focus? Bai Lingmiao, who is shown crumbling in a quiet, dignified way. This is a "slice of empty life," an emotional deep-dive that feels almost painfully human. Meanwhile, on the road, Li Huowang has a mind-breaking domestic epiphany: his inner parasite just called him "Dad." Get ready for some major psychological whiplash between warm familial angst and cosmic body horror.

## Key Plot Points

Li Huowang is trying to process the Black Tai Sui calling him "Dad." He flips from panicked rejection to a moment of chillingly pragmatic acceptance, realizing that whether this relationship is good or bad is still undecided. Back in Niuxin Village, Yang Xiaohai is the backbone of the morning, getting everything ready. Bai Lingmiao, weighed down by grief, tries to maintain her role as a leader by organizing a Mid-Autumn Festival dinner. The attempt is forced, creating a tense and awkward atmosphere. Lü Zhuangyuan’s subplot re-emerges as he tries to leave but is denied, revealing his discomfort as an outsider in this fragile community. The chapter’s emotional climax sees Bai Lingmiao, after a hollow toast, excuse herself to change the offerings in her family's ancestral hall—a powerful and lonely act that underscores her position as the "last bloodline."

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The "Moon Festival" (月夕, Yuèxī) is a poetic name for the Mid-Autumn Festival (中秋节). It’s a time for family reunions, moon-gazing, and eating mooncakes (月饼). The festival’s core symbolism is *fullness and reunion*—of the family unit and of the moon itself. For Bai Lingmiao, who has lost her entire clan, this holiday is a cruel mirror. The ancestral hall scene is heavy with meaning. It’s a traditional Chinese practice to present offerings of fresh food (like mooncakes) to the ancestors' spirit tablets. The sheer scale of the "black wall" of tablets emphasizes the totality of her loss. Also, note Yang Xiaohai’s mention of an old "Baldy Liu" who used to run his temple—a small world-building detail suggesting the scars of his past. The word "dessert palace" (伙房) is a simple but rustic term for a kitchen in a village or temple setting.

### Spirit tablet (灵牌)

A wooden plaque carved with the name of a deceased person, representing their spirit. It is placed in an ancestral hall for offerings. The "black wall" of tablets emphasizes the scope of the Bai family's massacre.

## Reading Guide

If you love chapters that prioritize character emotion and mood over action, this one is a gem. The horror here is purely psychological—watching a character try to perform normalcy when their world is broken. Pay close attention to Bai Lingmiao’s serene surface. The dialogue is painfully realistic; the forced cheer of the gathering doubles as a pressure cooker of unspoken grief. The core question the chapter poses is beautifully, brutally tragic: how do you celebrate a family reunion when your entire family is dead? The final image of her standing alone before the tablets is a gut-punch. This is a masterclass in *atmospheric hurt/comfort*—if *comfort* is even the right word here.

## Decoding the 'Fake Dad' Paradox

Li Huowang's interaction with the Black Tai Sui is a classic example of "cosmic horror wearing a domestic mask." The horror isn't in a monster attack, but in the dissonance of the monster learning to be a child. The Black Tai Sui is a parasitic entity of suffering and bodily corruption. For it to ask "Why not?" when rejected is profoundly unsettling because it’s mimicking human logic. Li Huowang’s ultimate acceptance ("Yeah, why not?") is a dangerous psychological turn—he's no longer just fighting an invader; he's potentially negotiating with, or even raising, something inside him. This blurs the line between host and parent in a way the novel hasn't explored before.
