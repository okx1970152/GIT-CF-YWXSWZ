## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang tries to debunk the mass hysteria gripping Pi County—only to have reality twist under his feet once more. Just when he thinks he’s killed a lie with logic, a genuine “ghost” emerges from the crowd, forcing him to confront something far more unsettling: a horror that weaponizes the town’s own fear against them. Get ready for a chapter where the monster isn’t the ghost—it’s the collective delusion, and the brilliant, sickly light it casts on human nature.

## Key Plot Points

Li Huowang gathers the entire town of Pi County to tell them the “shrinking ghost” is a lie. A woman interrupts with a baby she claims has been cursed—but Li Huowang calmly points out that the child is, in fact, a girl. Her desperate denial exposes the bitter social pressure women face. Just as the crowd begins to hesitate, a genuine supernatural attacker appears: a beggar who rips off a man’s genitals and floats like a ghost. He is completely immune to Li Huowang’s sword, fighting the protagonist to a standstill. After a prolonged battle, Li Huowang pins a talisman on the creature and kills it—then doubles down on the lie, telling the townsfolk the ghost was real and he has slain it. The chapter ends with an author’s note: this rumor is historically real and frighteningly recent.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The “Suoyang” (Penile Retraction) Panic** — Believe it or not, this is a documented folk panic phenomenon in parts of rural China and Southeast Asia. Known medically as “Koro” or “genital retraction syndrome,” it is a culture-bound mass delusion where men (and sometimes women) believe their genitals are shrinking into their bodies, often blamed on supernatural causes. Entire villages have been gripped by it. The novel’s decision to frame it as a social-horror trick is frighteningly grounded.
**The Copper Coin Mask** — More than just a disguise, a mask made of threaded old coins carries exorcistic symbolism in Chinese folk Daoism. Coins themselves are considered “yang” currency; a mask made of them can both conceal identity and ward off yin spirits. Li Huowang wears it as both a tactical tool and a psychological weapon.
**The Daoist Talisman (符箓)** — In xianxia and folk Daoist tradition, a talisman is a written charm empowered by the caster’s cultivation. Slapping it on a spirit’s forehead is a classic method to seal or immobilize it. The fact that Li Huowang’s sword cannot cut the ghost but a talisman can stop it reinforces the rule: some supernatural threats require ritual, not steel.

### Koro Syndrome

### Suoyang (缩阳)

The folk Chinese term for the perceived shrinking of a man’s penis; often attributed to supernatural causes in rural legends.

## Reading Guide

Here’s the gut-punch of this chapter: Li Huowang is *right*, but right doesn’t save anyone. He debunks the lie perfectly, and yet a real monster still shows up to make the lie true by force. The chapter is a masterclass in how fear rewrites reality. Pay close attention to the woman with the baby—that’s an expertly crafted social micro-horror: she would rather believe a ghost stole her son’s organ than admit she had a daughter. And Li Huowang’s final move? He kills the ghost and then *lies to the town* that the ghost was the one they feared. It’s a brilliant, morally gray end: he extinguishes the immediate terror by feeding the very delusion he was trying to dismantle. That’s the Dao-Twisted World for you—sometimes you win by losing.

## Deeper Dive: The Koro Epidemic of 1984-85

For the culturally curious—and if this chapter made your skin crawl—you should know that a massive Koro panic swept across China’s Hainan Island in 1984 and 1985. Thousands of men believed they were being poisoned or cursed into genital retraction. People grabbed strangers on the street, demanding antidotes; families tied ropes around their men’s waists to prevent the organs from “disappearing.” The government treated it as a mass sociogenic illness. The author’s final “fun fact” isn’t a joke—it’s a direct nod to this real, documented mass hysteria. The line between fiction and folk horror is thinner than you think.
