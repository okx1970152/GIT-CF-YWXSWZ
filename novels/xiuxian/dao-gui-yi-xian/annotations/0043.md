## Chapter Overview

In this bone-chilling chapter, Xin Hui, the abbot of Zhengde Temple, drops a bomb that shatters Li Huowang’s last hope for a clean resolution: Danyangzi hasn’t died from the poison—he has "become a Buddha." This isn't enlightenment. It's a catastrophic ontological shift. The Dao-Twisted World’s logic, where belief can violently reshape reality, has taken something Li Huowang *made up* and turned it into a real, roaming, sentient threat. Forced to navigate the riddles of a cryptic monk and the gilded normalcy of a bustling temple that hides a monstrosity behind its piety, Li Huowang must decide if he can afford to trust anyone. The chapter ends with him sending his crew to gather local intel on the temple, revealing his new, cautious strategy: never take holy ground at face value.

## Key Plot Points

- **The Abbot’s Revelation:** Xin Hui delivers the chapter’s core
horror: Danyangzi didn’t die from the bogus alchemy. He "became a
Buddha"—formless, mutable by perception, like the Wandering Lord. He is now a living lie given flesh.

- **The Riddle of Form:** Li Huowang struggles to parse the abbot's
cryptic, Mahayana-tinged answers. The abbot clarifies that Danyangzi
is now defined by *Li Huowang’s* belief in him, not his past
identity, because their karmic chains are now the anchor.

- **The Hidden Eye:** After Li Huowang leaves and the abbot resumes
chanting, a grotesque vertical eye bursts out from the back of his
head beneath the Five-Buddha Crown, spying on Li Huowang as he
walks away. The temple is not "safe."

- **A Moment of Doubt:** Standing in the temple square, Li Huowang
voices his internal conflict: "Should I trust him?" The very
question is the novel's heartbeat—faith in the supernatural world
is a gamble where the house always cheats.

- **The Old Beggar’s Fate:** The old beggar has been accepted by the
temple as a handyman and christened himself "Monk." His simple joy contrasts with the chapter's creeping dread, showing the trap of mistaken safety in this world.

- **Strategic Intelligence Gathering:** Breaking from his earlier
desperation, Li Huowang runs a proper field operation: he sends his
team out to canvass the locals. The intel comes back perfect—too
perfect: the temple is devout, the monks are strict, even the
Emperor visits. A perfect reputation that, in this story, is the
biggest red flag of all.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

"Becoming a Buddha" (成佛) in standard Buddhism means achieving
perfect enlightenment and liberation from samsara. In the
Dao-Twisted World, the term has been grotesquely twisted: it
describes a victim of *xiujiǎ wéi zhēn*—the principle that a bold
enough lie, if nurtured by belief and karma, becomes objectively
real. Danyangzi didn't become an awakened being; he became a
conceptual tumor held together by Li Huowang's own terrified
thoughts.

The abbot's line, "If I desire it, form is as I desire it," is a
corruption of classic Buddhist logic on impermanence and
non-attachment. Here, it's weaponized to describe a monster that
flattens the distance between belief and reality, turning an
argument against the self into a license for cosmic madness.

The **Five-Buddha Crown (五佛帽)** is a ceremonial hat in Chinese
Buddhism, often adorned with five small Buddha effigies. Here, it
serves as a façade—literally hiding a monstrous vertical eye
beneath it, making it a perfect symbol of the temple's rotten core
beneath pious garb. This tracks perfectly with the genre's pattern
of holy sites being nodes where corruption festers the deepest.

### Becoming a Buddha (成佛)

In the Dao-Twisted World, this is a corrupted state where a person becomes a formless entity whose shape and reality can be altered by the beliefs of others, often a consequence of the principle 'Cultivate the False into Reality.'

### Cultivate the False into Reality (修假为真)

The core cosmic horror principle of the novel, where a powerful enough lie or belief can physically manifest and alter reality.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is one of those moments where you, dear reader,
should feel the floor drop out from under you. The central
villain, Danyangzi—whom Li Huowang thought he'd conned to death
with a fake recipe—isn't dead. He's become a *concept*. A
Karma-born thought-monster. And Li Huowang is holding the leash.

Pay close attention to the abbot's core argument here. The
principle being laid out is not just about Danyangzi's
transformation, but the fundamental engine of the entire
Dao-Twisted World. If you are still expecting "clear rules" and
"power systems," this chapter is your final warning: the game is
run on belief, trauma, and terror. The moment the abbot's
vertical eye pops out? That was the chapter telling you that even
the "good" NPCs are horrifying. Trust nobody. *Especially* not the
nice ones giving you a bed.

The shift in tone between the two worlds is crucial here. The
sterile, clinical "reality" gave Li Huowang a framework for
doubt. Here, in a world where a man can *belong* the existence of a
monster into reality, trust is a liability. The chapter's ending
with the crew's glowing report is masterful foreshadowing, and a
trap of pure narrative irony—the intel is flawless, which is
exactly why it should terrify you.
