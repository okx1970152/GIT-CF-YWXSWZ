## Chapter Overview

If you’ve been holding your breath through the last few chapters, now’s the time to exhale—or, more accurately, to watch everything collide in a spectacular, bloody finale. Chapter 65 delivers the epic conclusion of the Li Zhi encounter, and it does *not* let up. This is not a chapter of clever schemes or delicate negotiations; this is a raw, visceral fight for survival where Li Huowang is backed into a corner and forced to fight on two fronts. The stakes are simple: if the drum keeps beating, the spirit immortals keep coming, and if the spirit immortals keep coming, Li Huowang is dogmeat. What makes this chapter truly sing is the way it weaponizes the novel's unique mechanics. The Wandering Lords, previously a nuisance or a tactical tool, become a full-on army. The Heavenly Scripture, that mysterious and deadly book, gets a practical combat use—it’s a shield. And the boundary between realities gets tested once again, as Li Zhi tries to end the fight not with a blade, but with a hallucination. This is Li Huowang at his most desperate and most determined, and the chapter ends with a single, brutal, conclusive stroke.

## Key Plot Points

The chapter opens with Li Huowang under immediate assault. The Second Spirit, having taken a hit from a Wandering Lord, proves unexpectedly solid and tears a chunk out of Li Huowang’s shoulder. He has to split his focus: he hands off the poison and knockout powder to Gouwa and Chun Xiaoman to deal with the Second Spirit, while he personally takes on Li Zhi.

The core of the fight is a battle of sounds—the drum vs. the bell. Li Zhi’s drumbeat summons and empowers the spirit immortals, who are invisible to the naked eye but block the Wandering Lords from reaching him. Li Huowang’s bell, meanwhile, empowers the Wandering Lords and allows him to command them. The clashing rhythms are a literal tug-of-war over reality itself.

Li Huowang makes a critical discovery: by shaking his head to use the Wandering Lords’ multiplication ability, he can overwhelm Li Zhi with sheer numbers. But the cost is severe nausea, creating a race against time. As he gets closer, he also gains the ability to see the spirit immortals—translucent shapes that shift constantly, requiring the drum’s melody to interact with the physical world.

Li Zhi counters with a desperate gambit. Tearing his own mouth, he shifts to a keening, woman-like wail that summons a powerful hallucination. For a moment, Li Huowang is back in the psychiatric hospital, seeing Old Man Liu being discharged. It is a dangerously effective trap—but Li Huowang’s grip on reality is too strong. He sees through it instantly, charges the hallucination, and lands a punch that shatters the illusion.

The finale comes when Li Huowang, having closed the distance, stabs his sword through Li Zhi’s hand and into his abdomen. The drum stops. The spirit immortals fade. The Second Spirit collapses. Li Zhi, defeated, mutters an apology as he slumps to the ground. The chapter ends in silence.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Wandering Lords (游老爷):** These entities were introduced as wandering spirits in earlier chapters. Their key feature here is their ability to multiply when Li Huowang shakes his head, a mechanic that implies they are tied to his perception or will. They are not simply summoned—they are *directed*, and their mass deployment comes at a tangible physical cost to the user (extreme nausea).

**Spirit Immortals (仙家):** In this context, the term refers to the spirits under Li Zhi’s command, distinct from the Wandering Lords. They are described as entities that exist in a permanently intangible, invisible state and require a specific "melody"—Li Zhi’s drum—to interact with the physical world. This mechanic underscores the novel’s theme of sound, ritual, and rhythm as the scaffolding of supernatural power. They shift forms constantly, evoking the chaotic, non-fixed nature of folk spirit beliefs.

**Second Spirit (二神/Er Shen):** The assistant in a spirit-dance ritual. Earlier chapters established that the Er Shen possesses her own will. Her collapse at the end of the chapter, following Li Zhi’s defeat, suggests she was tethered to his existence. The fact that she could physically resist a Wandering Lord, when Wandering Lords are usually intangible to normal flesh, hints at her special nature.

**Illusion vs. Reality:** Li Zhi’s hallucination is not a random trick; it is a targeted assault using Li Huowang’s own memories and fears. It tries to trap him in a comforting, familiar, *rational* setting. Li Huowang’s ability to break it is not just stubbornness—it is a direct testament to his honed paranoia and his refusal to accept any reality that feels too easy.

## Reading Guide

This is a fight chapter, and it’s a *good* one. Pay close attention to the sensory rhythm—the back-and-forth between the drum and the bell isn’t just flavor text; it’s the actual mechanical heart of the conflict. Each time the balance shifts, the battlefield changes. The multiplication of the Wandering Lords is a spectacular visual, but it’s also a perfect example of the novel’s core rule: every power has a price. Li Huowang isn’t winning without paying for it in pain.

Also, note how Li Huowang fails to avoid the hallucination entirely. He didn’t see through it because it was weak; he saw through it because he is fundamentally broken in the *right* way. His inability to trust in a peaceful reality is his greatest weapon here. That’s a grim, powerful character beat.

And finally, that ending. “Heh, Li Zhenren… my apologies…” Li Zhi’s last words are not a curse or a threat. They’re almost respectful. In the Dao-Twisted World, even enemies can acknowledge a worthy opponent. Or maybe it’s just another lie. Either way, the silence after that final sword thrust is a well-earned moment of peace.
