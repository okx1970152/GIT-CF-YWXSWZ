## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this chapter is a one-two punch to the gut that leaves Li Huowang—and us—questioning everything. Just when you thought you had a handle on the rules, the story yanks the rug out and swaps your reality for a funhouse mirror. We open with a full-blown existential crisis as the monk drops the most devastating lore bomb yet: Li Huowang isn't a transmigrator at all. He's one of the Three Corpses, a parasitic demon of suspicion named Peng Zhi. The chapter then pulls the classic *Dao Guai Yi Xian* double-world trick, shuttling Li Huowang between a desperate firefight on the Gobi and a heartbreakingly warm birthday party in the hospital—except *both* feel like traps. Is the monk a friend or a hallucination? Is Yang Na's love real or a memory belonging to someone else? This is identity horror at its finest, served with a side of brutal action and a cliffhanger that will leave you yelling at your screen.

## Key Plot Points
- **The Monk's Revelation**: The old monk drops the mother of all bombshells—Li Huowang is not a transmigrator. He is Peng Zhi, one of the Three Corpses (彭质), a parasitic demon of doubt and suspicion that was *born* fully formed with no childhood memories. The monk claims Li Huowang's memories of his past life are borrowed from the real Li Huowang's body.
- **The Crushing Detail Check**: Li Huowang's own mind betrays him. He realizes he *can't* remember his childhood. There's a blank void before Qingfeng Temple. The monk's words align with the half-truths and lies that have surrounded him since the beginning.
- **The Gobi Siege**: The black dots in the distance become a full army—Nuo opera performers with terrifying wooden masks lead a horde of masked bandits. The group is circled and fighting for their lives.
- **The Birthday Trap**: The world shatters, and Li Huowang wakes up strapped to a hospital bed. Yang Na and his mother are throwing him a birthday party, complete with a paper crown, a cake, and a gentle song. The timing is *too perfect*, and the monk's voice keeps whispering in his ear to "wake up."
- **Violent Disorientation**: Li Huowang realizes he can't trust his senses. He sees *two* identical monks. An arrow pierces his shoulder, and he feels nothing. His only move is to scream at both of them to stay back, refusing to trust anyone.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Three Corpses (三尸 / San Shi)** are a core concept in Daoist internal alchemy. They are three malevolent, parasitic demons residing in the human body—one in the head (彭质 / Peng Zhi—suspicion), one in the chest (彭矫 / Peng Jiao—greed), and one in the belly (彭居 / Peng Ju—lust). The goal of the "Cutting the Corpses" (斩三尸) practice is to kill these parasites, thereby purifying the spirit and achieving immortality. In this novel, this ancient concept is horrifically literalized: the Corpses are sentient, independent beings, and "cutting" them means their complete annihilation for the host's benefit. Li Huowang being "Peng Zhi" recontextualizes his constant paranoia and questioning—it's not an illness; it's his *nature*.

**"Being a Transmigrator" (穿越)** is a staple trope in Chinese web novels, where a modern person's soul enters the body of someone in a fantasy world, bringing foreknowledge and a "cheat." This chapter's ultimate horror is that it directly attacks this foundational comfort. If Li Huowang is not a transmigrator, then his entire identity—including the "escape" of knowing he's in a story—is a lie.

**Nuo Opera (傩戏)** continues to serve as the novel's signature folk-horror aesthetic. The "masks with fangs that writhe like anemones" is a brilliant, visceral description that turns a traditional exorcism art into a living, encroaching threat. The mask isn't just a prop; it's a living mouth.

## Reading Guide
*Dao Guai Yi Xian* has been playing with the "is this real?" game since chapter one, but Chapter 157 cranks the existential dread to eleven. The real gut-punch here isn't the Gobi battle or the revelation itself—it's the birthday scene. The warmth of the cake, the song, the smiles—and Li Huowang's chilling request for "a cloth to stuff in his mouth so he won't scare them." That single moment says more about his character than any fight scene could. He's not a monster lashing out; he's a man trying to *protect* the people he loves from the horror inside him, even as that horror tells him those people might not even be *his* to love. Pay close attention to the "two monks" split at the end. The tagline "they can't even see him" is a nasty trick—is the second monk real, or is Li Huowang's own Corpse-nature finally manifesting a visible hallucination? The answer will define the next arc.
