## Chapter Overview

Ah, look at that. We finally get to spend some quality time with the ladies, and it’s… not a spa day. Bai Lingmiao is done playing. She’s tired, she’s patient, but she’s also watching Li Huowang with the clinical suspicion of a woman who has been burned by “I’m fine” one too many times. Lü Xiucai provides some darkly comedic contrast—a coward trying on villainy like a cheap coat—before Bai Lingmiao retreats to her secret White Lotus cave to deal with her own growing power and the unsettling mystery of that six-donkey twin lotus mural. And just when you think the chapter is about to wrap up with a cozy book-burning session… something wrong is already staring right at them from the walls. This is the calm, pragmatic, and quietly ominous breathing space before the storm.

## Key Plot Points

- Lü Xiucai rages outside the door, brandishing a blade he has no intention of using. Bai Lingmiao dismisses him as too weak to be either good or evil, only a “piece of trash.”
- Gouwa defuses the situation with a bowl of food, revealing that there’s a feast tonight because “Brother Li” (Li Huowang) has returned.
- Bai Lingmiao and Chun Xiaoman discuss Li Huowang’s true mental state. Bai Lingmiao is skeptical of his reassurances and notices he dodged questions about his hallucinations.
- Chun Xiaoman asks if Bai Lingmiao will tell Li Huowang about the White Lotus divine skills she’s been learning. Bai Lingmiao refuses, seeing his power as already beyond her—and seeing him as a madman whose power has only grown more dangerous.
- Bai Lingmiao enters the secret chamber and contemplates the twin lotus mural, which has been appearing ever since the strange chanting began. She has no explanation for it.
- She searches for clues about the chanting’s origin among the locals and even her spirit-patrons, but finds nothing—only that her immortals seem *afraid* of it.
- She burns the stash of human-sacrifice rituals from the White Lotus materials, calling them “disgusting.”
- In the last moment, the narrative reveals that a strange, inverted form has begun to flicker inside the twin lotus mural—unnoticed by either woman.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

Get ready for a cultural deep dive. First up: that “married chicken follows the wind, married dog follows its master” line that Bai Lingmiao drops. This is a direct pull from the Chinese folk saying **嫁鸡随鸡，嫁狗随狗 (jià jī suí jī, jià gǒu suí gǒu)**. In traditional Chinese society, this reflected the idea that a woman’s fate was tied to her husband’s, no matter what. Bai Lingmiao uses it here to express a kind of resigned loyalty—but she twists it into something more active: *since he didn’t abandon me when I was the burden, I won’t abandon him now*. It’s a declaration of commitment, not subservience.

Then there’s the **twin lotus (并蒂莲, bìngdì lián)**. This is a classic symbol in Chinese art and literature, representing a pair of lotuses growing from a single stem. It’s deeply associated with *love, marriage, and inseparable union*. You see it in poetry, embroidery, and wedding decorations. In a typical romance novel, it’s a cute symbol of eternal love. Here? It’s a **creepy, unexplained tattoo on a wall** that appeared after haunted chanting started, and now it’s flickering with *something physically wrong inside it*. Talk about subverting expectations.

Also, for the Xianxia crowd: **Martial Possession (Wu Ji, 武乩)** is a specific type of spirit mediumship. Unlike the more passive “divination” style of spirit possession (Wen Ji), Martial Possession causes the medium to manifest superhuman combat abilities—often strength, speed, and skill they don’t naturally possess. Chun Xiaoman’s excitement about this technique makes sense; it’s a serious combat power-up. But Bai Lingmiao’s dismissal of it (“in his eyes it means nothing”) really sells how terrifyingly strong Li Huowang has become.

### Native Land of True Emptiness (真空家乡, Zhēnkōng Jiāxiāng)

The ultimate salvation-realm in White Lotus theology, where believers return to the Unborn Venerable Mother, free from worldly suffering and reincarnation.

## Reading Guide

This is a **character development / atmospheric horror chapter**. If you came here expecting Li Huowang to fight a giant pocket-watch monster, turn back. This is the aftermath of all that violence, and the real battle is happening in Bai Lingmiao’s head. She’s gone from the shy, crying girl who couldn’t say a word to a sharp-tongued pragmatist who openly judges a man’s character and talks about “married chickens.” That’s growth, folks.

The central tension here isn’t monsters—it’s the question of *what Li Huowang is hiding*. Bai Lingmiao knows he’s deflecting, and she’s not buying his “I’m fine” routine. This sets up a fantastic psychological undercurrent for the next arc: is their trust going to break before the supernatural threats even arrive?

And the **book-burning scene** is a wonderful thematic beat. Bai Lingmiao is trying to close the door on the worst of the White Lotus legacy. She wants to be something cleaner. But the novel reminds us (with that final, chilling image) that the past doesn’t just burn away so easily. The “inside-out thing” in the lotus is a great horror punchline—it says *we’re not done with you yet*. Absorb the character dynamics here, because they’re going to matter when things explode.
