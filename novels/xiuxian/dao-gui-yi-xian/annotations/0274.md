## Chapter Overview
In this chapter, the horror is no longer a monster you can fight—it’s a *rule*. Li Huowang and Gao Zhijian find themselves trapped inside an inn where a grotesque, invisible phenomenon is slowly stealing men’s manhoods. The waiter, paralyzed with fear, spills the secret and then, driven by the terror of being trapped in an eternal cycle of suffering, hangs himself in the kitchen. Li Huowang must now face a threat he cannot see, cannot stab, and cannot even prove exists. It’s a slow-burn psychological siege, and the only way out is to stop hunting ghosts and start hunting *knowledge*.

## Key Plot Points
- Panicked by the invisible threat, the waiter buckles under pressure and blurts out the existence of the “shrink-stealing ghost,” immediately begging for mercy from the unseen entity.
- Gao Zhijian instinctively blocks Li Huowang’s sword as the waiter bolts for the door, creating a moment of tense distrust between the two before Gao proves his identity.
- The waiter hangs himself in the kitchen; his corpse, with a grotesque, protruding tongue and a purple face, becomes a silent witness to the horror. His *choice* reveals that fear itself was the true killer.
- Li Huowang observes that the waiter’s genitals are indeed smaller, confirming the phenomenon is real but gradual, leaving him a sliver of time for a countermeasure.
- Instead of panicking, Li Huowang makes a tactical decision: since the curse spreads through speech, only he and Gao Zhijian are targets for now. He instructs Gao to hold the inn and protect the others.
- Abandoning the hunt for an invisible monster, Li Huowang decides to shift his strategy: he will go directly to the source of the town’s record—the County Magistrate—to gather intel and organize a collective defense.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter is a masterclass in “folk-horror logic.” The core of the horror is built around the Chinese folk belief in the *shrink-stealing ghost* (缩阳鬼), a malicious entity said to cause a man’s penis to retract into his body, often as a form of punishment or a precursor to death. In traditional Chinese medicine and folk religion, the penis (often called the “life-root” or 命根子, *ming gen zi*) is seen as a vital source of yang energy and a direct link to one’s vitality and lineage. The terror, therefore, is not just physical mutilation—it’s a threat to one’s *humanity* and *place in the ancestral line*.

The waiter’s final decision to hang himself is also steeped in this logic. In Chinese folk belief, a person who dies with their body “incomplete” (e.g., missing a limb or having a severed life-root) cannot properly reincarnate, becoming a hungry ghost. By taking his own life *before* the ghost steals his manhood, the waiter makes a desperate, tragic gamble: “If I die now, my soul is still whole. I can still go to the underworld and wait for my turn to be reborn. I won’t be trapped here forever.” It’s a chilling demonstration of how these supernatural rules shape human behavior in the most extreme ways.

The "deeds, not hearts" (论迹不论心) logic Li Huowang uses to question Gao Zhijian is a classic philosophical principle in Chinese martial and moral discourse. It asserts that a person's true nature is revealed by their actions, not their private thoughts. It’s a practical, survival-oriented tool for this world, and also a dark mirror: if your actions are monstrous, it doesn’t matter how kind your heart is.

## Reading Guide
Alright, new Daoist cultivators, take a deep breath. This chapter is *nasty*. It’s not the flesh-stitching horror of the Flesh Buddha or the visceral gore of the *Thousand Greats Record*. This is a *slow*, *psychological* horror. It’s the feeling of a nightmare you can’t wake up from because you can’t even *see* the monster. Li Huowang’s reaction here is perfect—he doesn't scream, he doesn't freeze. He immediately tests his invisibility, checks his own body, and then goes straight into detective mode. He treats this like a puzzle, and it’s that cold, methodical brain that keeps him alive.

Pay attention to the “rules” being established. The curse spreads through speech. It only seems to affect the man who *speaks the truth*. This means Li Huowang can’t just brute-force this with a sword. He needs a different kind of weapon. His decision to go to the County Magistrate is brilliant—it’s a rare moment where he chooses the *system* as his tool, using the town's own government as a source of information and legitimacy. The horror isn't in the monster; it's in the town's collective, silent terror. Get ready for a different kind of war.
