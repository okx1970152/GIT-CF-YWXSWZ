## Chapter Overview

Two worlds, two cages. In the modern reality, Li Huowang’s desperate gambit to prove his sanity backfires spectacularly—Wu Cheng baits him with a phone that turns into an electrified trap, and he’s subdued before he can call Yi Donglai. Meanwhile, in the Dao-Twisted World, Lü Zhuangyuan has traded his grueling life on the road for a luxurious courtyard, a private stage, and a family that’s starting to drive him up the wall. The contrast is brutal: one man fights a losing war against his own treatment, while another discovers that getting everything you wanted can taste just as hollow as having nothing.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang, still handcuffed, takes on six orderlies with brutal efficiency—headbutts, finger-breaking, and a chokehold that nearly tears a man’s throat out.
- Holding an orderly hostage, he demands to see Wu Cheng or call Yi Donglai, knowing he needs an outside witness to clear his name.
- Wu Cheng pretends to comply, handing over a phone—but the device has been rigged to deliver a massive electric shock the moment it’s touched.
- Paralyzed by the current, Li Huowang is netted, pinned, and injected with three syringes of sedatives, his last sight being Wu Cheng’s faint smirk.
- The scene cuts to a peaceful domestic vignette: Lü Zhuangyuan, now wealthy and retired, spends his mornings nitpicking opera performances and his afternoons scolding his daughter-in-law for hiring servants and buying pastries.
- A small family quarrel reveals the tension beneath the new wealth—Luo Juan feels entitled to comfort after years of hardship, while Lü Zhuangyuan can’t loosen his grip on frugality.
- Lü Juren quietly explains his father’s behavior: after a lifetime of struggle, the old man doesn’t know how to simply *stop* and enjoy the peace.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is a masterclass in contrast. The modern-world sequence follows the novel's grim logic of "the system is never on your side"—even a seemingly genuine attempt to prove sanity is weaponized against the protagonist. The electrified phone trap is a chilling inversion of the "clever escape using technology" trope, grounding the horror in mundane, bureaucratic betrayal.

The opera scene, meanwhile, offers a rare glimpse of *civilian life* in the Dao-Twisted World's downstream era. Lü Zhuangyuan's nitpicking and the domestic spat over servants and pastries are almost *relatable*—a stark departure from the flesh-shrines and existential dread of earlier arcs. This is intentional: the novel is reminding us that even in a twisted world, people still have petty squabbles, still grieve for dead wives, and still struggle to adjust to sudden good fortune. The luxury of complaining about *too many pastries* is, in its own way, a sign of how far he has come—and how alien that comfort feels to someone forged by hardship.

### Kangning Hospital (康宁医院)

The name of a private psychiatric hospital, meaning "Health and Peace." A new, more refined cage for Li Huowang.

## Reading Guide

This is a "breather chapter" with teeth. On Li Huowang's side, it's a crushing defeat—not by a monster or a Siming, but by a doctor with a grudge and a taser-phone. Wu Cheng’s smirk is one of the most chilling moments in recent chapters, precisely because it’s so *human*. Li Huowang’s worst enemy right now isn’t a god or a demon; it’s a man in a white coat who thinks he’s doing the right thing.

The Lü Zhuangyuan segment reads like a warm, comedic interlude, but it carries a subtle melancholy. The old man was always at his best when he was fighting—bargaining, scheming, surviving. Now that he has what he wanted, he’s left with nothing to do but find fault with everything. His late wife “Plum” gets a quiet, poignant moment: the one person who would have loved this life with him never got to see it. Enjoy the slice-of-life while it lasts—these peaceful domestic moments are precious in a story that never stays still for long.

## The Art of the Breather: Why We Need Lü Zhuangyuan Right Now

Placing this domestic squabble immediately after Li Huowang’s brutal takedown is a stroke of pacing genius. It gives the reader emotional whiplash—and that’s exactly the point. You need to remember that there are still people in this world who worry about *servants and pastries* instead of parasitic gods and reality-altering lies. It’s a reminder of what Li Huowang is fighting to protect: a world where the most stressful thing in your day is your father-in-law complaining about the opera.

But the shadow of the Dao-Twisted World still hangs over it. Lü Juren’s quiet observation—that his father doesn’t know how to stop fighting—could just as easily apply to Li Huowang. The man who survives a lifetime of struggle doesn’t suddenly learn to be at peace. He just finds new things to struggle against.
