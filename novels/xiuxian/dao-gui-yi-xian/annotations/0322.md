## Chapter Overview

Get ready, fellow travelers, because Chapter 322 throws us straight into one of the most unsettling folk-horror scenarios yet: the “Water Son.” Li Huowang witnesses a bizarre, almost festive ceremony where a village sends a toothless old woman adrift on a boat into a dark cave, all while congratulating her on her good fortune. Our boy knows a “eat the heirless inheritance” scheme when he sees one, but instead of just walking away, he makes a snap decision to hitch a ride—invisible, of course—to see exactly what this so-called “Water Son” monster really is. What follows is a tense, claustrophobic journey into darkness, culminating in a face-off with a truly grotesque creature that forces Li Huowang to confront a new, terrifying question: just what *are* the voices in his head?

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang observes a crowd joyfully sending an elderly, childless woman named Liu into a river cave on a small boat, claiming the “Water Son” will care for her. He correctly identifies this as a form of “eating the heirless inheritance,” a cruel practice to seize her property.
- Despite his cynicism, Li Huowang chooses not to intervene immediately. Instead, he leaps onto the boat, using his invisibility, to follow the old woman and investigate the nature of the “Water Son.”
- Inside the dark, silent cave, the old woman’s boat is guided toward a single, floating white candle. The boat runs aground on a rocky shore.
- Li Huowang finally sees the “Water Son” up close: a malformed, barely-humanoid creature with impossibly long hair, hideously curled fingernails, mismatched teeth, and strands of hair that have grown into its own empty eye sockets. It is blind.
- Hongzhong identifies the creature as a “Renxiao,” explaining it is a half-human, half-ghost being that results from an old person with bad karma living too long without dying.
- As the Renxiao lures the old woman deeper into the darkness with the candle, Li Huowang stops pursuing. He turns to interrogate Hongzhong, challenging the foundation of his reality: if Hongzhong is just a hallucination, how can he know things that Li Huowang himself doesn’t know?

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is a goldmine of folk horror. The central concept, “Eating the Heirless Inheritance” (吃绝户, chī juéhù), is a dark historical practice where a community would divvy up the property of a family that died out, often by actively hastening that demise. The novel twists this into a ritual where the community dresses up what is essentially a death sentence as a blessing, complete with music and congratulations. The “Water Son” (水儿子, shuǐ érzi) is a cruel euphemism, implying a supernatural son will care for the old woman, when the reality is a grotesque creature.

Then there’s the “Renxiao” (人魈, rénxiāo). This is a fantastic piece of Chinese folklore. The term itself refers to a strange, feral being, often like a wild man or a ghostly creature. The lore provided by Hongzhong—that it forms from the old and miserable who refuse to die—is a brutal commentary on the vulnerability of the elderly in a harsh world. The description, with its retrograde hair and teeth, paints a picture of a being that has physically “receded” from humanity into something more primal and monstrous. This is textbook “Dao Gui Yi Xian”: taking a real folkloric kernel and amplifying it into visceral, body-horror reality.

### eating the heirless inheritance (吃绝户)

A cruel historical practice where a community would seize and divide the property of a family that had no male heir, often by hastening the family's end.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in atmosphere. Really pay attention to the sensory shift: the loud, “joyful” gongs and drums on the shore versus the dead, dripping silence inside the cave. That contrast is the whole novel in a microcosm. Also, keep your eyes on the prize: the final scene. Li Huowang’s sudden question to Hongzhong isn’t just him being paranoid. It’s the single most important question in the entire story. If his hallucinations know things he doesn’t, then the neat category of “hallucination” breaks down. This chapter is a huge clue that the voices in his head—Hongzhong, the skinless man—are not just psychological noise. They are something else, something that sees and knows the Dao-Twisted World in ways Li Huowang himself cannot.
