## Chapter Overview

This chapter is a brutal masterclass in *cognitive dissonance*—the gap between what Li Huowang sees and what he *chooses to describe*. After last night’s silent confrontation with the Second Spirit, Li Huowang takes Bai Lingmiao on a rare outing through the Qingqiu market. But this isn’t a charming bazaar of exotic wares. It’s a slaughterhouse dressed as a fair. Bleeding corpses are “wrestlers,” disemboweled slaves are “fire-eaters,” and mass graves are “monkey shows.” Li Huowang, missing one eye and one leg, hobbles through this nightmare and translates every horror into a gentle, childlike fantasy for the blindfolded Bai Lingmiao. The tenderness of their interaction becomes a sharp, unbearable contrast to the reality around them. This is *protection* as a conscious act of erasure—and the chapter makes you feel every lie.

## Key Plot Points

- The morning after the Second Spirit’s cryptic interaction, Li Huowang and Bai Lingmiao share a quiet, domestic moment as she tends to his regenerating leg.
- Li Huowang probes Bai Lingmiao about the Second Spirit’s influence; she deflects with calm reassurance, but the unease lingers.
- Bai Lingmiao, tired of being sheltered, asks to go out and explore Qingqiu. Li Huowang agrees, feeling guilty for forgetting she is still young.
- Blindfolded to protect her albino eyes, Bai Lingmiao relies entirely on Li Huowang’s verbal descriptions of the market.
- Li Huowang systematically translates horrific sights—a decapitated corpse becomes a clever monkey; a slave being disemboweled becomes a fire-breathing performer—to preserve her innocence.
- The chapter ends with Mantou the goat-dog dropping a half-human skull at Li Huowang’s feet, a silent, grotesque punctuation to the lie he is spinning.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Qingqiu as a “Free City of Horror”** — In Chinese web novel tradition, *Qingqiu* (青丘) is historically associated with fox spirits and mythical beasts. Here, it is repurposed into a lawless border market where the economy runs on slave labor, body-part trinkets, and vulture-scavenged remains. The author uses the name to evoke a *strange, exotic, morally neutral* frontier—a place where the grotesque is simply business as usual.

**The “Live Sheep as Grain” Logic** — Sun Baolu’s reasoning (live sheep = milk, meat, fuel, self-transporting) is a perfect example of *frontier pragmatism* in Chinese folk survival logic. It reflects how nomadic or semi-nomadic cultures within Chinese history treated livestock as *walking resources*, not just food. In the Dao-Twisted World, this practical wisdom coexists with unspeakable brutality.

**Bone Adornment** — The practice of crafting jewelry from human remains is a recurring motif in Chinese fictions of the *jianghu* underworld, particularly in borderlands or tribal settings where death is commodified. Here, it is presented matter-of-factly—not as a shocked discovery, but as an everyday ritual Li Huowang is expected to accept.

## Reading Guide

This chapter doesn’t advance the plot so much as it *twists the knife*. The tension comes entirely from what Li Huowang *chooses not to say*. Every moment of gentle domesticity—the milk mustache, the tapping on his arm—is layered over an image of a dead man being picked clean by vultures. If you’re reading for action, this might feel like a breather chapter. But *pay attention to the silence*. The fact that Bai Lingmiao believes every lie, and that Li Huowang is getting *better* at telling them, is a darker character beat than any fight scene. Also: Mantou is an absolute menace, and I love them.
