## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang tries to settle a debt with Bai Lingmiao, only to be gently outmaneuvered by her newfound stubbornness. A nightmare yanks him back to the terror of Danyangzi, leaving him restless and on edge. He channels that nervous energy into action: buying supplies, pressing Lü Zhuangyuan about leaving sooner, and interrogating the road ahead. Along the way, he meets a suspicious old beggar who claims to be a monk—and whose act is so hollow, it raises Li Huowang’s hackles. But while Li Huowang is busy scanning for threats, it’s the Fool who finds something first: free food from the forest. And in this world, nothing is ever truly free.

## Key Plot Points

1. **Bai Lingmiao’s quiet rebellion**: Li Huowang tries to repay her for the melted gold anklet, but she refuses—and reveals she saw it melted down with her own eyes. It’s a small act of defiance, but it signals her growth from passive follower to someone with her own backbone and pride.

2. **The Danyangzi nightmare**: A brief but visceral dream sequence where Li Huowang sees Bai Lingmiao, his mother, and Yang Na roasting sweet potatoes around a campfire—only for Danyangzi, now a three-headed, feathered giant, to pulverize them with a stone pillar. The terror is not just in the image but in the implication: the Dao-Twisted World’s threat can reach even his happiest fantasies.

3. **Strategic panic**: Li Huowang wakes up jittery and immediately pivots to preparation. He buys bedding, a cooking pot, and a second cart. He sends Gouwa to press Lü Zhuangyuan on departure time. He is no longer a passive victim—he is actively managing logistics, even when his hands are shaking.

4. **The fake monk’s test**: The “monk” is a shabby old man with no teeth, a mendicant looking for a temple that will feed him. Li Huowang’s instinct is to cut contact, fully aware that in this world, even a harmless-looking beggar could be a predator wearing a mask of idiocy.

5. **The Fool’s discovery**: The mentally simple member of their group, ignored by Li Huowang, wanders into the woods and finds “wild white rice.” The chapter ends on this ominous note—because in the Dao-Twisted World, free food from the forest is almost never a good sign.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Wild white rice (野白米饭)**: The Fool claims he found “wild white rice” in the forest. In a real-world Chinese context, “wild rice” could refer to *Zizania latifolia*, a plant whose swollen stems are edible. But in the context of this novel, the term is deliberately ambiguous. Free, foraged food in the woods—especially something that looks like cooked rice—is an old trope in Chinese folk horror: offerings left for spirits, or the bait of a demon. The reader is meant to feel unease, not relief.

**Gold anklet (金脚环)**: In traditional Chinese culture, a gold anklet worn by a girl could be a betrothal gift, a family keepsake, or an asset reserved for emergencies. Bai Lingmiao’s decision to melt it down and convert it into usable silver is a practical, desperate act—but it also carries emotional weight. She is sacrificing a tangible link to her mother and her past life for the group’s survival.

**Homeless monk (游方僧)**: The old man who claims to be a monk but has no temple is a classic figure in Chinese Buddhist tradition: a *youfang seng* (wandering monk), a mendicant who travels from temple to temple seeking instruction or alms. In this novel, however, the figure is subverted—Li Huowang suspects he may be a fraud or something worse. The hollow piety on display (a threadbare robe, no teeth, a string of fruit-pit beads) mirrors the corrupt or false religious figures that populate the Dao-Twisted World.

### Wandering monk (游方僧)

A mendicant Buddhist monk who travels from temple to temple, seeking shelter or alms. In the Dao-Twisted World, such figures are often hollow or false, hiding something behind their piety.

## Reading Guide

Fellow Daoists, we’re in the quiet-before-the-storm phase now. Li Huowang is doing “normal” things—shopping, delegating, arguing over money—but the undercurrent is all wrong. The nightmare shows you exactly what’s haunting him: Danyangzi isn’t just a memory; he’s a living shadow that can step into Li Huowang’s dreams and crush his hope. The fake monk, meanwhile, is a perfect little litmus test: Li Huowang’s instincts are now sharp enough to spot a hollow performance from a mile away. But the thing that should really make your skin crawl is the Fool. He’s the one nobody watches, the one who wanders off, the one who eats the free food from the woods. And in this book, the innocent are never safe—they’re usually the first to stumble into something that shouldn’t have been eaten. Enjoy the calm, because the road to Xijing is never as boring as it looks.
