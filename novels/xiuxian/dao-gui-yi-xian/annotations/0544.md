## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang’s hard-won secret is about to crack open. After glimpsing a forbidden line of text on Zhuge Yuan’s fan—something about a “Great Siming”—he barely escapes the karmic trap through sheer ignorance of classical Chinese. But no sooner has he crawled out of that hole than he lands in another. Back in the capital, the Knife-Pawnbroker Hong Da and the Supervisory Heavenly Office are waiting at his door, and they are *not* here for tea. Accused of stealing the Void Sword, Li Huowang finds himself cornered by old enemies—and one old “ally”—in a confrontation that turns the political screws tighter than ever.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang accidentally reads part of the script on Zhuge Yuan’s fan, a line referencing the “Great Siming.” He escapes the supernatural consequences only because his poor grasp of classical Chinese prevents him from truly understanding it.
- Zhuge Yuan confirms the rule: comprehension invites karmic entanglement; ignorance is safety.
- After exiting the sinkhole, Li Huowang sees smoke signals on the horizon—a sign of war approaching.
- He returns to the capital, only to find his courtyard gate unlocked and Hong Da waiting inside.
- The woman who once owned Li Huowang’s spine-blade, along with other Office agents, accuses him of using illusion tricks to steal the Void Sword. They surround him.
- Li Huowang refuses to be intimidated, framing the accusation as a political attack on the prince he serves. He accuses them of being hired by rival factions.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The **Great Siming** (大司命) is a direct reference to an ancient Chinese deity from the *Nine Songs* of the *Chu Ci*. In classical myth, the Great Siming and the Junior Siming (少司命) were divine arbiters of human lifespan. Here, the novel twists that familiar name into a higher-order entity that dwarfs even the grotesque Simings the reader has encountered so far. The key horror mechanic in this scene is that *comprehension* is the vector—merely *understanding* the name and its associated text is enough to link your karma to it. This is a wonderfully insidious form of supernatural infection: ignorance is literally safety, and literacy becomes a liability.

The use of **狼烟** (wolf-smoke / beacon fire) to signal war is a deeply traditional Chinese military signal, dating back to the Zhou dynasty. Thick smoke, often generated by burning wolf dung, was used along the Great Wall and frontier posts to warn of invasion. Seeing it on the horizon means the conflict hinted at earlier is now manifesting.

### Karmic entanglement

In this novel, simply *understanding* forbidden knowledge about the Siming can bind a character to their influence.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in the novel’s core tension: Li Huowang is caught between two completely different threat vectors—the cosmic (karmic contamination from forbidden knowledge) and the political (being framed by a rival faction). The chapter’s opening scene with Zhuge Yuan provides a rare moment of dark comedy: the illiterate peasant is saved by his lack of education. But don’t get too comfortable. The second half hits like a hammer. Li Huowang’s confrontation with the Office agents shows him at his most strategically defiant—he doesn’t just defend; he *attacks*, redirecting the accusation toward political conspiracy. It’s a glimpse of the player he’s becoming.

## Further Analysis: The Art of the Political Bluff

Li Huowang’s counter-accusation in this chapter is a classic *jianghu* move: when cornered, expand the battlefield. By framing his arrest as an attack on a prince’s faction, he forces the Office to weigh consequences beyond the simple theft of a sword. Whether he’s actually protected by the prince or just bluffing, the tactic works because it makes his enemies pause to calculate. It’s the same principle he used against the Daoist temple—never fight on the ground the enemy chooses.
