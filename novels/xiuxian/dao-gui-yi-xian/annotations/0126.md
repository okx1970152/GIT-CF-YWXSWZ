## Chapter Overview

Whoa. This chapter swings like a pendulum between two poles: Li Huowang the shrewd opportunist and Li Huowang the shattered mental patient. On one side, he’s coolly securing a rare alchemical text from Yingzi and meticulously mapping the entire Ao-Jing Sect stronghold—a man playing 4D chess in a snake pit. On the other, we see him sliding into a full-blown hallucinatory episode, mistaking furniture for people, and talking to his mother about oranges. The real kicker? Yingzi—our seemingly devout ally—almost slips him a poisoned iron bramble while he’s vulnerable. This isn’t just a spy thriller; it’s a masterclass in how *this* novel weaponizes mental illness: not as a weakness to be pitied, but as a tactical opening for betrayal. Get ready, because the question hanging in the air—*Why?*—isn’t just an accusation. It’s a knife-twist.

## Key Plot Points

- **The Gift**: Yingzi presents Li Huowang with the *Fire-Cloak Admonition Scripture*, sealed in translucent wax. It’s a massive tactical upgrade—a pain-based power manual—but written in an alien script of nested circles. She also hands over a handmade glossary (“celestial-fire script”) so he can learn it.
- **The Pact**: Li Huowang reads the gift correctly: an act of extreme loyalty. He reaffirms his promise to get her out of the demon’s lair—a bond sealed with the kind of grim respect that only desperate survivors share.
- **The Recon**: For three days, with Yingzi as his guide, Li Huowang systematically memorizes every inch of the Ao-Jing Sect’s cave complex. No one bothers him, but the “permission” feels like a leash—a predator letting its prey stretch its legs.
- **The Interrogation**: Shou San ambushes them on the way back, oozing false cheer and offering yet another “new method.” Li Huowang shuts him down hard, asserting control even when he has none. The power dynamic here is a fragile, terrifying dance.
- **The Collapse**: Back in his room, Li Huowang’s madness hits like a freight train. He hallucinates his mother and father, asking for orange peels and boiled water. The scene is jarringly domestic—which makes it worse.
- **The Assassination Attempt**: While Li Huowang is raving, Yingzi hesitates—then produces a bottle of acid, drops in a rusty iron bramble, and feeds the smoking mass to her “Benefactor.”
- **The Reveal**: At the last second, Li Huowang stops. The madness drains from his eyes. He looks at her with ice-cold clarity. *Why?*

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

Let’s chew on the *Fire-Cloak Admonition Scripture* for a second. In the real world, the term “Fire-Cloak” (火袄) is a dead giveaway: it’s a fusion of Zoroastrianism (袄教)—an ancient Persian faith centered on fire worship—and Nestorian Christianity (景教), which also traveled the Silk Road into Tang Dynasty China. The Ao-Jing Sect’s whole aesthetic is a blend of these historical “foreign” religions, weaponized into a body-horror cult. That “celestial-fire script” and its *gong-shang-jue-zhi-yu* phonetic notation? That’s the standard pentatonic scale from traditional Chinese music theory (宫商角徵羽), eerily repurposed as a mystical language for invoking pain. It’s a beautiful, terrifying collision of Silk Road cultures: Persian fire rites + Chinese musical cosmology + a wooden-bone doctrine of suffering. This isn’t just worldbuilding—it’s the novel borrowing real, forgotten history to make its horror feel ancient and inevitable.

Also worth noting: the *Admonition*’s script—circles within circles—is a power move in itself. It’s a deliberate barrier to entry. In xianxia, secret texts usually require spiritual cultivation to read. Here, the barrier is *linguistic*. You need a teacher. You need trust. That’s why Yingzi giving him the glossary is a bigger deal than a sword manual—it’s handing him the key to the sect’s language.

### Ao-Jing Sect (袄景教)

A fictional sect fusing Zoroastrianism (袄教) and Nestorian Christianity (景教); the original owners of the Thousand Greats Record and a pain-worshipping cult.

## Reading Guide

Alright, fellow survivors. This chapter is a gut-check. Let’s talk about the elephant in the room: Yingzi’s betrayal. Or was it? The chapter ends on a masterful cliffhanger—Li Huowang catching that iron bramble mid-air and asking *Why*. But here’s the twist we have to sit with: he was *pretending*. Or, more precisely, he *let her think he was fully lost*. This is huge. Because Li Huowang is learning that his madness isn’t just a liability—it’s a weapon. He knows the pattern by now: when the hallucinations hit, people drop their guard. He let her watch him rave. He let Mantou get kicked. He let her produce the poison. Because he needed to find out *who* in this godforsaken cave was a friend and who was a tool. The cold question at the end isn’t shock—it’s a test. He’s giving her a chance to explain before he acts.

Also, pay attention to the small domestic texture of the hallucination. Oranges. Boiled water. A mother who peels fruit. This isn’t random. It’s a *safe memory*—the last sane warm thing his brain can project when the walls crumble. The novel uses these mundane details to make the horror of the Ao-Jing Sect feel even worse. Rust. Acid. Iron thorns. That’s the world that’s *real*. No wonder he keeps trying to crawl back to the hospital.

The fight isn’t just against Shou San or the cult—it’s against the part of Li Huowang that wants to stay in a room with his mother, eating oranges forever.
