## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, fellow daoists—we're on a spirit-hunting road trip through the strange and unsettling lands of Great Qi! Li Huowang, guided by the ever-reliable Zhuge Yuan, tracks the elusive Shangji Guankou by reading the feng shui of mountains and rivers. But when night falls and they stop at a suspiciously empty town for shelter, our protagonist stumbles into a domestic nightmare: a man with bound feet, a rabbit-eared altar, and a town boiling something in secret at every doorway. It's a quieter chapter than the blood-soaked battles of Zhengde Temple, but the unease simmers low and constant, like that mysterious pot that never stops cooking. Get ready for slow-burn folk horror and a deep dive into Great Qi's bizarre social customs.

## Key Plot Points
- Zhuge Yuan tracks the Shangji Guankou using feng shui and celestial observation, revealing it's a living entity that moves and hides, not a fixed location.
- After a day of travel, Li Huowang calls a halt for the night at a desolate town where not a single shop is open.
- He seeks lodging at a house and is met by a man with bound feet (three-inch golden lotions)—a sign of Great Qi's unfamiliar and unsettling social structure.
- Li Huowang's paranoia spikes: he draws his sword on the man, only to be calmed by Zhuge Yuan, who explains the man is merely a *yuse* household (a male sex worker).
- The protagonist notices the man worships two clay statues of "Lord Hare" (兔爷), the patron deity of male courtesans.
- During the night, Li Huowang wakes to find the man has left and every household in town is boiling large pots—none of which smell like zongzi (Dragon Boat Festival food).
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang seeing crowds carrying earthenware pots out of the city, leaving the mystery of what they're cooking (and for whom) ominously unsolved.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter is a treasure trove of niche Chinese folk culture and historical trivia. Let's break it down.

First, the **bound feet** on a *man*. In imperial China, foot binding was overwhelmingly a practice imposed on women, symbolizing beauty and status. The idea of a man with bound feet is deeply jarring because it inverts the social order. Here in Great Qi, it signals that the man belongs to a specific *yuse* (鬻色) profession—literally "selling one's looks." This isn't just a random perversion; it's a deliberate worldbuilding choice that tells you Great Qi's social norms are fundamentally different from (and more unsettling than) Great Liang's.

Next, **Lord Hare (兔爷)**. In Chinese folklore, the Jade Rabbit pounds medicine on the moon. But in Beijing's folk tradition, "Lord Hare" (also known as Rabbit God or 兔儿爷) is a clay figurine with rabbit ears, often dressed in armor, worshipped as a protective deity. However, there's a secondary, darker layer: in some contexts, Lord Hare was also a patron deity for male entertainers (including homosexual courtesans), precisely because the term "rabbit" (兔儿) evolved into slang for male prostitutes in certain periods. The novel is tapping directly into this obscure but very real folk-religion niche.

And finally, the **mysterious pots**. Boiling something all night, carrying it out of the city at dawn—this is classic folk horror setup. It could be a seasonal ritual, an offering to a local god, or something far worse. The fact that Li Huowang's keen sense of smell can *rule out* zongzi leaves (a strong, distinctive aroma) means whatever's in those pots is likely meat, medicine, or something you don't want to name. The ambiguity is the point: Great Qi's customs are a closed book to our protagonist, and that ignorance is its own kind of dread.

## Reading Guide
This is a "breather chapter," but don't let the lack of explosions fool you. The horror here is *atmospheric* and *social* rather than visceral. Pay close attention to how Li Huowang's paranoia is both justified and slightly out of step with reality—he draws a sword on a terrified *yuse* worker because the man's feet trigger his deep-seated cultural unease. Zhuge Yuan's role as a calm translator of local customs is invaluable here; without him, Li Huowang would have been in a fight before he even had breakfast.

Also note the *domestic squabbling and petty economics*: the unease of haggling for a night's stay, the silver pressed into a trembling hand, the observation that the house is "quite clean" despite the owner's profession. These details keep the narrative grounded and gritty, even when the supernatural threat is only implied.

For seasoned readers of the genre, this chapter is a masterclass in *unreliable folk-horror set-dressing*. Everything is ordinary: a man cooking, a town at dawn, clay statues on an altar. But the *wrongness* of the details—the bound feet, the rabbit god, the endless boiling—creates a subtle dread that's harder to shake than a straightforward monster attack.

**Spoiler-free verdict**: Great Qi is not a friendly place. It's weird in ways Li Huowang doesn't have the cultural vocabulary to understand, and that makes it terrifying.
