## Chapter Overview
Well, fellow Daoists, the Heavenly Calamity is over—but nobody’s popping champagne. Li Huowang wakes up blind, brain-fried from seeing something no mortal should ever lay eyes on. The sun is back, but the scars are deeper than any missing light. And just when he thinks he can catch a breath, he realizes little Li Sui—the Black Tai Sui who calls him “Dad”—has vanished into thin air. What follows is a quiet, aching chapter about aftermath: a blind man groping for warmth, a village searching for a monster-child, and a final, skin-crawling visitor at the window that proves nothing has ended.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang wakes blind and exhausted, struggling to process what he glimpsed during the Heavenly Calamity—an existence without lies that records everything like a bronze cauldron.
- He gently asks Bai Lingmiao about the killing intent, but her silence tells him some wounds haven’t healed.
- Li Huowang discovers Li Sui is missing and launches a desperate village-wide search.
- Lü Xiucai reports that Mantou the dog is also gone, deepening the dread.
- Night falls with no sign of Li Sui; Li Huowang orders everyone to rest, his hope withering.
- The faceless Zuowandao taunts him, but Zhuge Yuan delivers a satisfying slap on command.
- In a quiet moment, Bai Lingmiao patiently feeds Li Huowang cold noodles.
- As the bowl empties, a familiar voice calls from the window—“Dad”—but the Second Spirit reveals it’s a skinned dog’s face, grinning.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
The concept of a “Heavenly Calamity” in this novel diverges sharply from the xianxia trope of tribulation lightning. Here, it’s an ontological disaster that corrupts or removes a fundamental law of reality. The thing Li Huowang glimpsed is described as having “no lie” upon its body, like a bronze cauldron recording history. In ancient China, bronze vessels (鼎, ding) were literal state archives; the “inscriptions” on ritual bronzes were considered immutable records of truth and legitimacy. What Li Huowang saw was a living, mind-shattering version of that—a truth so pure it burns. The line he struggles to remember (“Each time it is recalled…”) echoes a Daoist paradox about formless speech; in some classical texts, speaking a true name consumes the speaker’s lifespan. Our man got off easy with just memory loss.

The image of a skinned dog’s face calling out “Dad” is pure folk-horror. In Chinese folklore, dogs are associated with loyalty and warding off evil; a flayed dog’s head, especially one that mimics a loved one’s voice, inverts that protection into predation. The skinned corpse is a classic rural horror trope—the familiar stripped of its face, reduced to a hollow mimic.

## Reading Guide
If this chapter feels slow and bruised, good. That’s the point. After the cosmic vertigo of the Heavenly Calamity, the narrative pulls back to a human scale: a blind man learning to navigate without sight, a silent woman feeding him noodles, a village that can’t find its monster-child. The horror here isn’t in what attacks—it’s in what’s *missing*. Li Sui’s absence leaves a Li Huowang-shaped hole in the story; he’s literally groping in the dark. Pay attention to how much of this chapter is told through touch and sound—the softness of Bai Lingmiao’s hands, the sound of her footsteps, the crack of a fist on wood. The writing lets us feel Li Huowang’s blindness with him. And that final beat—a loving “Dad” from a skinned dog’s face—is a masterclass in quiet terror. No chase, no explosion. Just a sentence that makes your skin remember what it feels like to crawl.

## Deeper Lore: The Bronze Cauldron as Truth-Recorder
The comparison to a “bronze cauldron” (青铜鼎) isn’t casual. In ancient China, the Nine Tripod Cauldrons (九鼎) were said to be cast by Yu the Great to symbolize legitimate rule. Inscriptions on ritual bronzes were permanent records—treaties, decrees, ancestral honors. The entity Li Huowang describes has a body that *is* its record; it carries no lie because there’s no separation between truth and existence. This is a nightmare version of the *dao*: a thing that simply *is*, without interpretation or mercy. No wonder the mere sight of it breaks something in a mortal mind.
