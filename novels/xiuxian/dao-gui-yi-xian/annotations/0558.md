## Chapter Overview

This chapter delivers a devastating gut punch disguised as a quiet, domestic interlude. We open with Li Huowang in the modern world, exchanging tender—almost painfully normal—words with his mother, Sun Xiaoqin. But nothing here is simple. His refusal to eat isn’t stubbornness; it’s a survival protocol for someone whose body is being used in two realities at once. Dr. Yi Donglai, the stubborn new psychiatrist, drops a bombshell: he’s found “patterns” in Li Huowang’s condition and has a new treatment plan. But before we can process that, the world flips. Li Huowang wakes in the saddle, under a violent storm, racing toward the capital. And what he finds there is a scene of irreversible political horror: Princess Anping is dead, her head added to a grisly pyramid by a patched-robed Daoist. The last obstacle to Ji Lin’s coronation is gone. The Dice has won. And Li Huowang, for once, arrives too late.

## Key Plot Points

- A quiet hospital scene with Sun Xiaoqin reveals the deep, fragile love between mother and son, undercut by the tragic reality that Li Huowang cannot eat without risking his physical continuity across worlds.
- Dr. Yi Donglai introduces himself as a man of principle—a doctor who refuses to give up. He reveals he’s found “weaknesses” and “patterns” in Li Huowang’s psychosis, hinting at a new, potentially invasive intervention.
  - Li Huowang bluntly refuses him: *“You really can’t help with this.”* It’s not contempt; it’s exhausted certainty.
- The transition back to the Dao-Twisted World is abrupt and sensory: thunder, rain, a muddy road, and a horse being pushed to its death with a blood talisman.
- Li Huowang arrives at the capital during a storm, only to find Princess Anping’s residence ablaze and a skull-pyramid built before the flames.
- The killer introduces himself: a slovenly, patched-robed Daoist with a tacky green ball tied to his head, holding Anping’s severed head. He casually calls Li Huowang “Erjiu” and mocks his slow arrival.
- With Anping dead, Ji Lin’s path to the throne is clear. The Dice’s long-term plan has reached its climax.
- Li Huowang makes a desperate decision: kill the Daoist—the only witness to Anping’s death—so the world can *believe* she is still alive, and delay Ji Lin’s coronation.
- The chapter ends with a head-retracting turtle-guile trick and a furious lunge from a tentacle-wreathed Li Huowang.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **The Dice (骰子) and Political Engineering**: The Dice, a high-ranking member of the Zuowandao, has been subtly manipulating Great Qi’s succession crisis for hundreds of chapters. His goal is not power for himself, but *instability*—a broken succession allows chaos to flourish. Ji Lin’s uncontested ascension is the final piece of an elaborate, long-game scheme.
- **The “Skull Pyramid” (京观 / Jingguan)**: A grim historical war practice where severed enemy heads were stacked into a monument to display military prowess. Its presence here signals a deliberate, theatrical act of political terror—not simple murder.
- **Head-Retracting Martial Trick**: The Daoist’s ability to retract his head into his robe like a turtle is an exaggerated martial arts trope, often associated with “Shrinking the Body” techniques in wuxia/xianxia. It’s a visual representation of a hardened body cultivator’s defense.
- **“Sending Charcoal in a Snowstorm vs. Adding Flowers to Brocade”**: This is a classic Chinese idiom pairing. Sending charcoal in a snowstorm = doing a valuable favor when someone is in desperate need. Adding flowers to brocade = a superficial, unnecessary gesture of goodwill. The Daoist uses it to taunt Li Huowang for showing up late—his timing is worthless because the *need* has already passed.

A classic Chinese idiom pairing. The former means doing a valuable favor in a time of desperate need; the latter means adding a superfluous, unnecessary gesture. The Daoist uses it to mock Li Huowang’s poor timing.

## Reading Guide

Well, folks, the Dice has played his final hand, and it’s a royal flush on a bed of skulls. This chapter is the payoff of a political plot that’s been simmering for hundreds of chapters—and it hits like a freight train. Li Huowang’s desperate logic at the end is both brilliant and tragic: he can’t undo the murder, but he *can* kill the messenger. If no one knows Anping is dead, she’s effectively still alive. It’s a Zuowandao-level move from a man who’s learned from the worst teachers.

Pay close attention to Dr. Yi Donglai’s new “treatment plan.” His stubbornness is a red flag—either he’s genuinely going to be the one to crack Li Huowang’s case, or he’s about to interfere in ways that will backfire catastrophically. Given this novel’s track record, I’d bet on the latter.

And that patched-robed Daoist? Don’t let the goofy green ball fool you. Anyone who can build a skull-pyramid mid-storm and taunt Li Huowang with casual familiarity is not a small fry. Get ready for a fight with stakes that aren’t just physical—but political, existential, and absolutely *time-critical*.
