## Chapter Overview
Two worlds, two fronts. While the monster Peng Longteng calmly justifies a massacre of thousands to her horrified supervisor, Li Huowang is in a stone forest, training a spectral projection ability that could give him an edge—if survival didn’t hinge on a dwindling food supply. Chapter 201 is a masterful juxtaposition of political terror and quiet desperation. On one side, a general who treats human life as bargaining chips; on the other, a fugitive refining his only trick while his companions weigh the difference between snake sashimi and starvation. The title “Both Sides” isn’t just about the two camps—it’s about the two kinds of horror this novel serves: the deafening roar of a tyrant’s rampage and the pin-drop anxiety of running out of rations.

## Key Plot Points
- **Peng Longteng’s confession**: She openly admits to slaughtering a town of thousands to pay her troops, framing it as “collecting her own reward” after the court skimmed the pay. She argues it’s a stable solution—no extra cost to the court, and the soldiers get paid.
- **Political calculation**: Cao Hai warns that the court will eventually know, but Peng Longteng dismisses this, trusting that the officials will cover it up to save face. She even claims to have “smoked out a few Siqi spies” as justification.
- **Personal vendetta**: Peng Longteng’s left eye is now blind from Li Huowang’s attack, and most of her “Loyal and True” tattoo is burned off. She demands the Supervisory Heavenly Office locate him—she will kill him herself, overruling the suggestion that the Ao-Jing Sect makes it someone else’s problem.
- **Team survival**: In the stone forest cave, Gao Zhijian kills a snake. Jin Shanzhao turns it into raw sashimi, which the group eats eagerly. Li Huowang ignores them to focus on perfecting his phantom projection.
- **Ability training**: After days of practice, Li Huowang can now produce his phantom anywhere within a zhang, and can also leave the phantom in place while moving his invisible body. He sees it as a powerful but not invincible trick.
- **Dry rations crisis**: Bai Lingmiao reports that even with rationing, food will only last two more days. Li Huowang estimates seven days of travel to the border—meaning they’re in a race against starvation.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Peng Longteng and the logic of irregular warfare**  
Peng Longteng’s behavior—sacking a town to pay her troops—draws on a grim historical reality in pre-modern Chinese armies: *cannon fodder soldiers* (贼配军, zéi pèi jūn) were often convicts conscripted into the army, treated as disposable. When pay was withheld, mutiny was common. A general who “borrowed” from civilians wasn’t acting out of cruelty alone, but out of cold pragmatism: keep the troops fed or be killed by your own men. Her line “General in the field may refuse orders from the capital” (将在外命有所不受) is a classic Chinese military adage, often invoked by warlords to justify independent action. Note how she also uses a fabricated “rebellion” pretext—this mirrors real historical justifications for looting: if you call the town rebels, their property becomes legitimate spoils of war.

**Li Huowang’s projection ability**  
This ability—splitting one’s awareness from the body, projecting a visible phantom while the real body becomes invisible—has roots in Daoist and folk *qigong* legends of “leaving the shell” (出壳). However, unlike a clean supernatural power-up, Li Huowang’s version is linked to his Heart-Element constitution and his fractured psyche. It’s not an acquired technique but a side effect of being a walking cosmological anomaly. The narrative never lets him feel triumphant about it—it’s just another tool he has to sharpen through grueling repetition. And note the potential cost: earlier chapters hinted that such abilities could attract unwanted attention or drain his lifeforce, though here he frames it purely as a tactical advantage.

**Dry rations vs. raw snake**  
Jin Shanzhao’s offer of raw snake meat (蛇脍) nods to an ancient Chinese practice of eating raw fish or meat, especially in times when fire was forbidden. Li Huowang’s group is under strict no-fire orders to avoid revealing their position—a classic survival constraint in pursuit narratives. The description of the snake as “chewing on fat” suggests poor flavor but passable nutrition. In xianxia terms, this is a “mortal stamina problem”—they aren’t cultivators who can go weeks without food. The countdown of two days’ rations vs. seven days’ travel creates a classic *resource race* subplot.

## Reading Guide
Chapter 201 is a lesson in how Dao Gui Yi Xian balances its horror with grounded stakes. Peng Longteng’s scene is operatic in its violence and dialogue—she’s a mad war machine, but her reasoning is terrifyingly sound. Watch how Cao Hai’s fussy, almost effeminate manner contrasts with her brutal directness. Then the scene cuts to Li Huowang, practicing a ghost trick while his friends eat raw snake and worry about dinner. The tonal whiplash is intentional: the macro threat (a murderous general hunting him) and the micro crisis (running out of food) are both real, and both require different kinds of cleverness to survive.

This chapter also subtly reinforces the novel’s theme of *pragmatic morality*. Li Huowang doesn’t have the luxury of moral outrage at the massacre—he’s too busy trying not to starve. The silence from the group about the town’s fate is a quiet horror in itself: everyone knows that news travels, and that death is closing in. And yet, life goes on. They eat. They train. They plan. That’s the Dao-Twisted World in a nutshell: no time to grieve, just time to adapt.

**What to watch**—Li Huowang’s projection ability will likely be pivotal in the coming escape. But the real question is: how many food sources can they find in seven days? The answer might be “none,” and then the real test begins.
