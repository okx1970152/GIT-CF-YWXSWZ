## Chapter Overview

Fellow Daoists, buckle up—Chapter 91 is a masterclass in *internal horror*. Our boy Li Huowang is back at it, and this time the threat isn't some temple abomination or a flesh demon. It's something far more insidious: *himself*. After a grueling night stalked by animal eyes that might or might not be supernatural, Li Huowang unleashes a brutal outburst and returns to interrogate the village chief, Wu Qing. But the real enemy isn't the trembling old man tied to a pillar. It's the creeping voice of Danyangzi inside his own skull. The chapter pivots on a single moment of self-recognition—a mirror held up by his own double—and leaves us staring into the abyss of what Li Huowang is becoming. Get ready for a gut-punch.

## Key Plot Points

- While returning to the village, the group is surrounded by countless pairs of green eyes in the dark. Li Huowang, fed up with being toyed with, tears off his own fingernails as a sacrificial cost to activate the *Thousand Greats Record*, slaughtering the animals instantly. The corpses are total carnage.
- Xiaoman quietly points out that the group may have broken the local "rules" by killing these animals. Li Huowang dismisses her concern as manipulation and bullies everyone onward.
- Back at the Wu family compound, the group corners the goateed village chief, Wu Qing, and ties him to a pillar. Li Huowang interrogates him about "Layue Shiba" (the evil spirit that resets every lunar year), but the old man claims total ignorance.
- Li Huowang grows increasingly violent and reveals a torture tool to force the truth out. He's inches away from piercing Wu Qing's collarbone when a large hand—belonging to his own doppelgänger in a blood-red robe—stops him and warns him he's becoming Danyangzi.
- The moment of recognition hits: Li Huowang realizes his brutality is not just tactical, it's *contagious*. He's internalized the monster he's fighting.
- The next morning, his double performs a stunning twist—he's already reconciled with Wu Qing over breakfast, showing a cold, social intelligence that contrasts sharply with Li Huowang's reactive rage.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Layue Shiba (腊月十八)** – This is not a person. "Layue" is the twelfth month of the lunar calendar, and "Shiba" is the 18th day. In Chinese folk religion, certain evil spirits are said to *reset* their appearance, powers, and even their weaknesses on specific calendar dates. Imagine a supernatural entity that gets a full stat-reset once a year—you can't learn its moves, you can't exploit its old weaknesses. It's a terrifying concept for anyone trying to hunt it down, and it explains why Li Huowang is so desperate: Danyangzi is re-forming inside him and there's a ticking clock.

**Self-reference as "Li-shixiong" and the Double** – A crucial narrative device. The Li Huowang we see in the blood-red robe is, in a sense, his idealized or surviving self—the calm, strategic, social operator. When he calls the other Li Huowang "shidi" (junior disciple brother), it's a chilling reminder that the protagonist has split. This is not multiple personality disorder as usually depicted; it's a *cultivation consequence*. The "gap" is where Danyangzi is creeping in. The double is both a survival mechanism and a warning.

**Thousand Greats Record (大千录)** – The red bamboo slip that Li Huowang uses is a foundational artifact of a *suffering-cult*. Its powers are not free; every use demands a tangible sacrifice—fingernails, blood, pain. It's the ultimate "no shortcuts" item in a genre usually full of power-ups. The more you use it, the more you trade your humanity for power.

### Thousand Greats Record (《大千录》)

A red bamboo slip artifact central to a suffering-cult. Its powers are accessed through self-inflicted pain and sacrifice (e.g., torn fingernails), reflecting the novel's theme that power always has a visceral, tangible cost.

### False "Rules" (规矩)

A recurring folk-horror concept in this novel. Local taboos are often not divine laws but social control mechanisms used by entities or families to manipulate outsiders. Li Huowang's defiance may be smart—or catastrophic.

## Reading Guide

This is a *character turning point* chapter. Don't skip it thinking nothing "happens"—because the real event is internal. Watch how Li Huowang reacts to his own reflection. The fact that the blood-robed Li Huowang has already smoothed things over with Wu Qing shows that the "rational" part of the protagonist is working in the background while the emotional part is spiraling. If you love psychological horror mixed with xianxia mechanics, this is where the series hits its stride. Also, keep an eye on the animal massacre—this might come back to bite them. Nothing in the Dao-Twisted World is truly random.

**Deep Dive: The Folk Horror of "Rules" in the Dao-Twisted World**

A subtle but important pattern emerges here. The villagers, the old man, Xiaoman—everyone keeps talking about "rules." Don't touch the animals. Don't break the taboos. In traditional Chinese rural horror (and indeed in the folk religion roots of this novel), the supernatural world is not chaotic; it's *legalistic*. Spirits, demons, and territorial gods are bound by rules—and so are humans. Breaking them is how you invite disaster, but *knowing* them is how you survive. Li Huowang's dismissal of the rules as "bluffs" may be his biggest blind spot yet. He's operating on modern skepticism in a world built on ancient, rigid logic. It's a perfect setup for a future trap.
