## Chapter Overview

Curfew falls on the Upper Capital, trapping our stranded crew inside a single inn. While the innkeeper tries to profit off the chaos with a shameless auction, Gao Zhijian spots a familiar figure wandering the empty streets: Li Sui, the Black Tai Sui–possessed “daughter” of Li Huowang, looking for her lost dad. What follows is a raw, bone-crunching display of pure martial grit as Gao Zhijian—a man of few words but terrifying strength—navigates a curfew enforced by armored cavalry and a towering military artist named Ma Wu. It’s a chapter that swaps cosmic horror for the simple, brutal physics of flesh and iron, and it reveals a new depth in the quietest member of Li Huowang’s crew.

## Key Plot Points

- The innkeeper tries to auction off the limited rooms as night falls and the curfew traps everyone inside; Gao Zhijian ignores the petty drama entirely.
- Gao Zhijian spots a beautiful young girl outside during curfew and realizes with a jolt: it’s Li Sui, the evil spirit who always wore a straw rain cape. She’s calling for her dad—Li Huowang.
- Gao Zhijian twists the lock off the door like tinfoil and charges into the street to find her, ignoring the stunned crowd behind him.
- Li Sui explains that Li Huowang flew away on a sword with a white-bearded “State Preceptor,” leaving her alone.
- A squad of cavalry armed with blood-dripping long sabers spots them and charges; Gao Zhijian hurls one horse and rider onto a rooftop with a single, devastating bear-hug and throw.
- A signal flare goes up, and a massive warrior named Ma Wu—wielding twin melon hammers—blocks their escape.
- After a brutal fistfight where Gao Zhijian is badly beaten but never falters, his body moves on instinct, and he stops his elbow an inch from Ma Wu’s throat.
- Ma Wu acknowledges Gao Zhijian’s worth, gives a warning about the night curfew, and invites him to the Ma family if he ever wants a proper military post.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Void Nian (虚年)** – The chapter title “Day Curfew” hints at a wider supernatural lockdown, but the *Void Nian* itself is a corrupted beast born from the legendary Nian monster of Chinese folklore. In traditional myth, the Nian is a fearsome beast that appears at New Year’s and is scared away by red paper and firecrackers. In the Dao-Twisted World, this folk memory is twisted into a yang-life–draining horror that stalks forbidden hours. The name “虚年” (Empty/Void Year) signals it’s not the real beast but a hollow, parasitic copy—feeding on the very concept of time and vitality.

**Military Arts (兵家功法) & the “Tiger’s Mouth” (虎口)** – Ma Wu’s challenge hinges on a key xianxia / wuxia principle: military cultivation arts are *not* for common practice. The phrase “兵家” (Bing Jia) refers to the ancient School of Military Strategy, but in the novel’s world, it means a special lineage of martial power that requires raw talent and the right bloodline. Gao Zhijian’s ability to catch a charging horse and throw it is a testament to the *blood-drunk berserker* archetype—a brute who doesn’t need elegant forms, only a massive foundation of innate strength and combat instinct. The “tiger’s mouth” (the webbed part of the palm between thumb and index finger) splitting open is a classic wuxia detail showing the strain of unrefined power meeting a superior technique.

**The Day Curfew (昼禁)** – Not just a military lockdown: in the Dao-Twisted World, a “Day Curfew” signals that the authorities are actively hunting something supernatural—or preparing for a ritual that cannot be witnessed. The fact that armored cavalry are patrolling in full force, with a *red smoke signal* system, suggests institutional preparedness for higher-tier threats. It’s a small but potent reminder that even mundane imperial power in this world is always on edge, always ready to kill first and ask questions later.

### Void Nian (虚年)

A corrupted Nian beast that drains yang life on contact; named to distinguish it from the traditional folkloric Nian or the “Wàn Nián” previously mentioned.

## Reading Guide

Get ready for a chapter that feels like a *muscle-bound one-shot* in an otherwise eldritch horror series. Gao Zhijian has always been the strong, silent type, but here he finally gets to show that “strong” means *throwing a horse onto a roof without breaking a sweat*. His internal monologue is refreshingly simple—he runs, he fights, he remembers the voice—and that directness makes every punch land harder.

The real treat is watching him earn Ma Wu’s respect through sheer *grit*. Ma Wu is a classic jianghu tough guy: arrogant, brutal, but fair when bested. His invitation—“look up the Ma family if you ever want a soldier’s pay”—is a rare moment of institutional recognition for a man who has always been a wanderer. It’s a quiet seed for a possible future path, buried in a chapter full of broken bones and bloody knuckles.

And then there’s Li Sui. Seeing her through Gao Zhijian’s eyes—a beautiful girl lost in the dark, crying for her dad—is a powerful reminder of the *child* inside the parasite. Her confusion and loneliness mirror Gao Zhijian’s own past, forging a bond that doesn’t need words. It’s a chapter about raw power, yes, but also about the fragile duty of protecting someone smaller than you.
