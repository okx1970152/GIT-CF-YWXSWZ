## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this chapter is a psychological minefield. Li Huowang finally gets a clear, terrifying answer about the Ao-Jing Sect's power structure: the Dung-Beetle Ascension isn't just a one-time torture session, but a ladder. Each rung puts you closer to the primordial god Bashe, and the big prize at the top (ten Calamities) is nothing less than bodily sainthood. The man kneeling before him, Zu Yi, has already gone through this grinder and is begging for the method. But instead of a triumphant power-up, this revelation nearly shatters Li Huowang. Because he knows exactly what the next step on that ladder would cost him—and the price is written in the faces of everyone he loves.

## Key Plot Points
- Zu Yi identifies Li Huowang as the Great Elder by the fact that he survived the Intercalating the Five Phases ritual, a badge that no imposter could fake.
- The Ao-Jing Sect's hierarchical structure is laid bare: each Ascension adds one "Calamity" (劫); three Calamities earn the title of Great Elder; five Calamities are enough to match the Zuowandao's top leader, the Dice.
- The legendary figure of the Gu-Spirit (牯神) is confirmed as an Ao-Jing initiate who achieved ten Calamities and seized a fragment of the Way from Bashe itself, achieving bodily sainthood.
- Li Huowang's initial, manic excitement at this shortcut to power is violently interrupted by the memory of his past Ascensions—the unbearable agony, the innocent blood, and the looming threat that the next victim would be his own mother.
- Zhuge Yuan's voice of reason pulls him back from the brink, and Li Huowang resolves to find another way, vowing not to become the same kind of monster as the Dice.
- A critical piece of intel emerges: the Ao-Jing Sect is also hunting the Dice, which means the Supervisory Heavenly Office has them on its list as well—an alliance of sorts is possible.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Dung-Beetle Ascension (苍蜣登阶) and the "Calamity" (劫) System**  
This chapter gives us our clearest look yet at how the Ao-Jing Sect's power system works. In standard xianxia, "Tribulations" (天劫) are heavenly lightning strikes that test a cultivator's Dao heart—you pass, you level up; you fail, you're dust. The Ao-Jing version twists this into something far more visceral. Each "Calamity" here isn't a punishment from heaven; it's a self-inflicted wound, a step deeper into the suffering-worship of their god Bashe. The fact that ten Calamities equals bodily sainthood is a direct inversion of the usual "ascend to heaven and become an immortal" trope. Here, sainthood is achieved by descending deeper into agony, not rising above it.

**The Gu-Spirit (牯神) and the "Mystic Female" Verse**  
When Li Huowang recalls the line "The Gu-Spirit dies not, and so it is called the Mystic Female," he's quoting the *Dao De Jing* (Chapter 6). In the original, Laozi uses the "Mystic Female" (玄牝) as a metaphor for the Dao's endless, generative power—the origin of all things. But in this twisted world, that same verse describes a mortal who *stole* part of that power from a god. The poem's original meaning is still intact, but the context is pure body horror: the "gate of the mystic female" is the threshold of a suffering-god's domain, and the Gu-Spirit is the one who crawled through it intact.

**The Logic of "Payback" (还人情) in Jianghu Culture**  
Zu Yi's behavior—first opposing Li Huowang because he owed a debt, then immediately switching sides when the debt was paid—is a perfect example of *jianghu* (rivers and lakes, the underworld) ethics. In this culture, personal loyalty and face (面子) often override institutional loyalty. A favor is a currency. Once it's spent, the relationship resets. It's not hypocrisy; it's a kind of rigid honor code. Zu Yi is being completely honest, which makes him both reliable and deeply unsettling.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in emotional whiplash. It starts as a dry, tactical info-dump about power levels—the kind of conversation that would normally have a xianxia protagonist rubbing his hands together. But the moment Li Huowang starts to calculate his next Ascension, the narrative pulls the rug out. The memory of his past pain isn't just a flashback; it's a full-on psychological assault. The way the text shifts from strategic thinking to screaming "Mom! I can't tell!" is the novel's core horror engine at work. This isn't a hero who hesitates because he's weak. He hesitates because he's already seen what the "power-up" does to the people around him, and the cost is no longer theoretical. Pay close attention to the line "Last time it was Yang Na. Who would be next?"—that's not a rhetorical question. It's a prophecy. The chapter's real victory isn't learning about the ten-Calamity god; it's Li Huowang's decision to refuse the easy path, even when every logical cell in his brain is screaming at him to take it.
