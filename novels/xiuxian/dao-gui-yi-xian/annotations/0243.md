## Chapter Overview

Our boy Li Huowang is having *one of those days*. Fresh off a brutal fight, his body half-shredded, he’s trying to get his battered group out of Qingqiu’s nightmare caves. The exit is a vertical climb that would be suicide for most—but that’s where the chapter’s first chilling pivot happens. Bai Lingmiao, under spirit-possession, does something that moves her from “supporting priestess” to “genuinely terrifying supernatural asset.” She slithers up the stone like she was born with no bones, a red-veiled human-snake, and throws down the lifeline.

But that’s just the setup. The real meat of the chapter is the ambush. Li Huowang insists on holding the rear, and the darkness delivers exactly what he’s expecting: Han Fu, the two-headed Daoist who’s been bleeding and waiting. What follows is a short, brutal scene where Li Huowang’s humanity seems to thin almost to nothing—he’s out of mercy, out of time, and out of fucks to give. Han Fu thinks he’s won, talismans stabbed into Li Huowang’s innards… until Li Huowang simply ignores the trap and guts him. It’s raw, it’s fast, and it’s a quiet statement: the old Li Huowang is burning away, and what’s left is a predator wearing his scars.

## Key Plot Points

- Gao Zhijian’s chain is repurposed from binding tool to climbing rope, showing the group’s tactical adaptability.
- Bai Lingmiao, possessed by her immortal, scales a near-vertical cave wall like a boneless snake—her movements are uncanny and beast-like, signaling her growing hybrid power as a spirit-medium.
- Li Huowang orders everyone to go first, insisting on holding the rear despite his grievous wounds; his reasoning is coldly pragmatic, not heroic—he knows he’s the strongest and wants no one else to die covering the retreat.
- The Black Tai Sui is laboriously lashed to the chain and hauled up by Gao Zhijian, a reminder that even their “cargo” is a grotesque living entity.
- Han Fu emerges from the shadows, having been waiting for Li Huowang to be alone. He reveals that he planted talismans inside Li Huowang’s body during their earlier fight.
- Han Fu activates a massive talisman that envelopes and immobilizes Li Huowang, then brazenly debates with his own second head about what to do with the “prize.”
- Li Huowang, apparently still able to move despite the talisman, drives his sword through Han Fu’s belly and carves him open from gut to skull, killing him mid-sentence.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is a masterclass in subverting the *taliman trap* trope, a staple of Chinese supernatural fiction. Talismans (符, *fu*) are typically drawn on paper or wood by Daoist practitioners to summon spirits, ward off evil, or bind enemies. Han Fu uses a “ghostly talisman”—a massive, black-and-yellow sigil drawn on the ground—combined with body-planted charms to create a physically and spiritually paralyzing cage. It’s textbook cultivation-world tactics: seal the opponent’s organs, immobilize them with a formation, and collect the prize.

Li Huowang breaks it by sheer, unexplained willpower. But here’s the fun bit: *he shouldn’t be able to*. In standard xianxia logic, a properly placed internal seal is the end of the fight. Li Huowang’s defiance of this rule is a quiet lore signal—the Heart-Element is not just a rare alchemical ingredient; it seems to operate on a different set of metaphysical rules. You can’t bind what doesn’t fully belong to this world’s logic.

Also, Bai Lingmiao’s possession-state is worth unpacking. When a chuma medium is “ridden” by a spirit immortal, their body can perform anatomically impossible feats—scaling sheer walls without limbs, moving like a liquid. The red veil she uses is a ritual prop: it covers her face to protect her own identity while the spirit uses her body. The fact that she *chooses* to do this, after a moment of visible hesitation, shows she is actively consenting to be a vessel—a big ethical and narrative step for her character.

### Spirit-possession (附身, fushen)

In chuma mediumship, a spirit immortal temporarily inhabits the medium’s body, granting supernatural physical abilities. The medium often uses a ritual veil to protect their own identity.

## Reading Guide

This is a short chapter, but it’s a *character statement*. Watch Li Huowang’s voice. He’s curt, practical, and stripped of melodrama. When he tells Sun Baolu to shut up and climb, it’s not machismo—it’s the cold arithmetic of survival. He knows his body is a wreck, but he also knows that wreck is still the deadliest thing in the cave. That’s a terrifying self-awareness to have.

Han Fu’s conversation with his own second head is a darkly comic highlight—two mouths debating market strategy over a frozen victim. But his overconfidence is the classic tragic flaw of the Dao-Twisted World: everyone thinks they understand the rules, and everyone is wrong. Li Huowang’s final, mechanical execution—cutting across the throat, mouth, nose, and forehead—is not a triumphant kill. It’s grim, methodical, and utterly without joy. This is not the victory of a hero. It’s the work of a man who has stopped asking questions about what he has to do to survive.

Also, *how* did he break the talisman? The chapter doesn’t explain it. And that uncertainty—Lingering questions about the nature of the Heart-Element’s resistance—is exactly the point. The Dao-Twisted World doesn’t owe us answers after every fight. Sometimes the monster just gets back up.
