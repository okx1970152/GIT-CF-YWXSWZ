## Chapter Overview

This chapter delivers the brutal, exhausted aftermath of the Dragon Vein assault, landing a decisive blow before pivoting into an atmosphere of profound ontological dread. Li Huowang executes a lethal gambit against the corrupted Great Qi emperor, separating him from the Dragon Vein and flinging him into Great Liang. But victory feels hollow. The landscape is ripped apart, the sun shows black spots, and the looming Void Nian retreats after reciting a cryptic poem. The chapter ends with Li Huowang and Zhuge Yuan sitting on a newly formed cliff, staring at a world that seems to be coming apart at the seams.

## Key Plot Points

- Peng Longteng intercepts Li Huowang midair and redirects him toward the child emperor for a killing strike.
- Zhuge Yuan warns that merely killing the emperor won’t stop the Dragon Vein from reviving him; Li Huowang must sever their connection first.
- Li Huowang severs the emperor’s back from the Dragon Vein, then, at Zhuge Yuan’s second warning, flings the corpse into a historical fissure toward Great Liang to prevent resurrection.
- An earthquake reshapes the battlefield, turning flat plains into steep cliffs and revealing a corrupted, spotted sun.
- The Void Nian returns but, after a prolonged, uneasy standoff, recites a line of classical poetry and retreats without further combat.
- Li Huowang barely has time to process the world’s fluid reality before collapsing from exhaustion, sitting on the cliff’s edge with Zhuge Yuan in shared silence.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Dragon Vein (龙脉)** is a core concept in Chinese geomancy and imperial cosmology. It’s the subterranean flow of 'dragon qi' (*longqi*) that determines a dynasty’s fortune, health, and legitimacy. If the Dragon Vein is cut, the imperial line falls. Here, the novel literalizes that idea: the vein is a sentient, grotesque mass that can empower and revive a dead emperor. Cutting it open is both a physical and metaphysical assassination.

**The Void Nian (虚年)** is a corrupted version of the legendary Nian beast. In Chinese folklore, the Nian is a monster that emerges at the Lunar New Year, repelled by fire, noise, and the color red. This *Void* Nian is twisted further: it mimics human speech, has a woman's face set into a lion's body, and drains *yang life* on contact. Its recitation of classical verse is not just mimickry—it suggests a tragic, trapped intelligence.

**The Sky Dog Devouring the Sun (天狗食日)** is one of the most famous Chinese folk explanations for a solar eclipse. The Sky Dog (*Tiangou*) was said to swallow the sun, causing darkness and chaos. In this novel, it is literalized as a phenomenon of the Heavenly Calamity (*Tianzai*), where a fundamental law of reality breaks down. The fact that the calamity 'passes' but leaves the sun spotted is a deeply unsettling suggestion that even when the event ends, the world *remains broken*.

## Reading Guide

Get ready for a heavy one. This chapter doesn't give you the adrenaline of the fight—it gives you the grim comedown. Li Huowang wins, but he's left crawling out of dirt, vomiting soil, and staring at a world that literally reshaped itself overnight. The sun has spots. The cliff wasn't there before. This is not celebratory victory, it's survival fatigue.

Pay close attention to the Void Nian's poem: it's quoting Yan Shu's *Butterflies in Love with Flowers*, a famous Song Dynasty lyric about longing and separation. In context, it reads like an expression of immense loneliness—a beast that mimics human emotion so perfectly it might actually feel something. Whether that makes it more tragic or more dangerous is left ambiguous.

Also note Li Huowang's internal moment of doubt, followed by immediate self-violence to *force himself not to doubt*. This is classic Heart-Element psychology: his power depends on stable belief, so he cannot afford even a second of hesitation. The chapter is meditative, atmospheric, and drenched in cosmic vertigo. Sit with it.
