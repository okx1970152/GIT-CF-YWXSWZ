## Chapter Overview
Welcome back, fellow survivors. Just when you thought Li Huowang had the upper hand, Chapter 64 reminds us that in the Dao-Twisted World, you *never* have the upper hand for long. After his clever ambush and interrogation of Li Zhi the spirit-dancer, our boy is hit with a brutal reality check: these mediums don’t play by the same rules. What starts as a clean, strategic capture turns into a full-body nightmare when Li Zhi and his Er Shen swap identities, and a drum made of *human skin* starts beating in the dark. This chapter is a masterclass in folk-horror escalation—one of those moments where you realize the rules you thought you understood are just the first layer of a much deeper, bloodier game.

## Key Plot Points
Li Huowang’s ambush plays out exactly as planned. With the help of Gouwa, the Fool, Chun Xiaoman, and the group, he captures Li Zhi by targeting his spirit-dance drum, correctly deducing that without it, the medium can’t control his summoned entities. The interrogation doesn’t last long, though. Li Zhi reveals that his Er Shen (“wife”) has a will of its own and has already fled into the woods, abandoning him. More importantly, Li Zhi calls Li Huowang a “Heart-Element Immortal” for the first time, deepening the mystery of what makes him so valuable to supernatural beings.

But the moment Li Huowang tries to force answers by killing him, things go sideways. Li Zhi begins to transform into a beast-like hybrid—sprouting porcupine quills, growing snake scales under his skin. Even after Gouwa caves his skull in with a club, the nightmare isn’t over. A drumbeat echoes from the forest. The Er Shen has returned—wearing Li Zhi’s face. They *swapped*. And the new Li Zhi is using his own torn abdominal skin as a drum to power a faster, more desperate spirit-invocation.

The climax is pure visceral chaos: the Er Shen absorbs every weapon into its body, Li Huowang summons the Wandering Lord to counter the drum, and the chapter ends with the Er Shen sinking its teeth into Li Huowang’s shoulder from behind. A clean win dissolves into a messy, painful draw.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Er Shen (二神)** : The “Second Spirit” or assistant in the spirit-dance (*tiao dashen*) ritual. In Chinese folk mediumship (especially the Northeast *chuma* tradition), the lead spirit-dancer (*yi shen* or *tiao dashen de*) is the primary vessel, while the Er Shen plays a crucial support role—often silent, masked, or veiled. In this novel, the relationship between the two is deliberately blurred: they can swap bodies, and each may have their own hidden agenda. This chapter confirms that Li Zhi’s “wife” is less a spouse and more a supernatural hostage or partner with independent will.

**Heart-Element / Heart-Element Immortal (心素 / 心素仙)** : A novel-specific term that previous chapters have hinted at as a rare human constitution prized by alchemists and spirits. The Er Shen and Li Zhi both use this title for Li Huowang, suggesting that in the supernatural world, he is viewed not as a struggling human but as a walking treasure or divine anomaly. The term “immortal” here carries a chilling irony: being valuable to spirits means being preyed upon by them.

**Skin-drum (人皮鼓)** : A horrifying literalization of folk custom. While spirit-drums are traditionally made of wood and animal hide, the image of a medium using his own stretched flesh as a drumhead is pure Dao-Twisted World innovation. It underscores a key rule of this universe: **ritual power comes from the *self*—your body, your lifespan, your pain.** No shortcuts, no clean magic.

**Spirit-invocation chant (请神调)** : The rhythmic, rhyming text Li Zhi chants is a stylized version of real *shen diao* (god-tunes) used by folk mediums to summon spirits. The lyrics reference folk geography (sunset, door locks) and immortal hierarchy (Red, Yellow, Clear Wind, Sorrow King). The faster the chant, the more desperate the summoning—and the more dangerous for everyone nearby.

## Reading Guide
Heads up, first-timers—if you’re squeamish about body horror, this chapter is a *field test*. The skin-drum image is one of the most visceral in the entire book, and it’s meant to make you feel the *physical weight* of ritual. The horror here isn’t just “something scary appears”—it’s that the cost of supernatural power is always paid in your own meat.

Watch how Li Huowang’s intelligence is both a strength and a weakness. He correctly figures out the drum’s importance, but he treats this world’s rules as if they’re fixed and *knowable*—like a game mechanic. The skin-drum is the novel telling him (and us) that the rules can be rewritten by anyone desperate enough to tear their own belly open. This is a world where “fighting smarter” only works until the enemy decides to fight *harder* in a way you never imagined.

For returning readers, note how this chapter mirrors the Zhengde Temple arc. Back then, Li Huowang was trapped by an institution that hid its corruption behind piety. Now, he’s up against a man who *is* his own temple—his flesh is the altar, his pain is the incense. The enemy isn’t an organization anymore; it’s a living ritual. And he’s losing.
