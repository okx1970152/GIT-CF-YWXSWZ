## Chapter Overview
This chapter isn’t about a big fight with a flashy boss—it’s Li Huowang having a raw, uncomfortable reckoning with the squatters in his own head. Hongzhong pokes the hornet’s nest with that “tiger and cheng-ghost” analogy, and Li Huowang nearly takes himself out in a fit of nihilistic rage before his own parasite literally pulls him back from the edge. Then, just when he thinks he’s got a handle on things, he stumbles onto something far more unsettling: a group of Renxiao—those twisted, blind creatures born from miserable old age—sitting in morning devotion like they’re a proper Daoist sect. The familiar horror of Qingfeng Temple comes rushing back, and he has to choose between his need for violent catharsis and a more urgent mission: saving the children.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang seriously questions for the first time whether the four “hallucinations” (Hongzhong, the monk, Peng Longteng, Jin Shan Zhao) are really just products of his Heart-Element, or something else entirely.
- Hongzhong baits Li Huowang with the metaphor of the tiger and the *cheng* ghost, suggesting they are parasitic spirits living off him rather than mere illusions.
- After Hongzhong calls him a lunatic outright, Li Huowang nearly commits suicide by stabbing himself in the neck—stopped only by the monk’s pleas, Peng Longteng’s intervention, and his own Black Tai Sui tentacles physically restraining him.
- Li Huowang reflects on the unfamiliarity of his own extreme reaction, wondering if that violent impulse was truly his.
- The monk urges him to prioritize the kidnapped children over his personal rage, anchoring him back to a purpose beyond self-destruction.
- Li Huowang witnesses a group of Renxiao sitting in formation, led by one with a black horsetail whisk, chanting what appears to be a morning lesson—a grotesque parody of religious cultivation.
- He feels a visceral revulsion, recognizing the same delusional quest for immortality that drove Qingfeng Temple, but the monk’s reminder steers him back to the search for the babies just as a Renxiao messenger arrives.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter introduces the term **人魈 (Renxiao)**, a creature rooted in Chinese folk horror. In the Dao-Twisted World, a Renxiao is formed when a person lives in misery long enough to reach extreme old age—their body twists, their eyes go blind, their hair and nails overgrow like a shell. They speak only in “ghost tongue” and serve as horrifying guardians or lures. The name itself echoes the *shānxiāo* (山魈), a legendary mountain spirit or man-ape known for its strength and malice, but here it’s been grounded into something even more pathetic and disgusting.

The **伥鬼 (cheng ghost)** Hongzhong mentions comes from the classic idiom “为虎作伥” (to act as a jackal to the tiger). Folklore says that when a tiger devours a person, the victim’s soul becomes a *cheng* ghost trapped in the tiger’s belly, forced to lure other travelers to their doom. It’s a chilling metaphor for being a slave to a greater predator—which, if you think about it, makes the whole “are they hallucinations or not” debate even more unsettling.

Also worth noting: the **morning lesson (早课, zǎokè)** is a real practice in Daoist and Buddhist temples, where adherents gather at dawn to chant scriptures and perform devotions. Seeing deformed, blind creatures mimicking this ritual is a direct callback to Qingfeng Temple’s twisted “cultivation”—the horror of hollow forms repeating sacred motions without a shred of genuine enlightenment.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in psychological escalation. Pay close attention to the way Li Huowang’s self-destructive impulse is triggered—not by a monster, but by a simple insult from a being he suspects isn’t even real. His reaction feels *too* extreme, and that’s the point. The author is showing you how frayed his mental wiring has become. The fact that his own Black Tai Sui (a parasite) has to physically restrain him from killing himself is both darkly comic and deeply tragic.

The Renxiao morning lesson scene is the chapter’s true emotional gut-punch. It mirrors Qingfeng Temple so closely that Li Huowang almost loses control again, but the monk’s plea about the mothers anchors him. This is a crucial character beat: even at his most nihilistic, he *can* be pulled back by empathy for others’ suffering.

Also, keep an eye on that whisk-wielding Renxiao leader and the messenger that arrives at the end. This is the first hint that the Renxiao have structure, hierarchy, and possibly a purpose beyond random predation. That’s bad news for anyone underground.
