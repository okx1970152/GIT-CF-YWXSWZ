## Chapter Overview

Grab your nose plugs, folks, because this chapter of *Daomen* dives headfirst into the raw, pulsating viscera of the Dao-Twisted World’s most grotesque birthing scene yet. Our dear, perpetually put-upon Li Huowang just wants to be a good little agent and report some juicy sedition, but the world (and a very annoying, newly corporeal monk hallucination) has other plans. What starts as a moral debate about saving a life spirals into a nightmare of self-surgery, a human-faced calf with an old man’s head, and a cryptic nursery rhyme that feels less like a lullaby and more like a death warrant. And just when you think the horror is over, Zhuge Yuan—the ever-calm, ever-placid scholar—loses his cool in a way we’ve never seen before. If you thought the body horror was bad, just wait until the existential dread kicks in.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang tries to leave a desolate Great Qi cold palace, refusing to aid a pregnant eunuch he suspects is a trap. The monk hallucination, however, is granted corporeal form and insists on helping.
- The eunuch performs a crude self-C-section with a knife and scissors, pleading with the monk to “save the child” and not himself.
- The monk fails to deliver the creature on his own; Li Huowang reaches in and pulls it out—a grotesque hybrid with the body of a calf and the face of the old eunuch.
- The human-faced calf recovers immediately, recites a bizarre rhyming prophecy, and then dies.
- Li Huowang scolds the monk for his misplaced compassion. He then prepares to leave, but Zhuge Yuan stops him.
- For the first time, Zhuge Yuan appears deeply shaken and almost regretful, urgently asking Li Huowang to deliver messages to his old acquaintances in Great Qi.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **The Cold Palace (冷宫):** This isn’t just any abandoned building. “Cold Palace” refers to a secluded residence within the Forbidden City where disgraced imperial consorts were sent to live out their days in shame and isolation. It’s a place of political death, adding a layer of grim, historical weight to the location.
- **Saving a Life (救人一命):** The monk quotes the classic Buddhist/folk saying, “救人一命，胜造七级浮屠” (Saving one life is more meritorious than building a seven-story pagoda). The “浮屠” (pagoda) here is a translation of the Buddha’s stupa, a monument to enshrine relics. It’s a core tenet of Mahayana Buddhist merit-making, which the monk uses to guilt-trip Li Huowang.
- **The Rebel’s Ditty (造反歌谣):** The song chanted by the human-faced calf isn’t just random madness. It’s a classic trope of Chinese historical fiction: the “prophetic children’s rhyme” or cryptic omen. Lines like “Kill the cattle, drink the wine” and “Eat his mother’s, wear hers” are darkly reminiscent of the *Water Margin* (水浒传) and other classic tales of rebellion against corrupt dynasties. It functions as a political omen of an impending uprising within the Great Qi.
- **People as Signs:** The way the eunuch gave birth to a monster bearing his own face is a form of grotesque omen. In Chinese folklore, deformed births and strange natural phenomena were often interpreted as signs of Heaven’s displeasure with the ruling dynasty or a portent of social upheaval.

## Reading Guide

Alright, fellow Daoists, let’s breathe. That was a lot. This chapter is a masterclass in tonal whiplash. You go from a mundane, hardened “I don’t have time for this” attitude straight into the most messed-up OB/GYN scene this side of the Styx. Key thing to watch: Li Huowang’s emotional state. He’s not freaking out. He’s *tired* and *annoyed*. His “What a mess” and the disgusted fling of the calf show he’s been hardened by the world’s horrors, and his voice here is flat, contemptuous, and dangerously calm. The real horror is how *normal* this body horror feels to him now.

But then the rug gets pulled. The song from the calf isn’t just disturbing; it’s political. And Zhuge Yuan’s reaction? That’s the true escalation. Zhuge Yuan has been the stable, wise rock in a storm of insanity. Seeing him so shaken suggests a threat that isn’t just visceral but *structural*—something in the very fabric of the Great Qi that even he cannot face alone. This chapter isn’t just about a creepy birth; it’s a turning point where the personal horror pivots back to the cosmic-political plot, and Zhuge Yuan is about to ask for a big, desperate favor.
