## Chapter Overview

Alright, fellow readers, buckle up. Chapter 77, “Bashe,” is the kind of chapter that makes you put the book down, stare at the wall for ten minutes, and question every life choice that led you to reading such wonderfully cursed fiction. Yuan Er, the blind, screaming, family-just-murdered boss fight, pulls out a last-ditch move that isn't just a spell—it's an invitation to a cosmic horror party. And the bouncer? It’s a thing called Bashe. The result isn't a fight; it's a *perceptual apocalypse*. Li Huowang and his crew get their five senses blenderized into a single new sense, and through it, they glimpse something in the depths of the universe that makes the Deity of Joy look like a friendly little garden gnome. This isn’t a power-up or a secret weapon; it’s a brutal reminder that the Dao-Twisted World has layers of horror that make everything before look like a warm-up.

## Key Plot Points

Li Huowang seizes the moment of stability after Yuan Er’s botched summoning. He doesn't hesitate; he moves with cold, surgical precision, decapitating the blind Yuan Er before the man can recover. The death is immediate, the threat over, but the atmosphere is suffocating. The crew has seen what they have seen, and it has left them trembling with a deep, internal cold.

The story then shifts focus to the source of the horror: a red bamboo slip. Li Huowang, ever the pragmatist, forces himself to read it despite wanting to vomit. He discovers a manual for trading pain and flesh with an entity named Bashe. The methods are a progression of escalating self-mutilation and sacrifice, from popping out fingernails to ingesting rusty iron to sacrificing blood relatives. Yuan Er failed because his *spiritual* pain didn't match his physical pain—a chilling requirement that suggests the cultivators of this path must be masters of their own damnation.

This leads to a crucial deduction. Li Huowang realizes that Yuan Er wasn't trying to scare them; he was attempting a full summoning on his first try, a desperate, amateur's gamble. More importantly, Li Huowang now suspects the existence of a sect that worships not flesh, but pure, refined suffering. A sect that could *survive* the practices on this scroll. This thought terrifies him far more than the river bandits did.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter introduces what is arguably the most important story item in the entire novel: **the *Daqian Lu* (《大千录》)**, or the **Thousand Greats Record**. The red bamboo slip Li Huowang finds is the core artifact of an entire cultivation path. The name “Bashe” (巴虺) is a masterful piece of character naming that any reader of classical Chinese myth should find unsettling. The first character, “Ba” (巴), directly references Ba-Shu, an ancient region of Sichuan, long associated in folklore with strange creatures and venomous snakes. “She” (虺) is an ancient word for a venomous snake or a serpent-like dragon. So, his name literally means “The Serpent of Ba,” grounding him in a very specific, ancient, and poisonous regional mythos.

The path described on the slip is the **methodology of pain and suffering**. The *Thousand Greats Record* isn't a “dark magic” spellbook; it is a litany of sacrificial technologies designed to appease Bashe. Each entry is a specific price for a specific result. The logic isn't “this spell costs mana,” but “this act of supreme agony pleases my god.” This is a form of what we might call *body cultivation* taken to its most extreme and self-destructive conclusion, where ritual self-harm is the only form of prayer. Li Huowang's immediate speculation about a sect that can survive this is a perfectly logical deduction, and it points towards future horrors.

### Thousand Greats Record (《大千录》)

A sinister scripture that details methods of trading one's own flesh, blood, and spiritual agony for supernatural power. It is the foundational text of a suffering-cult.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterpiece of escalating dread. The opening is pure cosmic horror, where the author takes the concept of Lovecraftian *indescribability* and weaponizes it by having Li Huowang’s senses literally merge. The realization that he can *taste* a gaze or *smell* a melody is deeply, primally wrong. Pay close attention to the prose here—it’s deliberately disorienting to make you feel what Li Huowang feels.

The most important takeaway is the contrast between the *incomprehensible* horror of Bashe and the *personal*, *methodical* horror of the *Thousand Greats Record*. Li Huowang cannot fight Bashe. He cannot even *remember* what he saw of it. But he *can* read a scroll, think about its implications, and deduce the existence of a suffering-cult. This shift from cosmic terror to tactical deduction is classic Li Huowang. He doesn’t break; he processes.

Also, note the quiet character beat: Li Huowang doesn't celebrate killing Yuan Er. He's troubled by the mystery of the slip. He's already looking for the next threat. This paranoia and forward-thinking, born from trauma, is the only thing keeping him alive. He’s not a master planner; he’s a traumatized survivor whose brain has been rewired to always scan for the next abyss.
