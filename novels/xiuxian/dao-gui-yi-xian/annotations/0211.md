## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, fellow Daoists, because this chapter is a full-throttle, skin-melting, life-for-life brawl that doesn’t let up for a single second. Li Huowang, battered and cornered, faces the monstrous general Peng Longteng in a desperate duel at the border pass. After a grueling back-and-forth, he emerges victorious through sheer audacity and self-destructive cunning—but the victory costs him his skin, his humanity, and almost his mind. And just when he thinks it’s over, the headless corpse of Peng Longteng rises one last time. This is visceral, high-stakes horror action at its finest, blending body horror with a tragic final twist.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang’s attack on the convict-soldiers forces Peng Longteng to split her forces: half continue the siege on Bai Lingmiao, half turn to engage Li Huowang.
- Peng Longteng unleashes the “Army Shriek” (军啸), a terrifying blood-sacrifice battle cry that envelops all convict-soldiers in a killing haze.
- Li Huowang’s attempts to deploy the *Thousand Greats Record* are constantly interrupted by the relentless enemy.
- Bai Lingmiao and the others break through to provide a brief respite.
- Inspired by Shou San, Li Huowang tears open his own skin and physically stuffs the *Thousand Greats Record* under his flesh, permanently grafting it into his body.
- Li Huowang summons a massive, black-smoke wreathed fire, turning the battlefield into an inferno and burning the convict-soldiers into torches.
- In the climactic duel, Li Huowang trades a mortal blow to land a sword strike through the gap in Peng Longteng’s red-hot visor, decapitating her.
- The surviving convict-soldiers flee in terror as the blood-mist dissipates.
- Cao Hai, the loyal adjutant, attempts to finish off the mangled Li Huowang but is instantly killed.
- As Li Huowang stumbles away, hallucinating from pain and near death, Peng Longteng’s headless body rises and swings its halberd one final time.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
- **The Army Shriek (军啸)**: Peng Longteng’s ultimate technique, the *Army Shriek*, is a mass blood-sacrifice ability where every soldier bleeds from their seven apertures (七孔流血). The evaporated blood forms a mist that drives the troops into a berserker state. This is a classic military horror trope in Chinese fiction—a general’s will and cruelty made manifest through her soldiers’ very life force. The shriek itself is not a weapon strike but a howl, a sound that invokes primal fear and strips away reason.
- **Rice-Meat (米肉)**: The convict-soldiers’ term for human flesh, literally translating to “rice-meat.” This dehumanizing euphemism reveals their open cannibalism and marks them as entities beyond normal human morality. In xianxia and wuxia, such terms are often used by bandits or demonic cultivators to signal their complete abandonment of civilized norms.
- **Battering One’s Own Flesh (苦肉计)**: Li Huowang explicitly names his tactic after the classic Chinese stratagem “Battering One’s Own Flesh to Win the Enemy’s Trust,” one of the Thirty-Six Stratagems. In the original strategy, a general would have his own soldiers beaten to convince the enemy that internal dissent exists, leading to a fatal trap. Here, Li Huowang weaponizes his own suffering to draw the enemy into close combat, turning his wounds into bait.
- **The General’s Final Strike**: Peng Longteng’s headless body attacking Li Huowang after decapitation is a powerful echo of the *beheaded warrior still fighting* motif found throughout Chinese martial folklore. It signifies extreme killing intent and martial spirit that outlasts the body itself, a terrifying testament to Peng Longteng’s nature as a war-born monster.

## Reading Guide
Alright, dear readers, let’s pause and appreciate the absolute *madness* of this chapter. Li Huowang has officially crossed a threshold. He’s not just using the *Thousand Greats Record*; he’s *becoming* it. Stuffing a forbidden scripture under your own skin so you can access its power anytime? That’s a move straight out of a cosmic horror survival guide. The imagery of his skinless, blackened, shrunken body is unforgettable—and deliberately meant to make you uncomfortable.

Pay close attention to the contrast between Li Huowang’s pain and Peng Longteng’s joy. She *loves* fighting. She *loves* being burned alive. This is the first time we see a truly worthy martial opponent who doesn’t break or flee. Her death, while victorious for Li Huowang, feels almost tragic in its final moment—and Cao Hai’s grief-stricken “General…” is the emotional dagger.

Also, note the “Zhao Wu died again” echo: earlier in the story, a headless body kept fighting. Now we’re seeing it at a much higher scale. The horror is escalating, and the body count is climbing right along with it. Li Huowang’s final, broken cry for his mother (“Mom… can you blow on it for me?”) is the most human moment in this whole bloodbath. It reminds us that, under all that blackened flesh, there’s still a scared kid who just wants the pain to stop.
