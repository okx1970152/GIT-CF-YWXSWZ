## Chapter Overview

Get ready for a breather chapter that *feels* almost like a normal day—right up until the moment it doesn’t. After delivering a dead Daoist girl’s ashes back to her family, Li Huowang is running on autopilot, numb to grief because he’s seen way too much death. He even makes a casual new friend, a cheerful old monk who just wants to do good deeds. The simmering tension between Lü Zhuangyuan’s opera troupe and the local Nuo performers provides a bit of low-stakes drama. And for a few precious hours, Li Huowang gets to sleep without nightmares. But the Dao-Twisted World doesn’t give free passes. When his newly sharpened perception picks up a disturbance in the dark, a bizarre, spider-like shadow-puppet entity shows up at his window—and the Second Spirit’s urgent bite alerts him just in time.

## Key Plot Points

1. **Returning the Ashes**: The family of the Daoist girl receives her urn. Their grief is total and raw. Li Huowang watches but feels nothing—he’s become numb to death.
2. **The Old Monk Stays**: Li Huowang offers to travel together with the good-natured old monk, who cheerfully chants sutras over the urn. The monk insists he has his own money and doesn't need charity.
3. **Lü Zhuangyuan’s Pride**: A heated argument breaks out between Lü Zhuangyuan and the local Nuo opera troupe over which performance tradition is the true ancestor. Li Huowang wisely stays out of it.
4. **Bai Lingmiao Exhausted**: She falls asleep before Li Huowang can share his day. He lies awake in the dark, comforted briefly by a rare moment of stillness.
5. **Heightened Perception**: Li Huowang realizes he can now see in pitch darkness—a side effect of his growing supernatural abilities.
6. **A Bite in the Dark**: The Second Spirit bites his finger without warning. When he investigates, he finds three pairs of shoes by the bed—including a pair of red embroidered ones that slowly slide under it.
7. **The Spider-Puppet**: A strange shadow-puppet entity—spider-like with six hooked arms and an old man’s face—skitters across the wall outside. It speaks aloud, identifying Li Huowang as a Heart-Element with the Ao-Jing Sect and revealing its own hostile intent.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Nuo Opera vs. Traditional Opera**: Lü Zhuangyuan’s outburst isn’t just petty pride—it reflects real historical tension. Nuo opera (傩戏) is an ancient ritual performance born from exorcism ceremonies, where performers wear ferocious masks to drive away plague spirits. Han Chinese traditional opera, especially forms like Peking opera or regional folk opera, evolved from very different court and popular entertainment roots. The argument over “whose art is the ancestor” is a classic performer’s rivalry that carries centuries of cultural weight. In the Dao-Twisted World, this tension becomes even more ominous because Nuo is tied to supernatural practice—not just art.

**The Second Spirit’s Warning**: Bai Lingmiao’s Er Shen (二神) acts beyond her control. The bite and the whispered warning (“Something’s here”) show that the Second Spirit possesses its own awareness and can perceive threats the medium or Li Huowang cannot. In real-world chuma mediumship, the Er Shen is the assistant who sings and drums while the Lead Medium (the one possessed) dances. In this novel, that dynamic is inverted and made uncanny—the “assistant” may be more than a partner, and its silence hides a terrifying competence.

**Spider-Puppet Entity**: The description of a shadow-puppet-like creature with six hooked arms, an old man’s face, and “complex patterns” is pure folk-horror surrealism. Shadow puppetry (皮影戏) is itself a traditional Chinese performance art using translucent leather cutouts lit from behind. This entity weaponizes that familiar cultural form into a crawling, living predator—a classic Dao-Twisted World trick: taking a harmless cultural object and making it *move wrong*.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in *quiet dread before the storm*. After the relentless body horror and betrayal of recent chapters, the author lets you sink into a brief illusion of normalcy. Li Huowang even laughs, makes a friend, and sleeps soundly. But watch the small signs: his growing supernatural perception isn’t framed as a power-up; it’s a warning that his “human” senses are eroding. And that moment when he sees three pairs of shoes by the bed, and the red embroidered ones slide out of sight? Pure chill. The Second Spirit biting him—drawing blood from an ally—is a terrifying sign that the danger is already inside his safe room. Then the spider-puppet speaks, and the chapter ends on a frozen beat: “The moment he heard those words, Li Huowang gripped his sword, rolled off the bed, and scrambled to his feet.” The next move is yours to imagine—and it won’t be peaceful.
