## Chapter Overview
Can't take a single step in this Great Qi without something else trying to kill you, and this chapter proves it in the most infuriating way possible. Li Huowang is *this close* to the Shangjiguan Pass exit—the one lifeline out of this crumbling horror of a realm—when a new breed of monster slams the door in his face. We finally get a namedrop for a classic piece of Chinese folklore weaponized into absolute nightmare fuel: the Nian. Except this isn't the zodiac-friendly New Year monster you might know from children's books. This is the **Void Nian**, and it doesn't just want to eat you—it wants to eat your *remaining years*. Literally. Get ready for a chapter that's 50% desperation, 40% body horror, and 10% Zhuge Yuan being a very depressed tour guide through the apocalypse.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang finally gets an ETA on Shangjiguan Pass from Zhuge Yuan, who gives the most defeated "almost there" in literary history. The reality of what happened to Great Qi is visibly breaking him.
- A sentient human skin ambushes Li Huowang—then immediately gets killed mid-attack by something else hunting in the same area, which is *never* a good sign.
- The real threat reveals itself: a beast with a woman's porcelain face, a lion's body, and a chestplate bolted on with chains. She has no interest in talking; she just repeats a cryptic phrase like a broken record.
- Li Huowang's first attack is useless; the Void Nian phases right through the rift. Worse, any physical contact drains Lifespan at an alarming rate.
- Trapped, unable to retreat or advance, Li Huowang pulls out a *nasty* trick: he stabs himself with his own ribs to trigger a pain response, finally getting a reaction out of the creature.
- He uses his own skin as a weapon, flaying it off mid-battle and slapping it over the Nian's face, buying himself precious seconds.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The "Nian" (年) Monster in Chinese Folklore vs. This Chapter** — The traditional Nian beast was a legendary creature that emerged at the end of every year to terrorize villages. The folk remedy? Loud noises (firecrackers), the color red, and bright lights. It's the *original* basis for Chinese New Year customs. In this novel, however, the Nian is not a seasonal pest but a parasitic predator that directly feeds on "years" (yang life/lifespan). The "Void Nian" (虚年) specifically implies a corrupted, hollow version—one that exists outside the normal cycle. The folk-remedy of "scaring it away with noise and light" is useless here, which reinforces how thoroughly broken Great Qi's reality has become. The creature's name is also a dark pun in Chinese: 年 (year) sounds like 年 (beast in legend), and 虚年 (Void Year) can also vaguely evoke the concept of "stolen time."

**Human Skin as a Weapon** — This chapter features Li Huowang ripping off his own skin to use as a combat tool. While grotesque, this is not random. In Chinese folk horror, human skin (especially from a possessor or a powerful practitioner) can carry residual qi, curses, or spiritual weight. Using it to blind or "seal" a supernatural enemy follows a grim logic: a piece of a "lesser" monster's body can become a temporary binding tool against a "greater" one. It's desperate, but it has its own twisted rationality.

## Reading Guide
This is a "cornered animal" chapter, and it shows. Li Huowang isn't fighting smart—he's fighting *desperate*, and the narrative rewards that by having him resort to increasingly brutal self-mutilation to gain any advantage. Notice how the chapter plays with the tension of "almost there": the exit is close, but the closer he gets, the stronger the obstacles become. This is classic path-horror, and it works because we know that Great Qi is a piece of *dead* world being propped up by an angry god. Also, keep an eye on Zhuge Yuan's silence in this chapter. He's not being useless—he's *mourning*. His entire identity was built on being Great Qi's biggest fan, and now he's walking through its corpse. That quiet sadness is more devastating than any monster attack.
