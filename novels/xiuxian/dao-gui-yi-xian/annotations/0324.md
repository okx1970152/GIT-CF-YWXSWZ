## Chapter Overview

Fellow Daoists, if you thought the Renxiao were just creepy background scenery, this chapter will change your mind—fast. This is the grimmest, most claustrophobic underground battle we’ve seen in a while. Li Huowang finally reaches the children’s cavern, but the real horror isn’t the monsters under the bed. It’s the old people *willingly* betraying them. Yeah, you read that right. The Water Sons (水儿子) aren’t just deformed guardians—they’re part of a twisted, ritualized ecosystem where the elderly act as lookouts and informants for their own jailers. Li Huowang’s rage here is cold and surgical, not heroic. Every time he cuts off a piece of himself to fuel a weapon, you can feel the toll piling up. And then the head Renxiao starts *performing a Daoist ritual*—with candles, a skull incense burner, and a sword. If you thought this world couldn’t get any more blasphemous, think again.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang locates the children’s cavern, guarded by elderly villagers and several dozen infants with cinnabar forehead marks.
- The elderly guardians immediately betray him, calling out to the “Water Sons” (Renxiao) for help.
- Li Huowang kills three of the old people but realizes the damage is done—the Renxiao have been alerted.
- A Renxiao attack begins with black glutinous rice that dissolves an old woman’s flesh on contact.
- Li Huowang uses his invisibility to evade, but the elders keep revealing his position.
- A prolonged, brutal melee ensues in the cramped cave; Li Huowang uses his bronze-coin sword, self-mutilation, and the Black Tai Sui’s tentacles to thin the Renxiao ranks.
- The lead Renxiao begins a ritual with a skull incense burner, candles, and a black-talisman peach-wood sword.
- Li Huowang sacrifices two fingers to interrupt the ritual, but the Renxiao intercept his attack.
- The chapter ends as a circular, arcane formation begins spreading from beneath Li Huowang’s feet—the ritual is proceeding.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Water Sons (水儿子) & the “Eating the Heirless Inheritance” Tradition**  
The term “水儿子” (Water Son) is likely a local folk euphemism for the Renxiao—deformed, blind creatures created from people who lived miserably into extreme old age. The chapter uncomfortably mirrors the real Chinese folk practice known as “吃绝户” (eating the heirless inheritance), where a community would claim the property of a family with no male heir. Here, the inversion is horrifying: the elderly are not victims but active participants, feeding information to the monsters in exchange for survival.

**The Skull Incense Burner & Ritual Pacing (罡步)**  
The lead Renxiao using a human skull as an incense burner is a grotesque subversion of Daoist ritual tools. In orthodox practice, incense burners (香炉) are used for offerings to deities. Adding a skull as the vessel turns it into a desecration device. The “罡步” (Gang Step or Dipper Step) is a ritual pacing technique used in Daoist and folk exorcisms to align the practitioner with celestial forces. Having a blind, twisted monster perform it is a deliberate corruption of sacred motion—a visual signal that even the most sacred actions have been hollowed out.

**Black Glutinous Rice (黑糯米) as a Folk Weapon**  
Glutinous rice is a staple in Chinese cuisine, but in folk belief, black glutinous rice (or rice mixed with other substances) is sometimes used in exorcistic countermeasures. Here, it’s weaponized as a flesh-dissolving projectile, blending everyday folk material with lethal body horror. This aligns with the novel’s tendency to turn mundane ritual items into instruments of visceral terror.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in *why* Li Huowang is losing his grip on his own humanity—and why he doesn’t care anymore. The moment the old people start ratting him out to the “Water Sons,” you realize that in this world, no one is on your side. Not even the victims. The real punch isn’t the monster ritual; it’s that Li Huowang has been fighting for a group of people who are willing to doom twenty children just to stay alive a little longer. The pacing of the fight is deliberately claustrophobic—short, sharp swings between action and strategy, with the occult set piece of the Renxiao ritual looming at the end. Pay attention to how Li Huowang’s self-harm is now *routine*: cutting off nails and fingers are presented as tactical decisions, not emotional spirals. That’s the character arc right there. He’s becoming a survival machine, and the cost is every piece of himself he’s willing to throw away.

## Deeper Dive: The Corruption of Daoist Ritual Language

Let’s look at that final incantation: “喃灵那震光急急摄！” (Nán líng nà zhèn guāng jíjí shè!). This is a deliberately garbled version of a standard Daoist exorcism formula (“急急如律令”— *Zhou Zhou Ru Lü Ling*, “Urgent as the Law”). The words are mashed into near-nonsense, but the rhythm is intact. This is a **deliberate corruption of liturgical language**—a blind, unholy creature chanting a spell that sounds *almost* right but is fundamentally wrong. That cognitive dissonance—hearing the shape of holy words wrapped in grotesque flesh—is one of the novel’s most potent horror tools. It says: nothing is sacred, not even the syllables used to call upon gods.
