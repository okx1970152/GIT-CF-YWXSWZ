## Chapter Overview
Holy dragon poop, fellow Daoists—he actually did it! Gao Zhijian, the simpleton who couldn’t even read a full sentence a few arcs ago, just went through a full imperial enthronement ceremony and became the legitimate Emperor of Great Liang. And who was the silent guardian watching from the rooftops? Our favorite paranoid, flesh-eating heart-element disaster, Li Huowang. This chapter is all about ceremony, transition, and that bittersweet feeling of watching your brother ascend to a throne you know is cursed to the bone. It’s less about monster-mashing and more about the soul-crushing weight of political reality settling in. But don’t worry—there’s still plenty of body horror when the Dragon Vein decides to *merge* with the new emperor. Dragon claws? Skin splitting? Blood everywhere? Check, check, and check. It’s the Dao-Twisted World’s version of a peaceful power transfer.

## Key Plot Points
- The chapter opens with the full ritual protocol of the enthronement: Gao Zhijian kneels, prostrates, and offers sacrifices to Heaven, Earth, and the Gods of Soil and Grain under the eunuchs’ guided chanting. Li Huowang watches from a distant rooftop with Li Sui.
- Li Huowang’s internal conflict is front and center: he worries about how the throne will change his simple brother, remembering what happened to Ji Lin (the previous emperor who was twisted into something monstrous by the same process).
- The National Preceptor warns Li Huowang about the severity of the Dragon Vein matter—it’s tied to national fate, and any mistake will harm everyone under the sky.
- The core ritual takes place at the Imperial Ancestral Temple. The National Preceptor reads from a bizarre jade scroll that moves like living paper, and the Nine-Dragon Coffin emits dragon roars that transform into an overwhelming pressure.
- The Dragon Vein’s Mandate literally *erupts* from the coffin and slams into Gao Zhijian, causing his skin to split like dragon scales and blood to drench his robes. This is the physical price of rulership.
- Gao Zhijian transforms: dragon claws, dragon whiskers, dragon horns. He completes the fusion with the Dragon Vein, becoming the legitimate Emperor of Great Liang.
- Li Huowang thinks of Zhuge Yuan and the crying girl, finding some comfort that their sacrifices may now have meaning. But he’s also reminded that Zhuge Yuan lied to him—a bitterness that lingers even in this moment of success.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Enthronement Ritual (登基大典):** The chapter describes a highly structured ceremonial sequence: sacrifices to Heaven, Earth, and the Gods of Soil and Grain (社稷), followed by the Imperial Ancestral Temple. This mirrors the traditional Chinese imperial *fengshan* (封禅) ritual complex, where the emperor performs cosmic alignment to legitimize his rule. The “three kneels and nine prostrations” (三拜九叩) is the highest form of reverence in Confucian ritual, reserved for Heaven, Earth, and the emperor himself.

**The Dragon Vein (龙脉) and Mandate of Heaven (气运):** In Chinese political cosmology, the Dragon Vein is the geomantic energy channel that determines a dynasty’s legitimacy and vitality. The novel literalizes this concept: the Mandate of Heaven is not an abstract idea but a *stolen fragment of divine power* that holds up reality itself. It is a parasitic burden passed through blood sacrifice—the emperors in the coffin are proof of the price. Gao Zhijian’s skin splitting and transformation into dragon-like features echo the folkloric idea that the emperor is a “living dragon” (真龙天子), but here it’s viscerally horrific rather than majestic.

**The Jade Scroll (玉简):** A physical artifact of imperial authority, the jade scroll is described as having jade exterior and paper-thin interior—an impossible, magical object that moves like a phoenix or dragon. This is a xianxia corruption of the historical “jade slip” (玉策), used in solemn ritual proclamations. The golden characters appearing and disappearing among the flipping pages suggest the scripture itself is alive, echoing the Dao-Twisted World’s theme of text as a living, dangerous entity.

**The National Preceptor (国师):** A key figure in Chinese imperial bureaucracy, the National Preceptor is both a political advisor and a master of esoteric arts. His role here is to oversee the ritual that binds the new emperor to the Dragon Vein—a position that implies he possesses knowledge of forbidden arts far beyond ordinary court officials.

## Reading Guide
Okay, let’s talk about that moment when Gao Zhijian’s skin literally splits open. If you came into *Dao Gui Yi Xian* expecting a clean, dignified transfer of power, you came to the wrong novel. This is the Dao-Twisted World—rulership isn’t a privilege; it’s a curse. The Mandate of Heaven is a stolen fragment of a divine power, and it *hurts* to bear it. Ji Lin was twisted into a walking corpse by the same process. The horror here isn’t a monster attack—it’s the *institutionalized violence* of statecraft. Gao Zhijian gets dragon claws, sure. But he also gets a lifetime of having his blood and soul drained to prop up a dying world.

And Li Huowang’s reaction? *Chef’s kiss.* He’s not celebrating. He’s not even relieved. He thinks about Zhuge Yuan’s lie. He thinks about the crying girl. He just… watches. That’s the sign of a protagonist who has seen too much to trust happy endings. The only person who’s genuinely excited is the monk, who sees this as a ticket to bodhisattva-hood for Li Huowang. Classic monk move—turn someone else’s sacrifice into a divine piggy bank.

One more thing: the detail about the old emperor laughing mournfully in the coffin? “In the end, our Ji family line is broken.” That line hits hard when you remember who Ji Lin was and what he became. The novel doesn’t forget its dead—it lets them rot in plain sight, whispering their regrets through dragon coffins.

Get ready, fellow Daoists. The world just gained a new emperor. But we all know how this story treats emperors.
