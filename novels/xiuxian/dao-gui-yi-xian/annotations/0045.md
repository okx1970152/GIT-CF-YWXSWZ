## Chapter Overview

“The Buddha” is the chapter where the mask finally comes off Zhengde Temple. What looked like a bustling, pious Buddhist institution reveals its true face—or rather, its true *mountain of flesh*. Li Huowang, guided by the well-meaning old beggar-monk, takes a casual tour of the temple’s statuary yard, only to have his perception violently rewired. The stone Buddhas were never stone. They were living monks, pressed and writhing into grotesque mounds. And the tour ends with a view of something so massive it breaks the scale of horror entirely. Get ready, fellow Daoists. This is one of those chapters where you have to put the book down and stare at a wall for a few minutes.

## Key Plot Points

- Jian Dun delivers a final, cryptic warning to Li Huowang: don’t look down on Buddhism, because Daoism is the same. He leaves behind an uncomfortable truth about institutional corruption.
- Li Huowang’s health improves; the nightmares stop. He waits for the abbot to summon him for the Great Deliverance Feast.
- The old beggar-monk, now a cleaner and happier resident, drags Li Huowang on a tour of the temple’s sculpture yard.
- Li Huowang initially sees monks carving stone Buddha statues in two orderly rows under the sun. He cynically notes that the temple is probably selling these to pilgrims for profit.
- A sudden perceptual glitch—the sound of chisels changes to wet *smacking*—and Li Huowang’s vision snaps into horror: the “stone” statues are actually living monks, piled into quivering white masses.
- The second courtyard escalates: flesh-mountains now contain not just humans but animals—pigs, dogs, horses—being slowly swallowed by the writhing mass.
- The old monk, oblivious, pulls Li Huowang into an empty hall to show him the main attraction: an enormous Buddha statue overhead. Judging by the tone, this is not a statue made of bronze or wood.
- The chapter ends on the old monk’s awed exclamation. Li Huowang cannot speak.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Zhengde Temple (正德寺)**: This is a Buddhist temple in Xijing renowned for its efficacious bodhisattva and charity porridge. But as we now see, its true nature is a site of systematic corruption where sacred rituals are perverted into grotesque forms of worship. The “Flesh Buddha” is a core symbol of corrupted religion in this world—a pulsating, parasitic mass of flesh.

**Flesh Buddha (肉佛)**: The true form of the temple’s deity. It is not a statue or a painting. It is a living mountain of pink flesh, covered in organs and pus, fed by monks who merge their own bodies with it. Buddhist concepts here are twisted: “becoming a Buddha” refers to a monstrous, shapeless transformation driven by karma and belief, not enlightenment.

**Hermaphrodites (阴阳人)**: In the Dao-Twisted World, monks may be half-male, half-female. This blurring of traditional gender boundaries is another form of the temple’s corruption—nothing sacred remains intact.

**Perceptual Horror**: This chapter illustrates the novel’s signature technique of showing the reader something “normal” (a statue yard) and then flipping a switch to reveal the underlying truth (a flesh yard). The horror is not just in the image, but in the *delay* of perception—Li Huowang’s brain takes a moment to catch up.

## Reading Guide

This is a *perception-check* chapter. If you’re reading for plot, the key takeaway is: the monks of Zhengde Temple are not just corrupt—they are *biologically part of* their god. The Flesh Buddha is not a metaphor. If you’re reading for atmosphere, pay attention to how the horror builds: the casual tour, the cheerful guide, the innocent pride of the old monk. That contrast—between mundane comfort and absolute body horror—is what makes this chapter stick.

Also, note Jian Dun’s parting words. He says he was a Daoist before becoming a monk, and that “the Mysterious Gate is the same.” This is a deliberate echo of Danyangzi’s temple. The horror is not sect-specific. It is systemic.

For future chapters, keep an eye on Li Huowang’s mental state. His earlier visual glitch (the dizziness before the switch) hints that his “recovery” may not be as clean as he thinks. The Dao-Twisted World does not let people off easily.

Finally, the old monk. He sees none of this. He lives in the temple, eats well, wears clean robes, and is proud of his work. That is the most terrifying part of all.
