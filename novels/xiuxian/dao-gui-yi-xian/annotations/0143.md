## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, because Chapter 143 delivers one of the most raw, emotionally charged exorcism scenes yet. Bai Lingmiao takes center stage as she performs a *tiao dashen* ritual on a chained, grieving mother, while Li Huowang arrives at the tail end—and does something that cuts straight to the heart of why this novel hurts so good. This isn’t a chapter about banishing a demon. It’s about a woman who broke because she lost her son, and the line between “haunted” and “heartbroken” is the thinnest, cruelest thread. Our boy Li Huowang, carrying two swords now and a whole lot of weariness, delivers a verdict that will make you want to cheer and sob at the same time.

## Key Plot Points

- Bai Lingmiao performs an intense, full-spirit-invocation *tiao dashen* on a bound elderly woman, complete with rhythmic chants, purple incense shoved down the throat, and beast-like signs emerging from under her red veil (skull outlines, a tail). The ritual is precise, visceral, and viscerally *folk*.
- The old woman oscillates between hatred and maternal grief, begging for her son and choking her husband Zhao Qin. It’s clear she’s not possessed by some generic evil—something in her own soul is fractured.
- Just as Bai Lingmiao reaches a crucial point, Li Huowang calls out and interrupts the ritual. He arrives with a group of people, having finished his business down the mountain.
- Li Huowang examines the situation coldly, decides it’s a lost cause, and tells Bai Lingmiao to leave, refusing the fee.
- When Zhao Qin’s anger flares and his men surround them, Li Huowang draws his sword just an inch—and the sheer killing intent emanating from the blade turns everyone’s blood cold. Wang Chengxing recognizes it immediately: they’ve run into a real master.
- Bai Lingmiao defuses the tension, and Li Huowang starts to leave. But the old woman’s broken whisper—“Son… Mother has failed you…”—stops him dead.
- In a move that’s pure Li Huowang, he shoves Yang Xiaohai into the old woman’s arms and *gives her back her son*. Then he cuts her chains, not with magical flair, but with a furious, almost trembling humanity. He asks why there’s no window. He tells Zhao Qin that madness doesn’t erase pain.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is an absolute goldmine for folk-religion authenticity. The *Bang Bing Jue* (帮兵决) is a real class of spirit-summoning verses used by northeastern Chinese spirit mediums (*chuma*). The lines about “sending the spirit over mountains and ridges” and “if you still won’t leave, I’ll slap you” are direct, raw, and unglamorous—just how the old folk traditions really sound. The *tiao dashen* (跳大神) ritual is a full-body performance: the veiled medium acts as a vessel, the drum provides the heartbeat of the ceremony, and the spirit’s presence manifests as physical movements (a muzzle pushing the veil, a tail appearing) rather than just words. The “red veil” itself is a classic prop—it hides the medium’s face, allowing the possessing spirit to “wear” her body without the distraction of her human identity.

Li Huowang’s sword, which emits battlefield-grade killing intent when drawn an inch, is described in a way that strongly suggests it’s a *military blade* (兵家剑)—a weapon forged in war and soaked in slaughter. Such swords are spiritually dangerous; they can devour their wielder’s mind. But a one-inch draw that makes everyone’s blood boil is exactly how such a blade would feel to people who don’t have the constitution to wield it.

### tiao dashen (跳大神)

A spirit-medium performance in which a practitioner, often in trance, serves as a vessel for a possessing entity. The drum, veil, and prescribed steps are all part of the ritual's physical grammar.

### military blade (兵家剑)

A sword forged in war and soaked in killing intent (sha qi). An ordinary person cannot wield it safely; its aura alone can make blood run cold and minds feel the phantom of battle.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in using supernatural horror to create *emotional* horror. Notice how there’s no real “exorcism” here—Bai Lingmiao’s *tiao dashen* can detect the shape of the pain, but it can’t fix a broken heart. Li Huowang’s intervention isn’t about a new spell or a power-up; it’s about him recognizing a kindred spirit in that screaming, chained woman. He’s been that person—losing control, hurting people, being locked up. His outburst about windows and loneliness is more than empathy; it’s a mirror. This is the kind of storytelling that makes *Dao Gui Yi Xian* unforgettable: the monster is grief, and the exorcism is an act of unspeakable kindness.

## Deeper Mythological Dive: The Wounded Mother & The Folk Logic of Madness

There’s a powerful folk motif here that transcends any single culture: the mother who loses her child and becomes something *other*. In Chinese folk religion, unexpected death or loss can create a “hungry ghost” state—not always for the *dead*, but for the living who cannot let go. The old woman’s chains, the dark windowless room, the constant oscillation between rage and grief—these aren’t just horror props. They’re the physical *shape* of unresolved trauma, translated into the language of folk ritual. Li Huowang’s solution (giving her a child to hold, cutting her chains, letting her see a window) is shockingly simple, but it’s also deeply *true* to the novel’s worldview: sometimes the demon isn’t inside the person. Sometimes the person is just hurts too much to heal.
