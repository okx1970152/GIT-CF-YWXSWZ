## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang returns from a bloody suicide attempt with proof that Danyangzi is truly dead—but the victory rings hollow. The living part of his parasitic master is gone, yet the cost keeps climbing: he has lost an arm, his literacy is fading, and his already fragile body is now a patchwork of self-inflicted wounds. Worse, the Zuowandao are still out there. This chapter is a quiet, brutal inventory of what remains—and a cold calculation of how to make the next move when you’re running out of pieces on the board.

## Key Plot Points

The chapter opens with Li Huowang performing an improvised self-surgery in a bath tub, cutting into his own stomach to confirm Danyangzi has been fully neutralized by the Zuowandao. The Second Spirit helps him—another unsettling reminder of how much he now relies on entities beyond his control.
His deduction that the Zuowandao killed the *living* Danyangzi to toy with him gives his obsession a new, personal edge. He now has a clear target for vengeance, but the five tricksters are powerful and elusive. Li Huowang’s plan to expose them to the Ao-Jing Sect’s lower ranks, and to enlist Abbess Jingxin—whose son was also a Heart-Element destroyed by Zuowandao—shows a brain still fighting even when the body is giving out.
The scene where everyone watches him in silent fear is a subtle but crucial beat: Li Huowang has become something his people are afraid of. Gao Zhijian’s baffling survival (twice now) is noted, but Li Huowang doesn’t dwell on miracles. He has more pressing, bloody work ahead.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Self-Surgery Scene as Confirmation Ritual**  
In Chinese folk-medical and martial traditions, “opening the body” to find an internal cause (like a parasitic worm or a “poison core”) is a recurring trope—almost always carried out by a third party, not by the patient themselves. Li Huowang doing it solo, with nothing but a spike and a cloth, underlines his absolute isolation. There is no healer here; only himself, his tools, and the alien entity (the Second Spirit) helping from behind. It is a brutal inversion of the orderly, ritualized surgery scenes found in medical classics like the *Huangdi Neijing*—here, the body is not a temple to be healed, but a cage to be searched.

**The Fading Literacy Metaphor**  
Li Huowang’s loss of literacy is explicitly tied to Danyangzi’s lingering influence—not a physical debuff but an erosion of self. In a world where written texts (*Heavenly Scripture*, *True Sutra of the Fire Vestments*) hold real power, losing the ability to read is doubly crippling: it cuts him off from knowledge and from his own identity. This is a characteristically *Dao Gui Yi Xian* touch—spiritual corruption manifests not as a glowing aura but as something as mundane and terrifying as forgetting how to read.

**The “Five Zuowandao” as Unresolved Threat**  
Li Huowang’s plan to ally with the Ao-Jing Sect’s lower ranks and enlist Abbess Jingxin is a classic *jianghu* (martial rivers and lakes) strategy: turn the deceived against the deceiver. The mention that Jingxin’s son was also a Heart-Element victim ties back to the novel’s core horror—this condition is a curse that destroys everyone it touches, including those with the highest authority. The Abbess is not just an ally; she is a mirror of what Li Huowang could become.

### Two-faced woman

A spirit with identical faces on both sides of her head; no back of the skull. She feeds on fear and is vulnerable to Daoist bells and yang substances.

### Erbing

A Zuowandao trickster who wears a copper coin mask; she uses illusion, dice talismans, and domino-tile rituals as weapons, and treats murder as performance.

### Three Corpses (San Shi)

A core Daoist concept of three internal parasitic demons located in the head, chest, and belly; they represent base instincts that must be 'cut off' for immortality. Peng Zhi is the demon of suspicion in the head.

## Reading Guide

Get ready for a *silent* chapter—no big fights, no revelations, just a man alone with his wounds and his hate. The meat here is all internal: Li Huowang is surviving, but readers need to watch how his survival instinct is reshaping him. He’s not a hero anymore; he’s a tactician who will use anyone and anything (the Second Spirit’s hands, the Abbess’s grief, the Ao-Jing followers’ anger) as pieces on a board. The chapter ends on a haunting note: Gouwa’s whispered observation that Li Huowang is starting to look like Baldy Tou. That’s the real horror here—not the scars, not the body count, but the slow, quiet erosion of the man himself.

## The Erosion of Self: Li Huowang’s Shifting Voice

This chapter deserves a special note on Li Huowang’s internal monologue. He is no longer just paranoid or calculating; there is a flat, professional coldness to his thoughts. When he thanks the Second Spirit, it is curt and functional. When he dismisses the monk’s moralizing, it is with a sharp “Can you just shut up?” When he lies to his group about why he’s returning to Siqi, he does so without hesitation or guilt. The old Li Huowang—the one who doubted, who agonized, who *felt*—is being replaced by a predator who only cares about the next move. That is the most terrifying transformation of all.
