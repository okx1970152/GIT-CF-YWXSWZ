## Chapter Overview
This chapter is Li Huowang’s final pre-departure housekeeping—a quiet, pragmatic scene where he distributes tools, assigns responsibility, and lays the cornerstones for Niuxin Village’s survival in his absence. There’s no monster, no ritual, and no combat. Instead, the real drama unfolds in the tense silences and small gestures between people who have survived unspeakable things together. It’s the *after* of horror: the cold, sober work of making sure the community doesn’t collapse the moment the anchor leaves. Get ready for a chapter where the real treasure isn’t a new artifact—it’s the trust Li Huowang is forced to build and the terrifying self-awareness of being the one everyone leans on.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang observes Li Sui’s new body, pieced together from Mantou’s corpse, with a mix of paternal warmth and visceral unease. His internal reaction is raw: *she’s horrifying, but she’s still my daughter*.
- He calls a meeting with Gao Zhijian, Chun Xiaoman, Zhao Wu, Gouwa, and the uninvited Yang Xiao Hai.
- Updates on the militia are glum: villagers are mostly disabled or frail. Only two passable recruits have been found.
- Li Huowang orders Zhao Wu to buy qualified private slaves—loyalty over cost—to speed up the militia formation, framing it as a self-defense measure in case he’s absent during another Heavenly Calamity.
- He warns Chun Xiaoman to be discreet with the White Lotus techniques, noting that the Supervisory Heavenly Office can easily identify them.
- Gouwa receives the rotten wooden *ruyi* artifact (a Heart-Element-tongue item gifted by the Chief Recorder) and a transcription of alchemy formulas, including medical talismans.
- Li Huowang bluntly raises the possibility of his own death or insanity, shocking the group into silence.
- In private with Gao Zhijian, he gifts the human-skin artifact (phylactery) and receives a blood-palm oath in return—a silent acknowledgment of brotherhood.
- Alone at last, Li Huowang uses his Heart-Element ability to erase his written concern about the recurring Heavenly Calamities, directly altering reality.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Private Slaves (私奴)**: These are not the chattel slaves of a Western plantation economy. In historical Chinese society, a *sinu* was bonded to a specific household or land, often through debt or poverty, and retained certain legal protections—the right to petition for freedom, to marry, and to own personal property. Li Huowang’s instruction to “buy loyal ones” reflects the pragmatic, survivalist logic of the Dao-Twisted World: he’s not constructing an evil empire; he’s trying to buy trustable bodies to form a last-ditch militia. For xianxia readers, this is a known trope: the protagonist building a power base by converting hired laborers into loyal retainers.

**The Human-Skin Artifact (皮人)**: This palm-sized doll, made from the cultivator’s own skin and mercury, functions as a phylactery—a life-backup. If the cultivator is decapitated, the *piren* absorbs the fatal damage, allowing survival. The cost? It takes massive amounts of time to craft, and its creation is a grotesque form of self-mutilation. Gao Zhijian’s shocked refusal tells you everything about its value. Xiao Bai readers should note: this is the Dao-Twisted World’s version of a “second life” item, but one earned through personal flesh, not a shop purchase.

**The Rotten Wooden Ruyi (朽木如意)**: The *ruyi* (如意) is a traditional Chinese talisman/scepter symbolizing “as you wish.” But this one has been corrupted: its core is a Heart-Element’s tongue, crafted by a eunuch from the Supervisory Heavenly Office. Its power is *bewilderment*—it fills a target’s mind with confusion and doubt. It’s a psychological weapon, not a physical one, perfectly suited to Gouwa’s trickster style.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is proof that *Dao Gui Yi Xian* doesn’t need a battle scene to be tense. The real horror here is *Li Huowang facing his own mortality*. He hands out artifacts and alchemy like a dying general giving final orders, even though he’s still standing. The moment where he explicitly says “What if I die?” is the red line: it forces everyone, including the reader, to acknowledge that Li Huowang is not an invincible main character. He’s a man running on borrowed time and stolen years.

Pay attention to Gao Zhijian’s blood oath—this is a rare moment of sincerity between two warriors who don’t do speeches. The silent nod, the shared cut, the pressed palm: in jianghu language, that’s a vow stronger than any contract. It also deepens Gao Zhijian’s mystery—Li Huowang flat-out says he’s “not simple,” and Gao’s refusal to go home just confirms it. He’s a man with a past too heavy to revisit.

And finally, the ending. Li Huowang erasing the written trouble—*Heavenly Calamities may recur; how to protect Niuxin Village?*—is a quiet, terrifying flex of his Heart-Element power. He doesn’t solve the problem; he *edits the problem out of existence*. That’s not a normal ability. That’s reality-bending through self-deception, and it raises the question: if he can delete his worries, what else might he accidentally delete?
