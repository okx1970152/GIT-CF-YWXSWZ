## Chapter Overview

The throne room of Great Liang is not what our scrappy survivors expected. Gao Zhijian—the simple, stuttering brute who once struggled to string two words together—sits on a solid gold dragon throne, robed in imperial yellow. But the reunion is anything but triumphant. Chun Xiaoman charges in for answers, Gouwa nearly loses his head to the eunuchs’ glares, and the old gang quickly learns that “emperor” isn’t just a title—it’s a gilded cage. Underneath the spectacle of power, something is terribly wrong. Li Huowang is “not fit to be seen,” and Bai Lingmiao, once a trembling girl, now moves with a calm that suggests she has seen what lies behind the curtain—and is not the same.

## Key Plot Points

- Gao Zhijian meets his old comrades on the dragon throne, his speech halting but healed by palace medicine. His crown slips when he scratches his head—a humble gesture that jars against the opulence.
- Gouwa’s joke about swapping places is met with terrifying hostility from the eunuchs; Gao Zhijian shuts it down with grim sincerity: “Being emperor isn’t a good thing.”
- Chun Xiaoman demands to see Li Huowang and Bai Lingmiao; Gao Zhijian refuses for Li Huowang, citing an unspeakable condition, but agrees to take her to Bai Lingmiao.
- Zhao Wu asks for nothing, Yang Xiaohai asks to find his parents—the old eunuch can help if he provides a birth date and hour, but the boy doesn’t know it. The eunuch reads his bones instead.
- Chun Xiaoman finds Bai Lingmiao in the imperial kitchen, stirring bird’s nest porridge. Bai Lingmiao is oddly serene, insists Li Huowang’s illness is improving, and refuses to let Chun Xiaoman see him.
- A heavy, unspoken dread lingers over the reunion—every answered question leads to a closed door.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Bird’s Nest Porridge (燕窝粥)**: In traditional Chinese medicine and cuisine, swiftlet nests are prized for nourishing the lungs, enhancing complexion, and restoring vitality. Here it is served in the imperial kitchen, reinforcing Bai Lingmiao’s role as caretaker—but also introducing a subtle unease: nests are homes, hollowed and consumed.  

**Birth Date & Hour (生辰八字)**: The Eight Characters—year, month, day, and hour of birth, each represented by a Heavenly Stem and Earthly Branch—form the foundation of Chinese astrology and fortune-telling. The old eunuch’s request is standard, but the bone-reading alternative suggests the imperial palace has deeper, more invasive ways to track a person’s fate.  

**The Dragon Throne & True Dragon**: “True Dragon Son of Heaven” is the classical title for the emperor, linking his legitimacy to the cosmic Dragon Veins. Gao Zhijian’s reluctance to share the throne hints that the Mandate of Heaven in this world is not a prize but a curse—a parasitic burden that feeds on the ruler as much as he feeds on it.

### Bird's nest porridge (燕窝粥)

### Mandate of Heaven (气运)

In this novel, a stolen fragment of divine power that holds up reality; the imperial family must sacrifice itself to maintain it.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in **unsettling normalcy**. The imperial palace should be a symbol of ultimate worldly power, but the Dao-Twisted World turns every institution into a mask. Gao Zhijian’s simple grin, the eunuchs’ razor-sharp deference, Bai Lingmiao’s patient serenity—every character is acting *normal* on the surface, and that’s what makes it so wrong. The true horror here is the invisible wall: Li Huowang is somewhere in the palace, “not fit to be seen,” and the reader is left to imagine what could be worse than the last time he peeled his own skin. The bird’s nest porridge, the missing birth date, the bone-reading—all hints that this reunion is being orchestrated by forces the old gang has only begun to understand.

## Deeper Lore: The Emperor’s Burden

In traditional Chinese political cosmology, the Son of Heaven holds the Mandate of Heaven by virtue of virtue—the emperor must be worthy, or heaven withdraws its favor and chaos follows. But in the Dao-Twisted World, that Mandate is stolen power, a fragment of divine authority that holds reality together at the cost of the ruling family’s self-destruction. Gao Zhijian’s muttered “not a good thing” isn’t modesty; it’s a warning. He now knows the throne is not a victory but a sentence.
