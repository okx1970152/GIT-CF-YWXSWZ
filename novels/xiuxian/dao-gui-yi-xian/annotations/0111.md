## Chapter Overview
Welp, folks, the Anci Nunnery exorcism didn’t exactly go to plan. Chapter 111 opens with Li Huowang licking his wounds—literally—after the nuns’ best attempt to peel Danyangzi out of him only managed to wound the bastard temporarily. The clock is ticking: two months until Li Huowang becomes Danyangzi completely. But instead of despair, our favorite walking disaster does something utterly heartbreaking: he slips into a hospital hallucination, has a perfectly normal conversation with his mom, gives her gold and jade to pay the family’s debts… and then coldly reminds himself it’s all fake. The chapter ends with Li Huowang’s quiet, lethal decision: no more waiting. Time to kill Danyangzi.

## Key Plot Points
- Jingxin explains the grim prognosis: Danyangzi’s roots are so entwined with Li Huowang that killing the master would require killing the disciple. They are, in effect, *sharing* a body, which is why the Black Tai Sui couldn’t be removed.
- Li Huowang requests a temporary loan from the nunnery and triggers a voluntary shift back to the hospital reality.
- In the hospital, Sun Xiaoqin is exhausted and unkempt, revealing the financial strain caused by Li Huowang’s illness. She is overjoyed when he speaks coherently.
- Li Huowang produces a string of gold prayer beads and a jade pendant from his hallucination—objects that “cross over” with him—and gives them to his mother, asking her to pay off debts and buy back their old house.
- When he mutters that he knows she is just a hallucination, Sun Xiaoqin panics, desperately insisting she is real. Li Huowang soothes her with a lie.
- He surreptitiously picks a red button from her clothes as a “souvenir” and shifts back.
- Back in the dark temple room, he reaches for the button—and finds nothing. The grim realization hits him coldly: the hospital was a fantasy.
- Li Huowang’s final resolution: **“I want Danyangzi dead.”**

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Three Corpses (三尸 / San Shi):** In Daoist internal alchemy, the Three Corpses are malevolent, parasitic spirits that reside in the human body, feeding on its vitality and reporting one’s sins to the heavens. They are a classic obstacle to immortality; practitioners seek to “kill” or expel them through meditation, diet, and ritual. In the Dao-Twisted World, this concept is literalized and inverted: Danyangzi is turning Li Huowang into one of his own Three Corpses—a flesh-and-blood parasitic extension of the master, not a spirit to be purged but an identity to be overwritten. Grotesque, right?

**Anci Nunnery (安慈庵) vs. Zhengde Temple:** The novel uses two flavors of corrupted Buddhism. Zhengde Temple is *polished* corruption—grand, secretive, and ritualistically perverse. Anci Nunnery is *decayed* corruption: dilapidated, openly vulgar, and indifferent to piety. Jingxin is still capable of helping, but she admits her limits—flesh-body horror is the monks’ specialty. This sets up a spiritual “division of labor” in the broken religious landscape of the Dao-Twisted World.

**Objects Crossing Realms:** The gold beads and jade pendant that Li Huowang passes to his mother are a recurring supernatural phenomenon—objects from the hallucinatory world manifesting physically in the hospital reality. But the red button he takes *from* his mother’s clothes *returns* to nothing, confirming that the hospital was *not* a crossover point but a closed loop inside his own mind. This is the novel using material proof as a knife to cut his last hope.

## Reading Guide
Get ready, fellow Daoists, because Chapter 111 is a punch to the gut disguised as a quiet, dimly lit room. The emotional core here is brutal: Li Huowang does something genuinely kind for his mother, and then immediately tells himself it was all fake—yet he still apologizes to the empty air when he comes back. The red button trick is *devastating*. The novel’s horror weaponizes love: it’s not the monster that breaks him, it’s the realization that the person he loves most in the world might not even be real. And yet—and *this* is why we keep reading—he doesn’t collapse. He makes a choice. The line *“I want Danyangzi dead”* isn’t a flashy battle cry; it’s the quiet, sharp sound of a man who has just lost everything and decided to make someone pay for it. There are no magic shortcuts or sudden power-ups here—just two months, a cursed body, and a plan that will almost certainly hurt.

If you’re new to the genre’s obsession with mental unwellness as a narrative engine, note how Li Huowang uses his hallucination as a *functional tool*—he asks his mother for news about the family’s finances, he gives away objects, he even steals a “proof” to test later. His madness is logical, systematic, and heartbreakingly practical. That’s the unique flavor of *Dao Gui Yi Xian*: a protagonist who wields his psychosis like a scalpel while bleeding out.
