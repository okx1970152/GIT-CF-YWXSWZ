## Chapter Overview

This chapter pulls back from the monster-fighting, sect-chasing action to give us something far more chilling: the quiet, internal disintegration of Bai Lingmiao. We watch her kneel before a false idol, sob out her impossible dilemma, and get verbally eviscerated by her own Second Spirit. There are no explosions, no flailing tentacles—just a young woman trapped between love and guilt, slowly realizing that even the gods won't answer her. And if you think that's less scary than a Flesh Buddha, think again.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang notes Bai Lingmiao's deteriorating eyesight and added lifespan issues, resolving to exchange his Office merits for remedies found with Liu Zongyuan at the capital's Supervisory Heavenly Office.
- Li Sui, sensing her mother's distress, follows the scent to the White Lotus ancestral hall, where Bai Lingmiao is praying and crying alone.
- Bai Lingmiao confesses her torment: her dead parents curse her in dreams for loving a man who slaughtered their family, but she cannot bear to leave him, and her indentured Immortal prevents her from escaping through death.
- Bai Lingmiao prays to Wusheng Laomu for guidance—but receives only silence, and the cold, mocking truth from her own Second Spirit.
- The Second Spirit strips away Bai Lingmiao's illusions: she is either a coward who can't choose revenge or acceptance, a fool who gives everything for scraps of affection, and a weakling who begs ghosts instead of acting herself.
- Bai Lingmiao is left with nothing but her own broken refrain: "I just wanted to live a lifetime with the person I love... Is that wrong of me?"

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The **white lotus** (白莲) is a loaded symbol in Chinese religious history. In the novel, Bai Lingmiao's family worship Wusheng Laomu (无生老母, the 'Unborn Venerable Mother'), a savior figure central to lay Buddhist-sectarian traditions that emerged during the Ming and Qing dynasties. Historically, these groups often promised a "Native Land of True Emptiness" (真空家乡) where believers would return, freed from suffering.

Yet the novel brutally inverts this promise: Bai Lingmiao's prayer is met with dead air. The twin lotus (并蒂莲) on the wall, a symbol of inseparable love in classical Chinese poetry, becomes a cruel visual pun—her love is real, but the deity she prays to is either nonexistent or indifferent. The Second Spirit's scathing monologue draws on *jianghu* (绿林) outlaw logic and plain folk wisdom, mirroring the harsh "tough love" speeches common in wuxia tradition, where a mentor forces a protagonist to see their own weakness. This is Bai Lingmiao's lowest point: a character who was once a soft, gentle anchor now shattered by the very faith and family that should have sustained her.

### jianghu (绿林)

Literally 'green forest,' a term for the outlaw or martial-arts underworld culture in Chinese fiction, with its own codes of loyalty, revenge, and honor.

## Reading Guide

Hold on, fellow Daoist—this is a **character chapter**, and it *hurts*. If you came here for Li Huowang scheming or a beast horde, you'll be disappointed. But if you want to understand why Bai Lingmiao is more than "the soft girl who follows the MC," this is where the layers peel. The Second Spirit's verbal beatdown is one of the most brutally honest exchanges in the entire novel so far—no magic, no monsters, just a woman being told the hard truth by herself. Pay attention to the symmetry: Bai Lingmiao prays to an absent mother goddess, while her own "mother" (the Second Spirit) is right there, screaming the truth at her. Sometimes the answer isn't from heaven. It's from the monster you've been hiding inside.
