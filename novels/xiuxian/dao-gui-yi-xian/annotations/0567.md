## Chapter Overview

Alright, buckle up, fellow Daoists. This chapter hits *hard*. After the chaos of the palace coup and the Emperor’s transformation, Li Huowang finally confronts the phantom of Zhuge Yuan—the one person he trusted absolutely. And that trust shatters. The conversation is raw, brutal, and cathartic. Zhuge Yuan admits he never fully trusted Li Huowang because he couldn’t gamble the lives of Great Qi on a single unstable variable. It’s a devastating confession that reframes their entire brotherhood. But even as Li Huowang rages, Zhuge Yuan offers one final gift: a method to dispel the phantoms haunting him. Then, with a scholar’s bow, he fades into nothing. Outside, the capital descends into curfew and chaos as lightning splits the palace sky and the Supervisory Heavenly Office hunts for answers. It’s an emotional gut-punch layered with cosmic horror and political fallout.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang confronts the phantom Zhuge Yuan, accusing him of never trusting him and always treating him as a Zuowandao threat.
- Zhuge Yuan admits he withheld the truth to protect Great Qi, but acknowledges his wrongdoing and offers to atone with his own death.
- Zhuge Yuan explains how to dispel Li Huowang’s phantoms: help them resolve their obsessions, and they will naturally disappear.
- He warns Li Huowang to set aside the cultivation technique Doumu Taiyin gave him until he understands its true purpose.
- Zhuge Yuan bows deeply and vanishes completely, leaving Li Huowang screaming, crying, and then falling into hollow silence.
- The spine-sword’s talisman is re-applied; when Li Huowang removes it, the sword no longer responds—it has “died” along with Zhuge Yuan’s phantom.
- The Supervisory Heavenly Office bursts in, demanding answers about the State Preceptor, the Emperor’s transformation, and a murdered spy.
- Li Huowang ignores them, grabs the sword, and tears open a spatial fissure to escape.
- Outside the palace, purple lightning strikes, and the State Preceptor’s office declares a city-wide curfew.
- Gao Zhijian, hiding in an inn, watches the panic and wonders if this is connected to Li Huowang.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

Zhuge Yuan’s final bow is a **deep formal bow (作揖, zuòyī)** —a classical Confucian gesture of respect, apology, and farewell. In traditional Chinese etiquette, a deep bow with clasped hands was the ultimate expression of sincerity, especially between equals or friends. For Zhuge Yuan, a self-proclaimed “gentleman” (君子), this gesture is the most fitting final act: he is apologizing, honoring their brotherhood, and taking his leave with all the gravity of a man who believes in propriety even at the moment of death. It’s a stark contrast to the grotesque, screaming world around him. Also worth noting: the concept of **obsession (执念, zhíniàn)** is a key driver of supernatural phenomena in Chinese folk horror and xianxia. A lingering obsession—unfinished business, a grudge, a promise—is what anchors a spirit or phantom to the material world. Zhuge Yuan’s advice to Li Huowang is literally operationalizing this folk belief: resolve the phantom’s obsession, and the phantom dissolves.

This chapter also deepens the novel’s signature twist on the **Mandate of Heaven (气运)**. Zhuge Yuan’s burden—choosing the safety of an entire nation over a single brother—frames rulership not as a celestial reward but as a crushing, transactional obligation. The throne is a parasite, and the one who sits on it pays in flesh, trust, and conscience.

## Reading Guide

This is the farewell arc for one of the novel’s most beloved characters. Zhuge Yuan has been Li Huowang’s moral compass, his strategic mind, and his most reliable ally in a world of lies. His confession hurts *because* it’s true: he *did* see Li Huowang as a potential Zuowandao threat, and he *did* hold back. But he doesn’t justify it—he accepts the blame and offers his life. That’s the tragedy. He’s not a villain; he’s a man caught between love for a brother and duty to a nation. For the reader, this chapter asks a painful question: can you ever fully trust someone who has already proven they will put the greater good above you? And if you can’t, what does that do to your sense of self? Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s final silence. He’s not just grieving; he’s realizing that the one person who helped him tell “real” from “fake” is gone. From now on, he’s navigating the Dao-Twisted World without his anchor.
