## Chapter Overview
This chapter is a short but emotionally packed breather—a transition that lets both the protagonist and the reader catch their breath after the inferno of the previous chapter. Li Huowang crawls out of a fissure, barely held together by his own guts and his daughter's tentacles, only to find himself back in a perfectly peaceful Great Liang. The whiplash is dizzying: one world is burning, the other hasn't even noticed. But while Li Huowang rushes headlong back into the fray (because the dead world of Great Qi won't stay stable forever), another, quieter plot thread begins to unspool. Gao Zhijian—sturdy, silent, and haunted by fractured memories of being a king—takes a different road. This chapter plants a seed for a major side story, and it does so with the understated dread of a man who doesn't know if his own brain is lying to him.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang emerges from the Great Qi fissure into the forests of Great Liang, severely wounded and held together by Li Sui's tentacles. The contrast between the two worlds is stark: Great Liang is utterly untouched by Great Qi's cataclysm.
- Li Huowang refuses to rest, intent on returning to Great Qi to find the Shangji Pass before that world collapses entirely. He uses Li Sui's tentacles to temporarily patch his body, a visceral cost-saving measure.
- A brief, mundane interlude: a brother and sister bickering in a borrowed carriage pass through the area, completely oblivious to the cosmic horror that just passed through.
- Gao Zhijian is on his way to the capital. His internal monologue reveals a desperate, fragmented plan: he believes he might be the emperor—or at least a general—and intends to use that authority to find the missing Gouwa and help Li Huowang.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Emperor's Mandate & Memory**: Gao Zhijian's belief that he might be the emperor touches on one of the most potent ideas in Chinese political cosmology—the *Mandate of Heaven* (天命). The idea that a ruler's legitimacy comes from heaven means that an emperor isn't just a political figure; he's a cosmic pivot point. If Gao Zhijian really *was* the emperor, the implications are staggering, not just politically but ontologically. His fragmented memories touch on a xianxia trope of "reincarnation of a great power" or "forgotten destiny," but the novel subverts it by making him question the very reality of those memories. A man who might be a god-king isn't striding into power—he's nervously wondering if the palace will even let him in.

**The Vast Gap Between Two Worlds**: This chapter visually demonstrates the novel's *dual-world cosmology*. Great Qi is a blasted, post-calamity hellscape. Great Liang is a serene, pastoral land bathed in sunset. They are geographically adjacent but ontologically separated. This isn't just a setting detail—it's a statement about the unreliable nature of reality in this universe. Two places can be right next to each other and exist in completely different states of decay and madness. The "border" isn't a wall; it's a fundamental rule of the universe that's broken.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in using a "bridge" scene to build character and tension. The plot doesn't advance much, but we get two crucial things: first, a hammer-blow reminder of Li Huowang's relentless, almost self-destructive drive. He could rest. He *should* rest. But the memory of a world dying pushes him forward. Second, we get a complete perspective shift into Gao Zhijian's head. He's been the quiet, loyal sidekick for so long, and now we see that his mind is a battlefield of its own. His decision to chase the emperor-memory isn't ambition—it's a desperate act of friendship. Keep an eye on him. The "mute" who might be a king is a thread with a lot of potential to fray beautifully.
