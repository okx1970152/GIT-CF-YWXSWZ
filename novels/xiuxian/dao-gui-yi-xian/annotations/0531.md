## Chapter Overview

Welcome back, fellow survivors of the Dao-Twisted World! If you thought the fight with the Wealth Gods was the climax, you were dead wrong. Li Huowang has just made a discovery that turns a personal brawl into a full-blown political time bomb: the Ren King, a contender for the Great Liang throne, is in bed with the Fa Sect—the same cult that worships the Child-God Yú’er Shén, a literal Heavenly Calamity. After a frantic chase through the ruins of Great Qi, our protagonist makes the cold, tactical call to stop fighting and start reporting. Because some lines, even a madman like Li Huowang knows you don’t cross alone. This chapter is all about the calm after the storm, the weight of a single discovery, and the kind of bureaucratic horror that makes monsters look almost friendly.

## Key Plot Points

- **The chase through Great Qi**: Li Huowang uses the Spine Sword’s fissure to pursue the Ren King into the decaying ruins of Great Qi—a parallel empire that mirrors the bedchamber of a Great Liang prince but is now abandoned and rotting.
- **The Fa Sect reveal**: Just as Li Huowang is about to intercept the Ren King’s palanquin, a black-clothed woman with a "sorcerer" (巫) character on her face attacks him. He instantly recognizes this as the mark of the Fa Sect, a cult that worships the Heavenly Calamity Yú’er Shén. The political stakes skyrocket.
- **Strategic retreat**: Instead of pressing the attack, Li Huowang makes a cold, calculating decision. The Ren King’s involvement with the Fa Sect is not a matter he needs to settle with his own sword—this is a violation the Supervisory Heavenly Office cannot ignore. He backs off and heads straight to report.
- **An unexpected detour**: While returning for his dropped bronze coin sword, Li Huowang stumbles upon a naked, pregnant old eunuch screaming in the ruins of the Cold Palace. The monk begs him to help with the birth, but Li Huowang refuses flatly—he has neither the skill nor the trust for such a grotesque trap.
- **Zhuge Yuan’s silence**: Throughout the chapter, Zhuge Yuan remains unusually pensive, hinting that the state of Great Qi weighs heavily on his mind.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Great Qi (大齐)**: This is the parallel empire that mirrors Great Liang, but in a state of collapse and ruin. In xianxia cosmology, parallel dynasties or "mirror realms" are a common trope—two versions of a kingdom existing in the same space but different times, or as yin-yang projections of each other. Great Qi’s desolation suggests something catastrophic happened here, and the fact that it’s now a convenient transit route for Li Huowang’s sword strikes adds a tactical dimension to the horror.

**The Fa Sect (法教)**: The "Fa" (巫) on the attacker’s cloth is a deep nod to Chinese occult history. The Fa Sect in this novel is a cult dedicated to the Child-God Yú’er Shén, a primordial entity from the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* described as having a human head and two serpent bodies. Historically, "Fa" is also used for sorcery or shamanic rites—so this isn’t just a rebel group, it’s a *theological terrorist organization*. For a prince to ally with them is political suicide in the eyes of the Office.

**The Cold Palace (冷宫)**: In imperial China, the Cold Palace wasn’t a frozen building—it was a euphemism for the abandoned quarters of disgraced concubines. A place out of favor, out of mind. Seeing it completely empty and overgrown in Great Qi tells us the empire is so dead even its outcasts have fled or died.

**The Pregnant Eunuch**: This is a direct callback to Zhengde Temple’s "vase-girl" and "son-granting" rituals. The Fa Sect’s magic or the temple’s corruption allows even a eunuch—a man whose body has been mutilated to prevent reproduction—to become pregnant. The grotesque result is a swollen, split abdomen leaking amniotic fluid. It’s body horror used as worldbuilding: the rules of nature are not just broken here, they’re mocked.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in *political horror*. The stakes shift from "can I survive this fight?" to "what does this alliance mean for the world?" Li Huowang’s decision to stop chasing and let the Office handle it shows his growth: he’s no longer a cornered animal lashing out, but a tactical survivor who knows when to use institutional weapons.

Pay close attention to Zhuge Yuan’s silence. He’s always been the calm, clear voice of reason—seeing him troubled without speaking is a red flag that Great Qi’s collapse might be more relevant to the main story than we think.

And don’t skip the eunuch scene as pure comic relief. It’s a visceral reminder that even in a "safe" zone, the Dao-Twisted World is still a place where male eunuchs can get pregnant from corrupt rituals. The horror isn't just in the monsters—it's in the broken biology.

**For the seasoned xianxia reader**: This is a classic "witnessing a forbidden alliance" trope, but the Fa Sect elevates it beyond mere coup politics. If the Child-God Yú’er Shén is involved, the Office’s response won’t be a quiet arrest—it’ll be an extermination. Buckle up.

## Excursus: The “Parallel Dynasties” as Narrative Geopolitics

Here’s where things get deep. The Great Liang / Great Qi relationship is one of the novel’s most brilliant structural mechanics. Liang is the "present" battlefield—lively, corrupt, politically active. Qi is the "past" or "shadow"—dead, silent, but still traversable. Li Huowang using the Spine Sword to cut a fissure between them is like stepping through a wound in reality.

This isn’t just teleportation. It implies that the two empires are literally stacked on top of each other, like layers of sediment. What happened to Qi that killed it so completely? And could the same fate be waiting for Liang? The unanswered question hangs over the chapter like the broken moon overhead.
