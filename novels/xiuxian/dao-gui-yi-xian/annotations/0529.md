## Chapter Overview
Get ready, dear readers—Li Huowang is about to pull off something that changes *everything*. In this chapter, our favorite paranoid reality-bender receives a commission from the unstable Prince of Liang, Ji Lin: assassinate the Prince of Ren, the second-in-line brother who’s got a stash of illicit cultivators and a dark family history. What follows isn’t just a stealth mission—it’s a *breakthrough* arc. Li Huowang tests the spine-sword’s forgotten function and realizes he can send people (maybe even himself) to Great Qi *alive*. The tactical thrill is real, laced with heavy implications for how he might finally corner the Dice. But first, the Prince of Ren’s luxurious, opera-drenched mansion awaits, with a surprise at the bedchamber that’s pure *Dao Gui Yi Xian*: unnerving, decadent, and wrong.

## Key Plot Points
- Ji Lin reveals that the Prince of Ren’s retainers include rogue cultivators—the same ones who killed Ji Lin’s mother and sister.
- Li Huowang receives a detailed map of the Prince of Ren’s Mansion and begins his infiltration.
- He decides to test the spine-sword’s ability to *transport* people to Great Qi instead of just killing them.
- In a controlled experiment, Li Huowang slices one of the mansion’s hidden eunuch sentries, sending most of his body to Great Qi while leaving a hand behind. Li Sui confirms the man is alive on the other side.
- The discovery sends a jolt of manic excitement through Li Huowang—Zhuge Yuan’s plan to counter the Dice suddenly seems feasible.
- Li Huowang restrains himself, choosing to finish the Prince of Ren first before pursuing the Dice, reasoning that he still needs Ji Lin’s power.
- He searches the maze-like mansion, finally locating the Prince of Ren watching an extravagant opera performance in an open courtyard.
- Observing the situation, Li Huowang decides against immediate action due to the open terrain and possible hidden retainers.
- He follows the Prince of Ren’s procession to his bedchamber and slips inside.
- The chapter ends with a grotesque and eerily sensual discovery: a fully made-up female opera performer, painted face but completely naked, singing before the prince in his private chambers.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
- **仁王 (Prince of Ren)**: The title “仁” (Ren) means “benevolence” or “humaneness”—a core Confucian virtue. The irony is not lost: in the Dao-Twisted World, this prince is anything but benevolent, having allegedly employed rogue cultivators to assassinate the empress dowager and princess. The use of virtuous titles to mask corruption is a recurring theme.
- **龙衮袍 (Dragon Robe)**: A formal court robe embroidered with dragon motifs, worn by emperors and high-ranking princes in imperial China. The dragon’s number of claws often signified rank—five-clawed dragons for the emperor, four for princes. Ji Lin’s identification of his brother by this robe underscores that this power struggle is within the imperial family itself.
- **旦 (Dan)**: In traditional Chinese opera (especially Peking opera), this is the general term for female roles, encompassing multiple subcategories like the virtuous *qingyi* or the vivacious *huadan*. The stark contrast between her meticulously painted face and her naked body is a chilling inversion of opera’s formal aesthetics—art made obscene by power.
- **二更天 (Second Watch)**: The traditional Chinese timekeeping system divided the night into five “watches” (geng), each roughly two hours long. The second watch corresponds to approximately 9:00–11:00 PM. Li Huowang’s patience—waiting an hour into the second watch—shows a hard-earned professionalism.
- **王府建于京都 vs. 封地 (Mansion in Capital vs. Fief)**: Traditionally, princes were enfeoffed to territories outside the capital to prevent them from amassing political power at court. The Prince of Ren’s mansion being *inside* the capital is a deliberate political statement, which Li Huowang immediately interprets as the imperial family “raising vipers in a basket” (养蛊)—a metaphor for fostering conflict among heirs to select the strongest.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in pacing—Li Huowang goes from quiet reconnaissance to a monumental system breakthrough in the span of a few rooftops. The spine-sword’s *transport* function is a complete game-changer. For anyone who’s been wondering how our broken hero could ever corner the Dice without sacrificing everything, this is the first real tactical answer. Zhuge Yuan’s plan now has teeth. Also note how Li Huowang’s paranoid patience has matured: he doesn’t rush the assassination despite being within striking distance. He waits. He observes. He sniffs out potential traps. The final image—a naked, painted woman singing for a prince alone in his bedchamber—is pure *Dao Gui Yi Xian*: unsettling, decadent, and haunted by the unspoken horror of power. It’s a short but dense chapter that redefines the story’s strategic landscape.

## Behind the Name: “Benevolent Prince” Ironies
“Ren” (仁) is one of the most revered concepts in Chinese philosophy—it’s the central virtue of Confucianism, roughly translated as “benevolence” or “human-heartedness.” A ruler should be *ren*, ruling by moral example. But here, the Prince of Ren is a suspected patricide (or at least fratricide-by-proxy) and a patron of forbidden arts. The author is making a dark joke: titles are masks, and in this world, sanctity is the first thing that rots.
