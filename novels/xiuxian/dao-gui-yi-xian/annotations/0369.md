## Chapter Overview

Pack your bags, fellow travelers—this chapter is a masterclass in emotional body horror and identity warfare. After the massive political and survival stakes of recent chapters, Li Huowang gets a dose of the *personal*, and boy, does it hit different. We open with a quiet carriage ride where our battered protagonist tries to help Bai Lingmiao with her immortal problem—only to veer wildly into a raw, bloody, and deeply unsettling confrontation in a rented inn room. What starts as a pragmatic discussion about "raising a Great Calamity" to counter the possessing immortal devolves into a power-struggle lover's quarrel fueled by jealousy, corruption, and a terrifying identity reveal. The chapter delivers a gut-punch revelation that reframes Bai Lingmiao’s entire character arc: is she still herself, or has she already been hollowed out and replaced? Buckle up—this is Dao-Twisted at its most intimate and most venomous.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang, still grievously wounded from his previous ordeals, explains Shentu Gang’s “raise a Great Calamity” theory to Bai Lingmiao as a counterbalance to her oppressive immortal. He describes it as a tightrope act—using one possessing entity to leash another, like balancing a scale.
- Bai Lingmiao deflects his concern, refusing to discuss her own immortal's growing threat. She shifts the focus to his own physical state, a clear act of self-protective deflection that suggests her situation is graver than she admits.
- Back at the inn, Bai Lingmiao’s behavior takes a sharp turn. She initiates a violent, sexually charged encounter that Li Huowang is too weak to resist—and during it, she explicitly confronts him about Yang Na and the Second Spirit, her voice dripping with vindictive jealousy and newfound aggression.
- The post-coital scene erupts into a psychological horror climax: Li Huowang looks down to find Bai Lingmiao has physically transformed into the Second Spirit—her distorted half-face, many eyes, weeping a plea for recognition and calling him "Brother Li."
- Li Huowang spirals into a terrifying hypothesis: has the Second Spirit already replaced Bai Lingmiao? Was her "personality change" from killing intent actually a body-snatching in progress? He roars into the empty room, demanding answers from the Second Spirit.
- The Second Spirit then morphs back into Bai Lingmiao—now with white hair and pink pupils—and delivers the chapter’s killer line: “Great Spirit, Second Spirit—what difference does it make? To you, what does it matter which one you use?” A taunt that leaves the identity boundary irrevocably shattered.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**“Raising a Great Calamity” to Counter an Immortal** — This chapter dives into a dark corner of folk spirit-medium theory. The logic Shentu Gang proposes is rooted in the “mutual restraint” philosophy found in both Daoist inner alchemy and Chinese folk religion: if one possessing entity dominates you, you can introduce a second, more ferocious entity to challenge or “balance” the first. The protagonist likens it to a scale. In real-world *chuma* and *tiao dashen* traditions, mediums rarely “raise” a second spirit deliberately—this is a violent heresy born from desperation, a gamble between two equally dangerous forces. It mirrors the “wolf and tiger” idiom Li Huowang mentions: using a stronger predator to drive off a weaker one, even if the cure might ultimately be as deadly as the bite.

**The Great Spirit / Second Spirit (大神/二神) Dynamic** — This chapter weaponizes the central ambiguity of the *tiao dashen* spirit-medium tradition. In authentic practice, the Great Spirit (dà shén) is the possessing entity, and the Second Spirit (èr shén) is the assistant or the possessed medium themselves. But here, the novel deliberately blurs the line until it’s unrecognizable: Bai Lingmiao and the Second Spirit become a single, shifting being whose emotions and identity bleed into each other. When the Second Spirit cries “Brother Li” with her own face and Bai Lingmiao replies with the Second Spirit’s mocking tone, it becomes a psychological trap where Li Huowang cannot tell if he is speaking to a woman he loves or a parasite wearing her skin. This is classic *Dao Gui Yi Xian* horror—the folk tradition is not just a source of magic; it’s a source of primal, cognitive dread.

### Chuma mediumship (出马仙)

A northeastern Chinese folk spirit-medium tradition where a human is selected by an animal or spirit immortal to serve as its vessel; the body becomes a temporary home and tool for the possessing entity.

### Spirit-dance (跳大神)

A ritual performance in which a medium, guided by a Second Spirit (assistant), invites a possessing immortal to manifest and answer questions or perform healing; the dance and chanting are the conduit.

## Reading Guide

Here’s your emotional safety briefing, dear reader: this chapter is going to hurt. Not from action or gore, but because it takes the one relationship Li Huowang thought was stable—his connection with Bai Lingmiao—and grinds it into an identity-blurred mess. Pay close attention to the *voice* here. Bai Lingmiao’s dialogue shifts from guarded and evasive (refusing to talk about the immortal) to violently possessive (demanding to know who Yang Na is, her words “gritted out between her teeth”). This isn’t the soft, submissive girl we met early in the story. The corruption of the Purple-Tasseled Sword and the pressure from the immortal have weaponized her emotions. And when she becomes the Second Spirit, weeping and calling him “Brother Li,” Li Huowang doesn’t just suspect a body-snatching—he confronts the possibility that he loved a ghost and didn’t notice.

The last line is a masterstroke of ambiguity: “Great Spirit, Second Spirit—what difference does it make?” Bai Lingmiao doesn’t answer the question. She *dismisses* it. And that silence is more terrifying than any monster. For readers coming from Western fantasy, this is the moment where the “possessed maiden” trope gets turned inside out. It’s not a demon to be exorcised. It’s a voice that was always hers, now twisted, asking if you can still love what you can’t label.
