## Chapter Overview

This chapter is a masterclass in quiet dread. Li Huowang, fresh from the horrors of Cangshui County, picks up his journey with a cart full of supplies and a newborn, tentative acceptance of the Black Tai Sui inside him. He reads aloud, feeds his internal passenger, and even shrugs off Red Center's provocations with practiced ease. But beneath this newly found peace, the story layers an unsettling honey trap: a roadside noodle stall, a loving pregnant wife, and a doting husband who leans in to ask his unborn child to call him "Daddy." The only problem? The answer comes from *Li Huowang's* gut. The chapter weaponizes warmth—domestic bliss, a loyal dog, the simple joy of a good bowl of noodles—and turns it into the most disquieting mirror yet for the protagonist's crumbling sense of self.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang leaves Cangshui County with the Black Tai Sui and a loaded cart of dried goods. His mental state is noticeably lighter; he allows himself a moment of satisfaction for helping others escape their suffering.
- He interacts with the Buddhist monk hallucination, sharing food and a quiet, melancholic philosophy on happiness in a painful world.
- Mantou the dog is caught stealing food and unceremoniously tossed out to run alongside the cart—a moment of earthy, mundane authority.
- Li Huowang teaches the Black Tai Sui to read from a primer, treating it less like a parasitic threat and more like a strange, nascent intelligence he is trying to domesticate.
- A tentacle emerges from Li Huowang's navel clutching a *binocular eyeball* that once belonged to Red Center (the Zuowandao). When Red Center appears and tries to bait him with its origin story, Li Huowang coolly ignores the provocation, showing he has learned to resist their games.
- Stopping for noodles at a roadside stall, Li Huowang observes a tender scene: a pregnant wife and her doting husband, who leans in to speak to his unborn child.
- The husband asks the fetus to call him “Daddy.” From inside Li Huowang, the Black Tai Sui responds with the same word—“Daddy”—transforming a moment of familial warmth into a terrifying eruption of the supernatural into the mundane.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is deceptively light on direct xianxia action, but it showcases the novel’s deep, visceral use of *inner alchemy* gone wrong. The Black Tai Sui is not an external spirit; it is a parasitic, living component of Li Huowang’s cultivation, responding to his teaching in real time. This twists the traditional Daoist concept of “cultivation as self-refinement” into a terrifying, shared, and volatile relationship. The scene where Li Huowang reads a *primer* to a creature growing inside his stomach subverts the classical Chinese ideal of literacy as the foundation of civilization—here, reading is used to *tame* a monster, not to enlighten a child. The act itself is darkly absurd. Additionally, the “fortune-teller” advice about talking to a pregnant child is a real folk belief in Chinese culture, where prenatal education (*胎教*) is taken seriously. The novel weaponizes this habitual kindness, making the Black Tai Sui hijack the gentlest of human rituals for its own emergence.

### Inner Alchemy

A Daoist practice that treats the body as a furnace for refining spiritual energy; in this novel, this classical concept is grotesquely inverted into physical parasitism and co-existence.

### Prenatal education (胎教)

A real Chinese folk tradition where expectant parents talk or play music to their unborn child, believed to shape its intelligence and character; the novel weaponizes this wholesome practice into a trigger for cosmic horror.

## Reading Guide

Fellow Daoists, prepare for a *punch to the gut* disguised as a midday rest stop. This chapter is a perfect example of *Dao Gui Yi Xian*'s ability to make you lower your guard before the horror. You’ll be lulled by the rhythm of the cart, the taste of the dried dates, the amusing interchange between a master and his gluttonous dog. You might even smile at the way Li Huowang out-stubborn a Zuowandao trickster. Then comes the noodle stall. Pay attention to how the “voice” of the Black Tai Sui has evolved—it no longer just *clatters*; it now *vocalizes*. And that final line? It’s not just a jump scare; it’s an existential claim. The child that the stall owner is speaking to isn’t coming yet, but something that recognizes the word “Daddy” already exists inside our protagonist. This is Li Huowang’s new family portrait, and it’s growing more grotesque with every mile. Enjoy your meal—you’ll need the energy for what comes next.
