## Chapter Overview

Get ready, fellow Daoists. If you thought Li Huowang had already scraped the bottom of the body-horror barrel, Chapter 480 politely escorts you to a new, freshly blood-slicked low. Having lured a Spite-Blight into possessing his own detached lower half—by *cutting himself in half at the waist*—Li Huowang now crawls around as a torso, collecting his prize while fighting back the black smoke of pure malice that seeps from the dead entity. The sequence is a masterclass in paying the extreme price of cleverness. And just when he thinks he’s home free, a new variable shows up: a woman whose head bristles with lit incense sticks like a hedgehog, packing a wolf-tooth club and a matching Office token. The chapter is a taut, gruesome duet between vulnerability and deception, all set against the quiet, dripping aftermath of a forest hunt.

## Key Plot Points

1. **The trap is sprung**: The Spite-Blight takes control of Li Huowang’s severed lower half, only to find it has no eyes and no direction. The bait is purely physical, and the monster is completely disoriented.
2. **Coin sword execution**: Li Huowang, still invisible, drops his bronze coin sword onto the Spite-Blight, pinning and killing it. The entity dissolves into black smoke.
3. **Contamination effects**: Touching the rising smoke from the dead Spite-Blight expands Li Huowang’s inner darkness—murderous intent, hatred, irritability all deepen visibly.
4. **Weird physics**: Li Huowang discovers that living flesh cannot physically touch the Spite-Blight’s remains, but inanimate objects (like a rotten stick) can. The entity leaves an empty space beneath its smoke.
5. **New arrival**: A fat woman with incense sticks driven through her skull, wearing a red talisman bib and carrying a wolf-tooth club, emerges from the woods.
6. **Token standoff**: Both parties produce identical “Supervisory Heavenly Office” tokens. The woman initially mistakes Li Huowang for a Spite-Blight, but his Ao-Jing Sect branding confirms him.
7. **Competitive job**: The woman reveals that multiple Office operatives can accept the same bounty—a “who gets it done first” system.
8. **The lie beneath her face**: Li Huowang reads the Ten Emotions and Eight Sufferings inside her and sees that her surprise is a performance. She wants the prize.
9. **Unspoken threat**: Li Huowang forces himself upright, claims the kill, and threatens her outright with Ao-Jing methods. She backs down.
10. **Exit with caution**: He carries his trophy out of the grove, still on high alert, his sword never leaving his hand.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Incense-Skull Ritualist**: The fat woman’s appearance—incense sticks piercing the scalp, small banners through the earlobes—is a striking example of practical folk-exorcist iconography. Incense is used in Chinese ritual as a medium between the human and divine realms; embedding it in one’s own flesh is not mere decoration but a form of sensory deprivation or offering, suggesting the practitioner is in a constant state of prayer or warding. The wolf-tooth club wrapped in yellow talisman paper similarly belongs to a class of improvised, “street-grade” ritual weapons distinct from the refined art of sword or seal.

**Office Job Competition**: The “one job, many takers” system within the Supervisory Heavenly Office is presented without judgment, but it quietly explains much about the Office’s internal culture. It is not a gongmen (衙门) with fixed pay grades but a contract-hunting bureaucracy where personal capability determines reward. This fosters distrust, pragmatism, and a willingness to kill for a claim.

**Ten Emotions and Eight Sufferings**: Li Huowang’s ability to read the woman’s inner state through this framework—knowing her face does not match her heart—is a quietly terrifying power in a world built on lies. It reduces social interaction to a kind of x-ray vision of intent, which is both a survival tool and a source of profound loneliness.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a nerve-rattling palate cleanser after the peach-grove action. It is a slow, wounded crawl toward safety, interrupted by a stranger who is *almost* an ally but *might* be a predator. The key tension is not the Spite-Blight—it is already dead—but the question of trust between Office colleagues. Li Huowang is at his most physically broken, yet he is also at his most intimidatingly competent. The threat he delivers (“you do not want to find out what the Ao-Jing Sect’s methods are like”) is a line that works because we *know* exactly what his sect’s methods are like. It is not a bluff. The reader is invited to admire the cold pragmatism of a man who can threaten while holding his own guts together, and to feel the unease of knowing that every Office encounter might be one step from a backstab. Pay attention to the Spite-Blight physics—living flesh cannot touch it—as this may become mechanically relevant.
