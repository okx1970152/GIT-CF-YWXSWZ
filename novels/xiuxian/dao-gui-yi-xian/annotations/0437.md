## Chapter Overview

Get ready for one of the heaviest lore drops yet, fellow Daoists. This chapter isn't just a fight scene—it's a complete paradigm shift. Li Huowang finally pieces together the true nature of Zhuge Yuan's "Great Qi," and the answer redefines everything we thought we knew about time, history, and the Dao-Twisted World. What follows is a high-stakes confrontation in the palace that pits the Great Qi loyalists (led by Zhuge Yuan) against the corrupted monks of Zhengde Temple, all while a literal god-monster from Great Liang bears down on them. The finale is a desperate, self-sacrificial gambit that shoves the *Thousand Greats Record* to its absolute limit.

## Key Plot Points

- **The Great Qi Reveal**: Li Huowang connects the final dots. Zhuge Yuan’s skill as a “Storyteller” isn't just narrative flavor—it's literal. His Heart-Core ability is to access an *alternate history*. The “Great Qi” around him is not the past, but a parallel timeline where the Mad Monk is the abbot of Zhengde Temple and the Zuowandao are native threats.
- **Hearts-Cores are Doors**: The metaphysics snap into place. A Heart-Core is a living gate to another branch of history, explaining the “time travel” and the oddities Li Huowang has witnessed.
- **Confrontation at the Palace**: Zhuge Yuan’s coalition of loyalists reaches the Empress Dowager’s meditation chamber, only to find the Mad Monk (as Abbot) forcibly tonsuring the boy-emperor of Great Qi, making him a disciple of Zhengde Temple as a protective measure.
- **The Eye Breaks Through**: The massive vertical eye of the Great Liang Siming exerts its ontological pressure on the Great Qi timeline. Its droning chant causes ordinary people to collapse and physically distort, threatening to erase the entire existence of Zhuge Yuan’s realm.
- **Intercalating the Five Phases**: With his own life on borrowed time, Li Huowang performs the final taboo from the *Thousand Greats Record*. He sacrifices his (Bei Feng’s) five viscera, filling his body with parasitic worms from Bashe to become immune to the Siming’s influence.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

### “Clearing the Monarch’s Side” (清君侧)
This is a classic, heavy-weight political trope in Chinese historical fiction and drama. It’s a banner raised by “loyalists” (usually generals or officials) who claim the ruler is being corrupted by evil advisors. They don’t say they’re rebelling—they’re there to *cleanse* the ruler’s surroundings. Of course, it’s almost always a pretext for a coup, which gives Zhuge Yuan’s “rebellion” a very specific, historically charged flavor.

### The Great Qi “History” vs “Time Travel”
This is the key lore shift. Li Huowang realizes Zhuge Yuan doesn’t travel *through time*—he accesses a *parallel history*. This is a crucial distinction. He’s not seeing the past of his world; he’s visiting an entire separate branch of reality where different events happened (e.g., the Mad Monk became a major abbott instead of a wandering vagrant). This reframes the Zuowandao attacks as coming from a different historical branch, not just the future/past.

### Intercalating the Five Phases (置闰五行)
This is the ultimate ace of the *Thousand Greats Record*. “Intercalation” is a calendar term for adding an extra leap month to align lunar and solar cycles. Here, it’s a horrific re-alignment of the body itself. By sacrificing the five visceral organs (Wood-Liver, Fire-Heart, Spleen-Earth, Lung-Metal, Kidney-Water), the practitioner merges their five senses and opens the door for Bashe (the primordial god of pain) to fill the void with writhing, parasitic life. The result is temporary immunity from *all* other forms of cognitive or spiritual interference, but the cost is the complete loss of one’s original body.

### The “Mad Monk” Occupation
This detail is brilliant character contrast. In Li Huowang’s personal Hell-World, the Monk is a pathetic, hallucinated burden. In this alternate Great Qi history, he is the powerful, respected abbot of a major temple. It shows that even “useless” characters in one timeline can be absolute powerhouses in another, adding a layer of cosmic tragedy to the personal cost of the Heart-Element.

## Reading Guide

This is the episode where the grand cosmic jigsaw puzzle finally clicks together. For chapters now, we’ve been wondering what the deal was with Zhuge Yuan’s Great Qi. Was it the past? A pocket dimension? A mass hallucination? The answer—that it’s a *parallel history* accessed through a Heart-Core—is both brilliant and terrifying. It means there are countless histories fighting for reality, and Li Huowang’s problem isn’t just schizophrenia; it’s being the linchpin in a multi-dimensional war.

Pay off to the *Thousand Greats Record* subplot: Li Huowang finally uses “Intercalating the Five Phases.” It’s not a triumphant power-up. It’s a monstrous, self-destructive act where he turns his chest cavity into a writhing nest for Bashe’s worms to save Zhuge Yuan. The final line, “it was Bei Feng’s body,” is a cold, strategic reminder that within all this cosmic horror, Li Huowang has become the ultimate pragmatist. He’s willing to burn through other people’s lives as easily as his own to achieve his goal.

Don’t miss the Mad Monk scene. Seeing the pathetic old wanderer from the White Tower as the Abbot of Zhengde, speaking in grand declarations, is a wonderful piece of alternative-timeline horror. It shows that in another history, *everyone* might be a monster.
