## Chapter Overview

The Shangsi Festival hits Shangjing with full force — lantern riddles, hobbyhorses, and fried snacks everywhere. For the first time in ages, the capital feels almost normal. But for Li Huowang, this festive night is a test. He has to give Bai Lingmiao the coming-of-age hairpin ceremony she never had, under the watchful guidance of Zhuge Yuan’s fan-pointing, while a strange young master with an overpriced jade cat keeps wandering into his line of sight. This isn't a battle chapter. It's a *breath*. And you know what that means in the Dao-Twisted World — the air’s always too still before the next scream.

## Key Plot Points

- Shangjing is eerily well-reconstructed after Doumu’s rampage, suggesting the Supervisory Heavenly Office has efficiency that’s almost unsettling in its own right. Li Huowang notes the craftsmanship, but the reader should note the *speed*.
- Li Huowang learns, through a silent gesture from Zhuge Yuan, that Bai Lingmiao never received her Shangsi hairpinning (笄礼/Jili) — she was already Danyangzi’s captive at fifteen. This revelation surfaces a deep, unhealed wound in her character arc.
- He takes her to a tailor, dresses her in a formal trailing gown, and walks her through the festival rites: fried snacks, tea, black rice, lantern guessing. For one night, she becomes visible and happy.
- Zhuge Yuan observes that Bai Lingmiao has something weighing on her heart. Li Huowang admits he knows but has no solution — he’s shelving it, hoping time will erode the pain.
- A pale young master with a jade cat draws Li Huowang’s suspicion. Li Huowang cannot read his Ten Emotions and Eight Sufferings — a dangerous sign. The man’s servants are also inscrutable. But the encounter turns out to be a false alarm: the boy is just a rich fool showing off a cat he overpaid for.
- The chapter’s final beat: the same young master walks right up to Bai Lingmiao, staring directly at her white hair. Li Huowang springs into action, bronze coin sword drawn.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Shangsi Festival (上巳节)** — Literally “Third Day of the Third Month” of the lunar calendar. In ancient China, it was a day of purification, river bathing, and spring outings. By the Ming/Qing period (the novel’s aesthetic era), the festival had merged with the *Jili* hairpinning ceremony for fifteen-year-old girls. These girls would wear pomegranate blossoms and pray to the Joy Spirit (Xi Shen) for a good match. After the ceremony, social restrictions on unmarried women tightened considerably. The novel preserves almost all of these folk customs — fried snacks, black rice, lantern riddles, praying to Xi Shen — making the Shangsi Festival a rare pocket of *normal* cultural memory in a world drowning in body horror.

**Jili (笄礼)** — The female coming-of-age ceremony: a pin or hair clasp is inserted into the girl’s coiled hair. It marks her as marriageable. In this chapter, Li Huowang physically places a pomegranate blossom in Bai Lingmiao’s hair in lieu of a formal pin — an improvised, emotionally charged substitute that says *I see you as a woman who deserves her day.*

**Pitch-Pot (投壶/Touhu)** — An ancient Chinese drinking game where players toss arrows into a narrow-necked bronze pot. It appears here as a festive street game, giving Bai Lingmiao a moment of childlike focus and joy. The contrast with her usual grimness is the point.

**Ten Emotions and Eight Sufferings (十情八苦)** — A twisted expansion of the Buddhist Eight Sufferings; in the Dao-Twisted World, it appears to be a passive perception ability tied to cultivation or innate constitution. Li Huowang’s inability to read the young master’s emotional profile is a red flag — it means the boy either has an extremely high cultivation or some kind of concealment artifact.

## Reading Guide

This is one of those chapters you *feel* before you *understand*. The Shangsi Festival sequence is deliberately paced as a temporary reprieve. Li Huowang is trying, hard, to give Bai Lingmiao a memory that doesn’t taste of Danyangzi’s rot. The tailor shop, the pomegranate flower, the guessing games — all of it is a fragile stage set. And you can almost smell the sand running out.

Zhuge Yuan’s intervention is elegant: he says nothing, just points with his fan. That’s the mark of a true scholar-ally. He sees what Li Huowang is too busy to notice, and makes space for a correction.

The false-alarm confrontation with the jade cat boy is a masterclass in tension management. Li Huowang has trained himself to treat the *inability to read someone* as an imminent threat. And he’s right to — in this world, that feeling has never been a false alarm. Until now. The scene deliberately misdirects: the boy really *is* just a wealthy fool. But the fact that the chapter ends with him approaching Bai Lingmiao anyway means the anxiety hasn’t left the building. It’s waiting for the next chapter, coiled like a spring.

Keep your eyes on the white hair. It’s not just beautiful — it’s a target.
