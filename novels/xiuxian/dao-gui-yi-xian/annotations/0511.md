## Chapter Overview
Get ready for a wild, one-sided beatdown that turns into a theological crisis! This chapter is a masterclass in psychological and supernatural tension. Our terrified duo—the scheming Gouwa and the pompous Lü Xiucai—find themselves completely outmatched by a ruthless, baby-headed Daoist who wants their precious *ruyi* and probably their lives. It’s a brutal, almost comedic display of their incompetence (Gouwa’s “brotherhood” runs away faster than a startled cat) until a last-ditch, desperate prayer flips the entire script. The payoff is a chilling, reality-bending moment where a denied god makes a very inconvenient comeback, leaving the Daoist questioning the very fabric of his world. It’s a short, sharp shock to the system that proves faith can be a weapon, even if the god using it is supposed to be dead.

## Key Plot Points
*   The yellow-robed Daoist ambushes Gouwa and Lü Xiucai in the rear courtyard, immediately disarming Gouwa and slicing off one of his fingers with a bronze coin sword.
*   Lü Xiucai’s attempt to threaten the Daoist with his master’s name (“Li Huowang”) fails completely, marking him as a rogue cultivator with no backing.
*   Gouwa’s threat of the White Lotus Society backfires spectacularly when the Daoist reveals that the White Lotus is considered easy prey with no powerful patrons.
*   In a moment of pure cowardice, Gouwa abandons Lü Xiucai and flees, leaving him to face the Daoist alone.
*   The Daoist overpowers Lü Xiucai with ease, mocking his weak Spirit Invocation technique, and prepares to finish him off.
*   A hidden ally sends a flying needle, but the Daoist reveals his grotesque secret—a second, wrinkled baby’s head that bites the needle out of the air.
*   Facing a guaranteed death, Lü Xiucai screams a desperate prayer to Wusheng Laomu, causing the Daoist’s enchanted sword to instantly shatter upon striking his skin.
*   The Daoist is horrified, denying the event as impossible since “Wusheng Laomu has long been dead,” yet his body’s terrified retreat betrays his words.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter is a powerhouse of worldbuilding and folk religion tropes. Let’s break down the key elements.

First, you have **Spirit Invocation (神打, Shén Dǎ)**. Lü Xiucai used this technique, which he assumed would make him invulnerable. In folk practice, it's a ritual that temporarily grants a physical boon, often imperviousness to blades, through chanting and blood sacrifice. But it's not absolute; it has clear limits, which is why the Daoist's specialized weapon could still cut him.

Then there's the Daoist's own crazy ability: growing a second, grumpy baby head from his own shoulder to intercept a sneak attack. This is a hardcore, body-horror take on a "Xiantian" (先天) or innate ability, often associated with cultivating a "second self" or a divine fetus within one's own body. It’s a brutal, visual reminder that in this world, power often comes with grotesque physical changes.

The core of the chapter, however, revolves around **Wusheng Laomu (无生老母, the Unborn Venerable Mother)**. She is the central savior figure of the White Lotus Society and its related sects. She is a maternal, merciful mother who promises to lead her children back to the "Native Land of True Emptiness." The idea is that all human souls are her children, sent to the mortal world to suffer. The Daoist’s shock is critical: he states she is dead, implying a major cosmic event or a sect purge. But Lü Xiucai’s genuine, desperate faith, possibly fueled by the latent power of his *ruyi* or the painting, proves that the connection is still there. This isn't just a prayer; it's a direct appeal that, for a moment, makes the "impossible" possible. The system of belief in the Dao-Twisted World is not abstract; it’s a tangible, dangerous force.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a fantastic example of how *Dao Gui Yi Xian* weaponizes its characters’ beliefs against them. As you read, pay close attention to the sheer *panic* of both Gouwa and Lü Xiucai. They are not heroes; they are grifters wearing a mask of bravery that shatters the second they face real danger. Gouwa’s selfish flight and Lü Xiucai’s pathetic begging make the climax hit much harder.

Also, savor the Daoist’s arrogance. He is a classic predator of the xianxia world: professional, confident, and contemptuous of his prey. His horror at the end is genuine and delicious. He’s not just a villain; he’s a believer whose entire worldview is being violently challenged. The monstrous baby head is a great visual, but his panic is the real horror. He can’t reconcile the fact of a dead god’s power with his own lived experience.

The subtext here is fantastic. Is the *ruyi* amplifying Lü Xiucai’s faith? Is the painting a sacred relic? Or did his desperate prayer genuinely tap into a cosmic power that is not as dead as the Daoist believes? The ambiguity is the point. This chapter isn’t about a power-up; it’s about a loophole.

## Behind the Scenes: Why "Impossible" Matters
This chapter's title, "Impossible," isn't just a dramatic flourish. It is the core mechanic of the Dao-Twisted World. The Daoist believes Wusheng Laomu is dead because he operates within a logical framework of power: the strong prey on the weak, and the dead have no power. Lü Xiucai’s prayer is "impossible" because it violates that logic. The "horror of a rational explanation" often arises when a character's core understanding of reality is invalidated. Here, the miracle is terrifying *because* it shouldn't have happened. It forces the Daoist to face a gaping hole in his knowledge, leaving him more shaken than any beating ever could. This is the novel’s unique brand of cosmic dread: not just the presence of monsters, but the collapse of certainty.
