## Chapter Overview

Hold onto your hats, fellow seekers of the bizarre, because this chapter is a masterclass in ontological vertigo. "The False and the True" doesn't just blur the line between reality and delusion—it yanks it out from under you and then asks *you* to prove the ground was ever there. Li Huowang is yanked between three realities in a single heartbeat: a dying Xu Shou revealing his origin, a Kangning therapy session that recontextualizes everything as a psychotic break, and a philosophical showdown with his own future self, Ji Zai, who now holds the keys to the kingdom of lies and truth. This isn’t a chapter that answers questions; it’s a chapter that makes you wonder if the questions themselves are even real. Get ready for a trip.

## Key Plot Points

*   The chapter opens with the aftermath of the last chapter’s confrontation. Li Huowang, holding the dying Xu Shou, learns that Xu Shou is the son of Abbess Jingxin and a Heart-Element whose existence bridges both worlds. This revelation is immediately cut short by a helicopter's roar, preventing Li Huowang from hearing Xu Shou's final words.
*   Reality abruptly shifts. Li Huowang finds himself back in Kangning Hospital, in a group therapy session. All his wounds are gone. Dr. Wu Cheng, his face unmarred, calmly listens to Li Huowang's account of the "hallucination" and diagnoses it as a combination of psychosis and "thought fragmentation."
*   Li Huowang, desperate for a stable anchor, borrows Wu Cheng's phone to call Dr. Yi Donglai. The moment he dials, reality shifts again, this time to Niuxin Village’s pond.
*   From this new reality, Li Huowang summons his future self, Ji Zai, demanding an explanation for the chaos. Ji Zai reveals that he has stolen the "True and False" Heavenly Law from Yin-Yang Doumu. Because of this, all of Doumu’s prior arrangements—including the events on the ship—have been retroactively nullified, turned into "falsehoods."
*   The core of the chapter is a brutal philosophical debate: Li Huowang insists that his experiences *were* real, while Ji Zai dismantles the very concept of proof. Ji Zai states that he now holds three and a half Heavenly Laws: Bewilderment, Truth, Falsehood, and the Chaos of Ghostly Mountain.
*   Frustrated and unable to accept Ji Zai’s cryptic explanation, Li Huowang attacks his own reflection. The attack is ineffective. Ji Zai leaves Li Huowang with the chilling prediction that he will one day be the one speaking these words to a past self.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter dives deep into the novel’s core philosophical horror, which is a twisted re-imagining of Daoist concepts. The phrase "真假" (True and False) here is not just about trickery; it’s a **Heavenly Law (天道)**. In traditional Chinese thought, the *Dao* is the ultimate principle of order and reality. Here, it has been broken into splintered, malevolent pieces that can be "stolen" and "controlled." Ji Zai now controls the "Heavenly Law of Bewilderment," which directly parallels the core tenet of the Zuowangdao—the beautiful nihilism of cognitive collapse. The concept of a "Heart-Pan (心蟠)" is also central; as Li Huowang is Ji Zai's past self and link to the world, the changes to Ji Zai's power retroactively affect Li Huowang’s reality. This is a brutal inversion of the classic "cause and effect" (因果) that governs most xianxia worlds. Here, a future action can erase a past event, making memory and experience unreliable. Finally, the term "思維破裂" (thought fragmentation), a clinical term used by Dr. Wu, provides a terrifying counterpoint to the supernatural explanation. It suggests that all of this cosmic horror could simply be a mental disorder, a diagnosis that is equally as damning as being a puppet of a Siming.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a test of faith for the reader. Do you trust the visceral, bloody reality of the Dao-Twisted World, or the clean, sterile, "rational" diagnosis of the modern world? The genius of *Dao Gui Yi Xian* is that it gives you no comfortable place to stand. The guide here is not to find "the truth" but to track the *shift*. Pay close attention to the transitional objects: the helicopter, the phone call, the pond. These are the seams in the world. Also, watch how Li Huowang’s language changes. He moves from aggressive interrogation ("Why did you do this to me?") to desperate questioning ("What is real?") to a raw, frustrated plea ("Stop making me guess!"). The growth of the character is not in power level, but in the depth of his existential crisis. He is being trained to see the world not as a sequence of solid events, but as a malleable narrative written by gods. And the most horrifying part? The author is implying that the very process of reading this story is just another layer of the same constructed reality. Don't try to "win" the chapter. Just feel the dread of a world where the answer to "Did this happen?" is a patient, pitying smile.
