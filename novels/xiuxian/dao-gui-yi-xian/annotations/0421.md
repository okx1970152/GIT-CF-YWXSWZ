## Chapter Overview
This chapter—Chapter 420, "Bai Lingmiao"—is a powerhouse character moment that shifts the spotlight entirely onto Li Huowang's most steadfast ally (and maybe something more). We watch Bai Lingmiao perform a full-blown exorcism with chilling professionalism, then pivot to her quietly obsessive quest: learning to read. It’s not just self-improvement; it’s a survival tactic born from the trauma of being deceived. The chapter shows her grit, her growing edge, and the way she’s completely redefining herself under the weight of the White Lotus legacy. The final scene, with a surprise glimpse of a ragged former acquaintance, teases a reunion with dangerous strings attached.

## Key Plot Points
- Bai Lingmiao, with the Second Spirit as her silent enforcer, performs an exorcism on a possessed man. Using a drum, a spirit-summoning chant, and a soaked red veil, she drives out a red mist entity that tries to flee into a roof beam.
- The Second Spirit exposes a miniature blood-red palace carved inside the beam—clear evidence of deliberate black magic. Bai Lingmiao refuses the client’s copper coin payment, telling them to fix the beam and avoid the person who installed it.
- On the carriage ride back to Niuxin Village, Bai Lingmiao is buried in a literacy primer hand-drawn by Gao Zhijian. She studies with fierce, obsessive focus, determined not to be tricked by misinformation ever again.
- Chun Xiaoman worries about her, offering practical suggestions (hire a tutor), but Bai Lingmiao shoots them down with sharp, bitter logic—any tutor would learn she’s a White Lotus remnant and turn her in.
- A quiet moment of intimacy at night: the three of them—Bai Lingmiao, Chun Xiaoman, and the Second Spirit—end up sleeping curled together, blending comfort with the uncanny.
- The next day, the carriage arrives at a bustling county market, and Bai Lingmiao spots a familiar face: Xiucai Lü, the resentful scholar from their earlier travels.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Spirit-Summoning Verse (*Bang Bing Jue*)** is a core ritual text in the northeastern Chinese folk tradition of *chuma* mediumship. Its rhythm is rapid and urgent, designed to induce a trance-like state in the medium and compel the presence of a spirit. In this chapter, Bai Lingmiao uses it not just to summon, but to *drive out* a possessing entity, showing her growing mastery.

**The Red Bridal Veil (*Honggaitou*)** is a deeply symbolic item in Chinese marriage customs, meant to shield the bride from evil spirits before the wedding. In the hands of a *chuma* medium, it becomes a weapon—soaked in water, it traps and burns the possessing entity, taking on monstrous shapes (wolf’s head, snake) as it works.

**Small Palace Carved in a Beam** is a classic device in Chinese folk horror and sorcery. By carving a cursed model of a dwelling into the structural core of a house, a sorcerer can inflict binding, possession, or misfortune on the inhabitants. The red color signifies blood magic, and destroying the miniature palace breaks the curse.

## Reading Guide
If you’ve been wondering whether Bai Lingmiao is just the “gentle girl” tag—wonder no more. She’s building herself into a force of nature: a pragmatic exorcist with a steady hand, a paranoid strategist who refuses to be tricked again by lies or illiteracy. Her edge is sharp, her humor is dry, and her focus is terrifying. The contrast with the softer girl from earlier arcs is intentional and earned. The final appearance of Xiucai Lü is a ticking clock—his bruised ego and seething resentment make him a powder keg waiting to explode.

## Deeper Dive: The Concept of "Self-Reliance" in Bai Lingmiao’s Arc
Bai Lingmiao’s refusal to hire a tutor isn’t just stubbornness—it’s a survival philosophy born from the Dao-Twisted World’s brutal lesson: *trust nobody*. The memory of Li Huowang’s deception (the “lai zi tou” incident) is burned into her mind. In a world where literacy itself is gatekept by corrupted factions, self-education becomes an act of resistance. She’d rather pore over a pig picture for hours than hand control of her knowledge to a potential informant.

## Folklore Undercurrent: The Second Spirit’s Ambiguity
The Second Spirit continues to blur the line between tool and person. She collects wild vegetables, holds chopsticks, and curls up for warmth without being asked. Bai Lingmiao’s bond with her feels less like master-servant and more like silent sisterhood. This uncanny domesticity—a horror entity doing housework—is a hallmark of folk-horror storytelling.
