## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang finally enjoys a moment of relative peace, listening to Lü Zhuangyuan's gleeful account of his theatrical triumph over local opera rivals. But this quiet interlude is immediately poisoned by suspicion. The Nuo opera performers—whose eerie dance Li Huowang witnessed upon entering town—become the focus of his paranoia. Could the masked man from the previous night be one of them? The monk's sudden reappearance to drag him to another "fun place" only deepens Li Huowang's uncertainty. As hallucinations of Danyangzi and Jiang Yingzi sneer at him from the corner, Li Huowang must decide whether to trust the monk or treat him as another enemy in disguise. The chapter's climax is a quiet, tension-soaked walk to watch the Nuo performers remove their masks—a boundary that could reveal a liar or confirm a trail of clues.

## Key Plot Points

- Lü Zhuangyuan recounts his encounter with local opera performers, bragging about how he intimidated them with his veteran expertise. The opera they performed is *The Great Nuo Expels the Twelve Ghosts*, a myth about a being called "The Great Nuo" who drove away twelve ghosts from a ghost country and civilized humanity.
- Li Huowang connects elements of the Nuo opera—the masks, the tooth-manipulation act—to the masked figure from the previous night's attack. He suspects the three dancers may be linked to his assailant.
- The disheveled monk reappears, inviting Li Huowang to a "fun place." Li Huowang is wary, remembering how the monk's previous invitation led him to the Flesh Buddha at Zhengde Temple.
- Li Huowang's hallucinations of Danyangzi and Jiang Yingzi interrupt his thoughts, laughing at him from a corner, triggering a cycle of self-doubt about what is real and what is illusion.
- Despite his suspicion, Li Huowang resolves to test the monk further. If real, good; if fake, he will strike and learn the truth. His experience from the previous night has made him bolder in seeking direct confrontation.
- The monk leads Li Huowang to the three Nuo performers, who are now taking off their masks. The chapter ends on this moment of potential revelation.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The *Great Nuo Expels the Twelve Ghosts* is a fictional opera within the novel, but it draws heavily from the real Chinese folk tradition of **Nuo opera**. Nuo culture is one of China's oldest ritual performance systems, dating back at least three thousand years. Its core function is exorcism: performers wear fierce wooden masks that are deliberately terrifying—uglier than ghosts—to frighten away evil entities and disease spirits. The novel weaponizes this logic perfectly: if a mask is meant to look scarier than a demon, then who is hiding behind it, and what are *they* doing here?

Li Huowang's suspicion of the Nuo dancers is not unfounded. The tooth-manipulation technique (耍牙, shuaya) is a real and unsettling folk art, often performed alongside Nuo opera in rural China. Performers hide dozens of boar or cow fangs inside their mouths, then rapidly switch and roll them into view—a sight designed to shock and awe audiences. Given that the masked figure from the previous night wore a copper-coin face cover, the connection between hidden teeth and hidden identities becomes too strong for Li Huowang to ignore.

The chapter also deepens Li Huowang's relationship with his own hallucinations. The mocking laughter of Danyangzi and Jiang Yingzi is not just noise; it's a deliberate attack on his reality-anchor. Every time Li Huowang tries to think clearly, the visions shove doubts back into his head. This is the central mechanism of the novel's horror: the protagonist's own mind is weaponized against his judgment.

## Reading Guide

Here's the thing about Li Huowang at this stage: he's paranoid for a reason, but paranoia doesn't make him *right*. The chapter's tension comes from watching him oscillate between logical deduction and near-spiraling self-doubt. The Nuo performers are a perfect suspect—masked, ritualistic, and connected to the town's eeriness—but that's almost *too* neat. Is this a genuine lead, or is Li Huowang's suspicion being quietly fed by Danyangzi's influence? The fact that the novel doesn't hand you an answer is the whole point.

Also, Lü Zhuangyuan's bragging session is a masterclass in characterization through voice. The man is vain, proud, and desperately needs validation—but his pride is also his armor. In a world where performing troupes are at the bottom of the social ladder, his joy at being called "Old Senior" is touching in its vulnerability. He's not just a comic relief character; he's a man holding onto dignity with both hands.

The monk remains the wildcard. Li Huowang's memory of the Flesh Buddha trauma is a sharp reminder that "fun places" in this world are never safe. Yet the monk's genuine cheerfulness and his lack of malice make it hard to write him off as a villain. The question is not whether he's lying—it's whether he knows the truth of what he's showing Li Huowang.

Get ready for the mask reveal. After that, nothing will be the same.

## Deeper Lore: The Nuo Mask as a Horror Device

The Nuo mask is not just a prop—it is the central icon of a folk-horror philosophy. In traditional Nuo culture, the mask is not a costume; it is a vessel. Performers believe that wearing the mask allows the spirit of the deity or exorcist to temporarily inhabit the wearer. The mask becomes a living interface between the human world and the spirit world. This is why, when the Nuo performers in the chapter refuse to remove their masks even while drinking tea, it's not just about convenience or performance discipline. The mask is their identity, their power, and possibly their protection. Li Huowang's relentless focus on the moment of unmasking is not curiosity—it's survival logic. He knows that in this world, a mask that stays on is a lie that hasn't been caught. When it finally comes off, the truth beneath will decide whether he lives or dies.
