## Chapter Overview

A well-earned breather chapter! After the relentless supernatural horrors, we get a slice-of-life look at the camp's social dynamics—and more importantly, two of our favorite side characters (Gouwa and Lü Xiucai) try their hand at romance. But as always in the Dao-Twisted World, even a casual flirtation carries an undercurrent of something… off. And the chapter’s closing beat? Absolutely classic webnovel-style dread—a moment of petty showing off that suddenly goes *very* wrong.

## Key Plot Points

- Morning in the camp: Yang Xiaohai savors the simple joy of having meat to eat, while Gouwa scolds him for getting too comfortable.
- Gouwa reveals his new grooming regimen, driven by Chun Xiaoman’s brutally honest advice about cleanliness and attraction.
- Gouwa and Lü Xiucai pair up to approach the women’s tents, engaging in crude banter about the women’s appearances and Lü Xiucai’s hilariously misapplied poetry line.
- The women are more interested in hearing about Gao Zhijian than in either of them.
- A Qingqiu man on horseback successfully charms a woman named Lan’er, sparking jealousy from Gouwa.
- Determined not to be outdone, Gouwa pulls out a “divine ability”—a blowgun loaded with a white-threaded needle—and tries to impress the crowd.
- The attempt backfires catastrophically: instead of a cool demonstration, Gouwa inhales the needle and starts choking, his face turning red.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**“Real Person” (真人, Zhēnrén)** – When Gouwa claims he learned his divine ability from a “Real Person,” he’s dropping a serious title. In Daoist cultivation hierarchy, *Zhēnrén* denotes a high-level cultivator who has attained profound spiritual realization—basically a certified grandmaster. Faking a connection to one is both desperate face-saving and a subtle reminder that in this world, real power is terrifyingly scarce.
**Blowgun folk tricks** – The blowgun loaded with a needle wrapped in thread has roots in actual Chinese folk performance arts (and sometimes self-defense techniques), often associated with itinerant “street magicians” or martial artists. Its failure here isn’t just a gag—it’s a deliberate subversion of the classic “show-off-turns-hero” trope, hammering home that trying to be impressive can get you killed just as fast as any monster.
**“Moon on the willow-head evening”** – Lü Xiucai’s mangled poetry line is a garbled version of a famous verse from Ouyang Xiu’s *Shengcha Zi·Yuanxi*, a classic Song Dynasty lyric about a lover’s tryst on the Lantern Festival night. His butchered delivery is both funny and a bit sad—he knows culture is a status marker but has absolutely no idea what it means.

### Blowgun (吹箭)

A simple ranged weapon using a hollow tube to launch darts or needles; in Chinese folk performance, it is sometimes used as a theatrical trick.

## Reading Guide

Look, after the last arc’s sheer misery, we *need* this chapter. Gouwa and Lü Xiucai are perfectly matched as comic relief: one is a schemer with no game, the other is a pretentious idiot with one line of bad poetry. The slow zoom into Gouwa’s “divine ability” fail is masterfully played—it starts as lighthearted boasting and ends in a choked gag. But don’t let the laughs fool you. This chapter subtly reinforces one of the novel’s core rules: everyone is desperately performing, and every performance has a price. Gouwa isn’t just choking on a needle; he’s choking on his own hunger to matter.
