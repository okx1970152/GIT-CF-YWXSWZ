## Chapter Overview
Alright, folks, strap in. This chapter is a masterclass in dread-by-atmosphere. No monsters jump out from the shadows (yet). Instead, Li Huowang’s group descends into a cave that feels bottomless, a descent into the earth that is as much psychological as it is physical. The horror is slow, creeping, and built on the uncanny—a child’s nightmare location revisited by an adult who knows the world is far more dangerous than he ever imagined. Prepare for a reveal that will make you look at sheep in a whole new, deeply unsettling light.

## Key Plot Points
- The group continues their descent into the cave where Sun Baolu’s friend disappeared. Li Huowang spots adult footprints, a sign that someone besides a lost child has been here.
- Sun Baolu’s fear is palpable; he warns them that his friend vanished around the next bend. The group is tense, but finds only two dead sheep when they turn the corner.
- Li Huowang, ever cautious, destroys the sheep carcasses to prevent them from being a threat, then leads the group deeper into the widening cave.
- They encounter a massive sea of dead sheep—hundreds of them. Li Huowang deduces this is not random death; someone drove the flock here to die on purpose.
- The trail of corpses leads to a colossal bronze *ding* (a ritual cauldron), surrounded by jade plates and the bones of sacrificial animals. Sun Baolu confirms this cauldron was not here during his childhood.
- The chapter climaxes with a visceral body-horror reveal: the “sheep” in the deeper, darker part of the cave are not sheep at all. They are grotesque, twisted half-human, half-sheep hybrids.
- Li Huowang has a chilling epiphany: every single sheep corpse they saw was once a human being. Someone has transformed over a thousand people into sheep and slaughtered them as a sacrifice.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The *Ding* (鼎) as a Ritual Symbol:** In ancient China, the *ding* was not just a cooking pot. It was the paramount ritual vessel, a symbol of state power and a direct conduit to the gods and ancestors. The famous "Nine Tripods" (九鼎) of the Xia dynasty were legendary symbols of imperial legitimacy. Finding a massive *ding* in a forbidden cave is a huge red flag—it implies a ritual of immense importance and power, but one that is twisted and forgotten, far from the grand ancestral halls it belongs in. Li Huowang's immediate mental link between the *ding* and a sacrifice is a good example of his growing, world-wise intuition.

**The “Human Transformation” Horror:** The central terror of this chapter—turning people into animals for sacrifice—has dark roots in folklore. While not common in orthodox practice, the concept of a malevolent sorcerer or deity “changing the form” of victims to debase or consume them is a recurring folk-horror motif. It’s the ultimate act of dehumanization. A person becomes a sheep, is led to slaughter among the flock, and dies thinking with a sheep’s brain, unaware of their own humanity. The *visceral* wrongness of the half-formed hybrids—the old man’s face pierced by horns—is what makes this horror land so hard. It suggests the transformation wasn’t quick or clean, but a slow, agonizing corruption of the flesh and soul.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is pure setup, but it's *perfect* setup. Don't expect a fight. Expect a slow, sinking dread. Pay attention to Li Huowang’s almost obsessive caution—destroying the dead sheep, checking for traps. This is a man who has learned that the world is eager to kill you if you take it for granted. The final reveal of the herd of human-sheep is the kind of body horror that this novel does best: it’s not about gore for the sake of gore, but about the systematic, ritualistic degradation of what it means to be human. The question now isn't “what monster lives here?” The question is “what kind of god accepts a sacrifice of a thousand souls?”
