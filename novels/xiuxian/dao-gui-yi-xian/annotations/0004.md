## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang continues to test the boundaries between his hospital bed and the cave world, using the jade pendant as a trophy that crosses over. After escaping hostile disciples by waking up, he returns with newfound confidence, dismissing everyone as "NPCs." But Danyangzi's brutal punishment of escapees—forcing disciples to witness a black cauldron that houses a tentacled, flesh-eating entity—shatters Li Huowang's composure. For the first time, he doubts whether the world is merely a hallucination. He resorts to self-therapy techniques taught by Dr. Li, anchoring himself to the thought of Yang Na, and manages to steady his nerves. The chapter juxtaposes sterile hospital reality with visceral, bloody ritual horror, deepening the central ambiguity.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang uses the transition to the hospital to escape the hostile disciples who threatened to kill him for tipping off escapees. He discovers the jade pendant has crossed over and feels invincible.
- Back in the cave world, he returns to the communal bunk and waits for daylight, mocking the other disciples as "NPCs."
- A Daoist messenger announces Danyangzi's order to gather at Huian Hall. Li Huowang notices that both Xuanyang and Danyangzi's direct disciples are missing.
- Danyangzi leads the assembled disciples to a damp cave where the escapees, including his own disciples, are tied up. He opens the ground to reveal a deep pit containing a large black cauldron.
- The captive escapees fall into the pit; muffled screams and tearing flesh sounds follow. Li Huowang sees a writhing black mass with a fleshy texture and short quivering black tentacles snatch a disciple next to him.
- The other disciples panic, but Li Huowang stands close enough to observe the entity clearly, recognizing it as something not of this world.
- He experiences a moment of genuine terror, wondering if this world might be real and if death here would be permanent.
- Using Dr. Li's self-reassurance techniques, he repeats to himself that it is all a hallucination and that Yang Na is waiting for him, regaining enough calm to face the horror with a composed demeanor.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The black cauldron (鼎, ding) is a central ritual vessel in Chinese alchemy and statecraft. Traditionally used for cooking offerings or refining elixirs, it symbolizes authority and transformation. Here, Danyangzi perverts this sacred object into a prison for a grotesque, tentacled being that consumes victims—a literal inversion of alchemical sublimation. Danyangzi's performance of "deeply pained" speech while displaying murderous fury mirrors the hypocrisy of power in many xianxia narratives. Li Huowang's self-therapy echoes real-world cognitive behavioral techniques, yet the story undercuts it by never confirming the hallucination's falsehood. The details—slimy tentacles, sickening blood smell, pitch-black pit—are hallmarks of the novel's signature body horror, grounded in Chinese folk-religious imagery.

## Reading Guide

This chapter marks a psychological turning point: Li Huowang evolves from a passive observer to an active manipulator of the hallucination, using the hospital as a safety valve. Yet the black cauldron scene forces him to confront his own vulnerability—the entity's physicality (fleshy texture, tentacles) triggers a primal fear that his usual dismissal fails to contain. Readers should note the contrast between the hospital's sterile night shift (Nurse Wang binge-watching) and the cave's crude ritualism (torchlight, pit, chants). The jade pendant's crossing-over suggests the worlds share a material connection, raising the stakes: if objects can pass, so can death. Li Huowang's final reliance on Yang Na as an emotional anchor reinforces his humanity but also his growing dependence on a figure from the "real" world.

## The Black Cauldron {#black-cauldron}

In traditional Chinese mythology, the cauldron (鼎) is one of the most potent ritual vessels, used for cooking offerings in state ceremonies and Daoist alchemy. The legendary Nine Cauldrons were said to symbolize imperial sovereignty and the mandate of heaven. In the Dao-Twisted World, Danyangzi has corrupted this tradition: instead of refining elixirs, his cauldron houses a living, tentacled horror that feeds on his disciples. The unexpected presence of such a creature reinforces the novel's theme of sacred objects turned to monstrous purposes, blending historical weight with visceral terror.
