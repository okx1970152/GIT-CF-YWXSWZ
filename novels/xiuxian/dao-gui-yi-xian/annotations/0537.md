## Chapter Overview

Forget cosmic gods and flesh Buddhas—this chapter reminds us that in the Dao-Twisted World, the *mundane* is often the cruelest horror. We cross back to Lü Zhuangyuan’s opera troupe, now struggling in the capital, trying to survive by performing spring opera (read: bawdy folk songs). The constabulary shuts them down, fines them, and leaves them grinding their teeth. Meanwhile, Li Huowang makes another trip into Great Qi—and walks straight into a sky-blackening locust swarm. Zhuge Yuan names it plainly: this *is* a Heavenly Calamity. Not a missing Dao or a vanishing corpse, but *hunger*. The quiet gut-punch here is that for the common people, a failed crop is more terrifying than any god-shaped tear in reality. Get ready for a breather chapter that hits harder than most battles.

## Key Plot Points

- Lü Zhuangyuan’s troupe attempts a paid spring opera (the “Eighteen Sighs,” a well-known folk erotica parody) in the capital. They earn a pile of coppers before city constables shut them down, confiscate the take, and scold them for “corrupting public morals.”
- Lü Xiucai and Gouwa return still arguing over their failed search for Li Huowang. Lü Xiucai’s temper flares; Lü Zhuangyuan cools the tension but privately worries how to explain going home empty-handed.
- Luo Juan, Lü Zhuangyuan’s daughter-in-law, points out the stark economics: capital prices are brutal. A single flatbread costs five coppers. The troupe is burning resources fast.
- Gouwa decides to send a pigeon message to Chun Xiaoman, passing the buck on the failed search before it lands on him.
- Li Huowang crosses the fissure into Great Qi, now more practiced with Li Sui’s tentacle-assisted leap. But the first thing he sees is a titanic locust swarm blotting out the sun.
- The swarm strips the landscape bare in half an hour. Zhuge Yuan identifies it as a Heavenly Calamity—not of the supernatural kind, but the agricultural kind that will bring drought, war, and pestilence in its wake.
- Li Huowang offers to help Zhuge Yuan intervene in Great Qi’s crisis. Zhuge Yuan refuses: “This is not your karma.”

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Spring Opera (粉戏, fěn xì)** – This is not just “bawdy theater.” In historical China, opera troupes were often pushed to the margins of society, and spring opera—sexually suggestive or explicit folk performance—was a last resort for starving troupes. The name literally means “powder opera,” a reference to the heavy makeup and suggestive costuming. In the Dao-Twisted World, this detail roots the troupe’s desperation in very real historical class dynamics: they are not just performers, but *outcasts* performing for *outcasts*, under constant threat of the law.

**Heavenly Calamity (天灾, tiān zāi)** – We’ve seen this term applied to missing Daos and vanishing cosmic laws. But here it gets its most grounded meaning: a locust plague. In Chinese folk and xianxia tradition, Heavenly Calamities can be natural disasters sent by heaven as punishment for a dynasty’s loss of the Mandate of Heaven. Zhuge Yuan’s fear—that drought, war, and plague will follow—is not paranoia; it’s a classic four-stage collapse pattern that marks a full dynastic crisis. The *cosmic* horror is that Great Qi’s rulers are too deep in their own supernatural mess to care about starving farmers.

**Locust Plague Logic** – Locust swarms in historical China were devastating: a single swarm could contain tens of billions of locusts, stripping fields in hours. The horror here is not supernatural but *inevitable*—a famine chain reaction that no amount of sword-fighting can stop. This chapter deliberately grounds the grand “Heavenly Calamity” concept in the most banal, material terror there is: having nothing to eat.

### Mandate of Heaven (天命, tiān mìng)

A traditional Chinese political doctrine that grants a dynasty the right to rule; natural disasters were often interpreted as a sign that the mandate had been lost.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a palate cleanser in the worst way—it swaps body horror for economic horror and cosmic dread for agricultural dread. Watch how the novel uses the *mundane* as a counterpoint: the troupe’s petty squabbles about copper coins hit differently when you realize they’re surviving day-to-day next to Li Huowang’s dimension-hopping god-wrestling. The genius move is that Zhuge Yuan *refuses* Li Huowang’s offer of help. He knows the kid is drowning in his own world. That “no” is heavy—it’s the first time someone has explicitly told Li Huowang that he can’t fix everything, and that trying to do so would be a form of selfishness, not heroism. Also, note how Li Huowang is now comfortable enough with Li Sui to use her tentacles as propulsion. They’re not just father-daughter anymore; they’re a combat team. That’s growth, hidden under locusts and poverty.
