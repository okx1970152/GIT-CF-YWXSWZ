## Chapter Overview

Brace yourselves, fellow Daoists, for a chapter that isn’t about monsters or mad gods, but something far more terrifying: a quiet conversation under fluorescent lights. After the mind-shattering revelations of the Zuowandao and the truth about his own nature, Li Huowang finds himself back in the sterile reality of the White Tower Psychiatric Hospital. But this time, he sees it as vibrant and *real*. This is not a descent into madness; it's a brief, painful oasis of sanity. The chapter is a masterclass in emotional horror, trading blood and viscera for the gut-wrenching realism of a father who has aged years in months and a mother who refuses to leave her son’s side. There are no spells cast, no flesh eaten—only the slow, quiet violence of guilt and love. Get ready for a heavy one, because the monsters here are memory and regret.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang wakes up in his hospital bed, but his perception has fundamentally shifted. He sees the mundane world as beautiful and *real*, a stark contrast to the earlier cognitive collapse.
- A flashback to Hong Zhong’s memories reveals the core truth: a Heart-Element’s innate pneuma is a fragment of the Heavenly Dao. The path to power is no longer about distinguishing true from false, but about willing the ‘false’ he cherishes into reality.
- Sun Xiaoqin enters with his meal, complaining loudly about the two armed guards forced to watch her. The guards’ own weary complaints ground the scene in darkly comedic, bureaucratic realism.
- Li Huowang enjoys a normal meal of winter melon and spare rib soup without any hallucinatory interference, a serene moment of bliss.
- Li Huowang’s father, Li Jiecheng, visits. He appears disheveled, exhausted, and older, carrying a bag of small oranges. The interaction is awkward and full of unspoken pain.
- Li Huowang tries to explain the concept of being a ‘Heart-Element’ to his father, only to realize the chasm between their knowledge is unbridgeable. His father hears it as proof of his illness.
- In a heartbreaking moment of pure paternal devotion, Li Jiecheng tells his son he doesn’t have to come back to reality. He promises to shoulder the burden of the real world, giving Li Huowang permission to stay in his happy dream, even if it means they never truly connect again.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **The White Tower as a Mirror of the Heart**: The ‘White Jade Capital’ of the Siming and the ‘White Tower Psychiatric Hospital’ are not just thematic parallels—they are ontological anchors. In Daoist cosmology, the ‘Jade Capital’ is the celestial seat of the highest gods. Here, the novel twists that concept into a modern, clinical asylum. The pristine white walls of the hospital are a perfect inversion of the chaotic, blood-soaked Dao-Twisted World. Li Huowang’s perception of the hospital as being ‘more real’ marks a critical shift: he is no longer a lost child being pulled between worlds, but a nascent Heart-Element learning to *choose* which world has the greater existential weight.
- **The Subtle Horror of the Parent-Child Bond**: Western narratives often frame a parent’s acceptance of a child’s delusion as a form of enabling or codependency. In Chinese culture, however, the concept of filial piety (孝, xiào) and the profound, sacrificial love of parents (especially fathers who are often the ‘stern’ figure) creates a different, deeper emotional horror. Li Jiecheng’s plea for his son to stay in his happy world is not seen as giving up; it is the ultimate expression of a father’s love. He is offering his own misery and loneliness as a sacrifice so his son can have peace. It is a far more devastating turn than any monster’s attack.
- **Small Symbols of a Hard Life**: The small details in this chapter are masterfully deployed to illustrate a family in quiet crisis. The father’s too-large clothes, the yellowed collar, the unshaved stubble, the crumpled *dry* cigarette pack (signifying he already thought he had quit), and the use of an orange peel as an ashtray—all of these details paint a picture of a man who is slowly unraveling under the weight of his son’s illness. This is the true ‘cost’ of Li Huowang’s supernatural journey, paid not in lifespan, but in the happiness of the people he loves.

## Reading Guide

This is a ‘breather episode’ of the most painful kind. There are no power-ups, no new techniques to learn, no strategies to devise. The pacing is deliberately slow, forcing you to sit in the same awkward silence that Li Huowang experiences with his father. Pay close attention to the dialogue, not for hidden lore, but for the subtext of every unspoken sentence. The guards’ complaints about their salary are a brilliant way to anchor the scene in a reality so mundane it feels alien. And the game of mahjong Li Huowang plays in his mind—with tiles like ‘North Wind’ and ‘Hong Zhong’—is a not-so-subtle hint that his perspective has forever changed. He is now playing a game of the gods, viewing his own human life as just another tile on the board. The true horror isn't the monsters in the other world; it’s that his father is bravely fighting a losing battle in *this* one.

## Deeper Dive: The Alchemy of the Modern Reality

This chapter is a perfect example of how *Dao Gui Yi Xian* weaponizes its dual-world structure. The ‘modern reality’ is not just a source of contrast; it is an alchemical vessel itself. The sterile environment, the rational doctors, the bureaucratic red tape of the prison—these are the ‘mundane ingredients’ being combined. Li Huowang’s newly discovered ‘innate pneuma’ (一个心素，这是他的命) is the catalyst. He is no longer a victim of the Dao-Twisted World, but a nascent alchemist who can choose which cauldron to refine himself in. The tragedy is that for him to succeed, the people he loves must remain in the dark, burning themselves up to keep a little light on for him.
