## Chapter Overview
Get ready, because this chapter is a gut-punch. The long-feared consequence of the Purple-Tasseled Sword’s *sha qi* has finally arrived, and it strikes not Li Huowang but the person he cares about most. The Bai Lingmiao we know is gone, replaced by someone who laughs at violence, craves blood, and refuses to be saved. This is not a subtle corruption—it’s a complete rewrite of her personality, and it forces Li Huowang to confront a devastating question: If he has changed so much himself, who is he to decide what “the real her” should be? Buckle up; this one hurts.

## Key Plot Points
- The chapter opens with Bai Lingmiao laughing uncontrollably after killing the Heart-Turbid. She stabs Li Huowang with the Purple-Tasseled Sword, opening his chest to the open air.
- She presses against him, drinking in his blood, and then kisses him. Her behavior oscillates between manic excitement and defiant mockery.
- Bai Lingmiao explicitly rejects any attempt to “cure” her, stating she feels more free and alive than ever. She calls Li Huowang out for his own denial about having changed.
- Li Huowang’s internal parasites (Li Sui’s tentacles) react protectively when Bai Lingmiao’s nails threaten his heart, but he commands them to stand down.
- The rest of the group arrives—Tuoba Danqing cracks a crude joke, His Honor the Chief Recorder dismisses the topic to move on.
- During the night around the campfire, Bai Lingmiao teases Li Huowang about sex, then argues that change is not inherently bad. She challenges his core identity: “If the old Li Huowang was Li Huowang, then isn’t the current Li Huowang still Li Huowang?”
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang silently resolving to find a way to change her back, while being haunted by her words.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Sha Qi (煞气) and Weapon Corruption**: The “killing intent” or “malevolent aura” of weapons in xianxia isn’t just a passive tag—it’s a living, corrupting force. The Purple-Tasseled Sword’s *sha qi* doesn’t simply empower the wielder; it actively rewrites their personality, amplifying aggression, recklessness, and bloodlust. This is a recurring horror in the genre: cultivation tools demand a price, and the strongest ones always extract it from your soul. Lü Xiucai fell to it earlier; now Bai Lingmiao has followed. The tragedy lies in her own words: she doesn’t feel corrupted—she feels *free*.

**The Question of Self in Xianxia**: This chapter dramatizes a core philosophical tension in Chinese cultivation fiction. Is “self” a fixed essence that can be preserved through tribulation, or is it a continuous process that evolves—and sometimes breaks—with every trial? Bai Lingmiao’s argument is deeply provocative: if Li Huowang can mutilate himself, see ghosts, and act in ways no “normal” man would, why does *her* change count as a loss of self, while his qualifies as survival? The novel refuses to offer a comfortable answer, forcing both the protagonist and the reader to sit with the discomfort.

## Reading Guide
This is a chapter that will split readers. The transformation of Bai Lingmiao is unsettling because it strips away the soft, nurturing presence that balanced Li Huowang’s chaos. Some will see it as a thrilling evolution into a more proactive character; others will mourn the loss of her original warmth. Both reactions are valid, and the novel leverages that tension. Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s posture: he is not angry, not disgusted—he is *guilty*. He blames himself, and Bai Lingmiao’s sharpness exposes his hypocrisy. Her final question is the thematic dagger: can you accept that you have changed, but demand that others stay the same? That’s the real horror of this chapter, and it’s not supernatural at all.

## Thematic Deep Dive: The Price of a Sword
The Purple-Tasseled Sword is not the first weapon to corrupt its bearer in this story, and it won’t be the last. In xianxia tradition, high-grade weapons are often sentient or semi-sentient; they have their own karma, their own appetites. To wield a “military path” (兵家) blade is to enter into a relationship with a predator. Bai Lingmiao’s transformation is the novel’s way of literalizing the old adage: “The sword is hungry.” The tragedy is that the prey doesn’t always realize they’ve been eaten.
