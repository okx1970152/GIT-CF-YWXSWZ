## Chapter Overview

We knew this confrontation couldn't end well, but nobody predicted the sheer brutality of the pivot. Li Huowang walks into the Bai family's trap with the best of intentions—only to walk out with half a hand, a ruptured stomach, and a psychotic break that hurls him back into modern reality. The chapter title, “The Stomach Rupture,” is meticulously literal: once the Black Tai Sui inside him is damaged, his grip on reality dissolves faster than the rain soaking his vagrant disguise. What makes this chapter especially cruel is that Li Huowang’s restraint—his refusal to kill Bai Lingmiao’s family—is what gets him pinned like a butterfly to a board. The Bai family doesn’t know (or care) that he was trying to protect them. They only see the Supervisory Heavenly Office token and react with a century of accumulated hatred. This isn’t a fight. It’s an execution. citeturn1view0

## Key Plot Points

The episode begins with Li Huowang’s cold tactical retreat after realizing the Bai family’s resolve is steel-hard. But the reed marsh is cultivated territory—Bai Sai’s earlier warning about getting lost wasn’t a courtesy; it was a setup. Hidden archers and pre-stashed weapons confirm that the Bai family had already chosen violence before Li Huowang even arrived. First the giant arrows, then the Wandering Lords tracking him by blood trail. Li Huowang tries the honorable exit: neutralizing the threat without killing Bai Lingmiao’s relatives. But when Bai Lingmiao’s father swings a saber, Li Huowang raises a hand instead of a blade—and loses half a palm. The spears that pin him to the ground are the physical punctuation of his failed diplomacy. Everyone’s indifference is the real knife: Bai Lingmiao’s mother accusing him of being an “imperial dog” shows that this is not personal hatred but inherited, systemic rage. The chapter’s violent pivot—from his tortured body in the reeds to a trash bin, a rotten orange, and a rain-swept street—is the moment his suppressed hallucinations reclaim control. citeturn1view0

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Wandering Lord.** In Daoist-influenced folk sorcery, the *Wandering Lord* is a summoned entity used for reconnaissance and material gathering. In this chapter, the entity’s tracking logic is brutally simple: it follows the blood trail. That’s not a supernatural trick but a mundane predator’s instinct, which makes it all the more terrifying—the monster isn’t breaking any rules, just exploiting a very biological one. citeturn3view0turn3view4turn2view0

**The “imperial dog” charge.** The Bai family’s hatred for the “imperial dogs” (朝廷狗) roots itself in the historical memory of Chinese secret societies (like the White Lotus) that were violently suppressed by imperial governments. The Supervisory Heavenly Office functions as a supernatural police force that would have persecuted folk practitioners. From their perspective, Li Huowang’s token doesn’t identify an ally—it identifies the enemy that burnt their ancestors’ temples. citeturn10view0turn9view0

**Psychiatric realism in hallucination breaks.** The transition from a torturous abduction to a city street is brutal but clinically accurate: a sudden, disorienting shift with physical props (the rotten orange) that bridge both worlds. The Black Tai Sui inside Li Huowang’s stomach acts as a pharmacological anchor—once damaged, the hallucinogenic floodgates open. The novel treats his schizophrenia not as a gimmick but as a physical vulnerability tied to visceral body horror. citeturn1view0

### Suo-yuan (索元)

A ritual item of torture/investigation from the Thousand Greats Record; when activated, it grows spikes to impale the interrogated from within, used here as a threat to the Bai family.

### Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司)

A government-backed supernatural police force operating under the imperial board of war; they prioritize political stability over saving individual lives, earning them deep hatred from rural folk cults.

## Reading Guide

I need you to sit with the nausea this chapter leaves behind—not just the gore, but the betrayal. Li Huowang genuinely tried to extend an olive branch. He held back. He remembered Bai Lingmiao. And it was that very act of remembering that cost him his hand and nearly his life. The Bai family is not evil in the cartoonish sense; they are traumatized, and trauma has a long memory. What makes this chapter hurt is the “what if”: what if Li Huowang had shown them the Wandering Lord summoning bell first? What if Bai Lingmiao were there to translate his intentions? But the Dao-Twisted World doesn’t run on “what ifs.” It runs on blood debt and old grudges.
