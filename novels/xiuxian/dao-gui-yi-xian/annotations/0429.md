## Chapter Overview
Alright, strap in, folks, because Chapter 428 of *Dao Gui Yi Xian* just ripped the bandage off—and the wound is a battlefield. The capital is sealed, the Supervisory Heavenly Office has declared open season on the Zuowandao, and the bounty is so insane it makes the treasure of a thousand years look like pocket change. But the real monster here isn't the enemy. It's the order itself: “Better to kill a thousand innocents than let a single one escape.” This isn't a hunt. It's a pogrom. And Li Huowang, our favorite mentally-tortured reality-bender, is forced to sit in the front row and watch the whole city burn while his own conscience screams louder than the dying.

## Key Plot Points
- The Deputy Supervisor of the Heavenly Office announces a city-wide purge of the Zuowandao, with staggering bounties: 50 Yang-Life Pills for a common head, 300 and a three-grade promotion for a “San Yuan” or “Si Xi,” and the grand prize—180 years of pure Yang-Life from the Grand Supervisor himself.
- The four great gates of the capital slam shut. Soldiers line the walls with siege weapons turned inward. The city is locked down.
- Li Huowang heads to his assigned sector on horseback. As he rides, he witnesses the horrifying consequences of the order: homes are broken into, families are slaughtered in the street by both sides.
- A particularly brutal moment: a woman is stabbed by her own baby—a quick-witted Zuowandao imposter—who uses her as a shield and escapes.
- Li Huowang arrives at a target house. He can't bring himself to kill a terrified family. Instead, he cuts the wife's face open to check for a mask underneath—a brutal, methodical test. She's clean. He leaves silver, but the existential dread remains.
- From a rooftop, Li Huowang sees the capital transformed into a living hell. He screams at the city, demanding to know what human life means to the warring parties. But even as he rages, he feels powerless.
- Jin Shan Zhao (half his body gone) weeps blood. The monk chants futile sutras. Hong Zhong urges action. The headless Peng Longteng stands silent.
- Three new people, chased by Heavenly Office agents, mistake Li Huowang for their “Boss Hong Zhong” and shield him with their lives, showing how deep the chaos and misidentification have spread.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
- **Yang-Life Pills & The Bounty System**: The idea of trading life-extending elixirs for enemy heads is a brutal twist on the classic “bounty hunting” trope in xianxia. Here, it’s not just wealth, but raw lifespan—the most precious currency in a world where cultivation is about transcending mortality. The Grand Supervisor’s “pure” Yang-Life is especially coveted because it bypasses the risk of becoming a “Renxiao,” the monstrous creatures that ordinary long-lived people can degenerate into. This is a masterful use of the novel’s own grotesque internal logic.
- **“Better to Kill a Thousand Innocents”**: This phrase is a chilling inversion of a well-known Chinese legal maxim (宁枉勿纵) that was historically used to justify harsh measures against rebellion. It inverts the principle of “better to let a thousand guilty go free than to wrong one innocent,” a concept found in many cultures. In the Dao-Twisted World, it’s a direct ordinance for atrocity, illustrating how the system itself has become corrupted.
- **The Check Under the Skin**: Li Huowang’s method of cutting a victim’s face to peel back the skin is a terrifyingly practical test for Zuowandao infiltration. It’s a grotesque inversion of the folk belief that demons or shape-shifters can be revealed through physical tests—except here, the test itself is a brutal act of violence. It perfectly captures the novel’s horror: the only way to verify reality is through self-mutilation or harming others.
- **Door Gods (门神)**: The mention of “Door Gods” on the shattered door is a subtle but powerful detail. In Chinese folk religion, door gods are protective deities affixed to doors to ward off evil spirits during the Lunar New Year. Seeing these guardians impotent as a family is destroyed within their own home reinforces that in the Dao-Twisted World, even the most sacred protections are meaningless.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is the thematic heart of the entire “Siege of the Capital” arc. It’s not a battle chapter; it’s a *consequence* chapter. Every explosion, every scream, every body in the snow is the physical manifestation of the order, “Better to kill a thousand innocents.” If you’ve been waiting for Li Huowang to finally face the moral cost of the world he’s been dragged into, this is it. Pay close attention to his two moments of stillness: the first, in the house with the terrified family, and the second, on the rooftop. Both show a man who is *trying* to hold onto a shred of his humanity even as the world around him demands he become a monster. The silent gesture of the three strangers who sacrifice themselves for “Boss Hong Zhong” is the ultimate tragic irony—they die believing a lie, a death that has no meaning other than being collateral damage in a god’s cruel game. Get ready for the inevitable splintering of Li Huowang’s soul.

## New Stares into the Abyss
What’s especially cruel about this chapter is the unbearable weight of empathy. Li Huowang screams at the city, “They are people! Living, breathing people!” But he knows he can’t save them. The narrative traps the reader in that excruciating space between moral outrage and utter impotence. The author uses the panorama from the rooftop not just as a visual, but an emotional weapon—showing the reader the full scale of the tragedy, while simultaneously making Li Huowang feel smaller and more helpless with every passing second.
