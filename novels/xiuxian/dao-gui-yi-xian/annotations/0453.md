## Chapter Overview
Li Huowang tries to juggle two lives—and fails gracefully at both. In the Dao-Twisted World, he’s handing out military cultivation manuals like party favors and fretting over his wife’s stubbed toe. In the modern world, he’s trapped in a psychiatric prison, arguing with hospital staff through his mother and staring at a night-vision camera like it holds the answer to everything. This chapter is a quiet, character-driven breather, but the tension between the two realities is palpable: one world is a chaotic village he’s slowly building, the other is a well-guarded cage he can’t talk his way out of.

## Key Plot Points
- Gao Zhijian asks Li Huowang to train the military text with him, but Li Huowang declines, noting his own cultivation and Zuowandao powers now outclass it.
- Li Huowang suggests Gao Zhijian teach the military technique to the village’s tenant farmers, hoping to build a small guard force for White Family Village.
- Bai Lingmiao approves of the plan and shows she’s been integrating into the community; Li Huowang notices she’s limping, but she brushes it off.
- Li Huowang heads to Ox-Heart Mountain to switch to the modern world, leaving Li Sui on watch. He sees a field of fresh graves but isn’t fazed.
- In the modern world, Li Huowang hallucinates Sun Xiaoqin’s face in a scene of tender domestic care, but the illusion breaks when he hears her arguing with a staff member about getting him released.
- The staff member flatly refuses, citing Li Huowang’s violent potential and labeling him a permanent danger, no matter how much money his mother offers.
- Li Huowang ends the chapter staring at the night-vision camera, quietly trying to figure out the legal and practical path to freedom.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Military cultivation (兵家)** is one of the classic schools of thought from the pre-Qin “Hundred Schools” era. In xianxia, it often represents body-tempering, killing-intent-based combat arts that rely on martial formations and group synergy rather than individual enlightenment. Li Huowang’s observation that it’s “meant for an army of thousands” is spot on: military cultivators are essentially shock troops whose power scales with numbers and discipline, not solo breakthroughs.
**Private slaves (私奴)** in historical Chinese society were not “slaves” in the transatlantic sense. They were bonded laborers with specific legal and social status, often tied to land or households. Their children could sometimes be freed, and families frequently retained significant agency within their master’s estate—as Bai Lingmiao points out, even a master needs to “buy their loyalty” to keep the system stable.
**Psychiatric hospitals in modern China** follow a national legal framework that emphasizes public safety before patient rights. The staff member’s threat about killing seven students is a brutally direct expression of the state’s fear of mentally ill individuals who have a documented history of violence—a very real and very current issue that the novel doesn’t shy away from portraying.

## Reading Guide
This is a rare “sandwich chapter”: two layers of reality pressed together, held together by the sticky bread of Li Huowang’s self-loathing. Watch how the modern-world hospital scene mirrors the Dao-Twisted World’s powerlessness—Li Huowang can command a village and teach martial arts on one side, but on the other, he can’t even make a phone call without permission. That cognitive dissonance is the real horror here. Also, pay close attention to Bai Lingmiao’s limp. It’s the only unspoken beat in the chapter, and in this story, unspoken beats tend to grow teeth.

## Worldbuilding Note: The Ox-Heart Mass Grave
The hundreds of fresh graves on Ox-Heart Mountain aren’t just spooky set dressing. This is likely the aftermath of the Bai family’s failed defense against the Wandering Lord or some other mass casualty event the village has already buried and moved on from. Li Huowang’s deliberate indifference to it (“he had yet to actually see a ghost”) is a disturbing sign of his desensitization—he no longer registers mass death as abnormal. In a folk-horror world where ghosts are real, a man who ignores a graveyard might be the most dangerous thing in it.
