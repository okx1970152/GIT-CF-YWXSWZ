## Chapter Overview

This chapter delivers a bittersweet, twisted resolution to Gao Zhijian’s arc. After Li Huowang reunites with Li Sui, he finds his simple-minded junior brother and reveals the staggering truth: Gao Zhijian isn’t just a former imperial guard—he’s the last true heir to the throne of Great Liang, and the only one who can restart its dynasty. What should be a glorious coronation is undercut by the visceral horror of Ji Lin’s fate. She gave up everything—her family, her humanity, her sanity—to become a monster fit for the throne, only to have it stripped away the moment she was no longer needed. Her suicide is a brutal, heartbreaking punctuation mark on the chapter’s theme: the throne is not a reward, but a poison that consumes everyone who touches it.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang finds Li Sui and Gao Zhijian in Shangjing, surprising them both with his timely arrival. He reveals the entire truth about Gao Zhijian’s identity as the legitimate emperor of Great Liang and plans for his ascension.
- Gao Zhijian, despite his stammer and simple nature, accepts the mantle with surprising resolve. His motivation is not power but protection: he wants to be strong enough that no one will ever dare bully Chun Xiaoman or his friends again.
- The coronation procession is detailed with eerie grandeur. Gao Zhijian’s posture and demeanor shift as he approaches the throne, suggesting the identity itself remakes the man.
- The Grand Preceptor, Huangfu Tiangang, confronts Li Huowang with suspicion and hostility, blaming him for the decimation of the Liang royal family. Their tension is interrupted by Gao Zhijian’s new authority.
- Ji Lin’s final scene is devastating. She attempts to attack Gao Zhijian, screaming that she did everything asked of her—murdering her own family—only to be betrayed. After being rejected, she takes her own life on the white marble stairs.
- Li Huowang comforts her in her final moments, but his kindness cannot undo the trap she was placed in. The chapter ends with his quiet acknowledgment of her death.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Mandate of Heaven (天命 / 气运) as a Parasitic Burden** — In this chapter, the throne is not a symbol of divine favor but a gruesome inheritance. The novel inverts the traditional Chinese concept of the “Mandate of Heaven” (the belief that a dynasty rules by heavenly will) into a visceral, transactional curse. The earlier lore established that the Liang royal line must continuously kill off its own members until only one remains—a “royal bug-fighting” method to determine the next bearer of the Mandate. This perverts the classical idea of virtuous dynastic succession into a bloody, mechanical process. Ji Lin’s final words—“I killed my mother, I killed my sister, I killed my whole family! I became so evil!”—are a direct indictment of this system. The throne is not something you *deserve*; it is something that *consumes* you.

**Dragon Vein (龙脉) Succession Across Realms** — The Grand Preceptor’s exasperated line, “We had to seek out a man from Great Qi to continue the dragon vein,” is a fascinating collision of parallel-world lore. In Chinese geomancy and political cosmology, the Dragon Vein is an underground energy channel that determines a dynasty’s fortune and legitimacy. Here, the destruction of Great Liang’s own royal line has forced the empire to borrow an heir from a different world entirely. This is a profound cosmological loophole: Gao Zhijian’s “Dragon Vein” originates from Great Qi, yet he is being installed as the emperor of Great Liang. The chapter treats this not as a triumphant fusion of worlds but as a desperate, last-ditch act of repair.

### Mandate of Heaven (气运)

In this novel's context, the Mandate is a stolen fragment of divine power that holds up reality; it can only be supported by a family willing to commit self-slaughter, making it a curse as much as a blessing.

## Reading Guide

Buckle up, fellow readers. This chapter is what happens when a “destined emperor” arc meets a cosmic horror story that *hates* the very idea of power as a reward. Gao Zhijian finally gets the answer to his lifelong question: he’s not worthless; he’s the last emperor of a broken lineage. But his reaction is perfect. He doesn’t swell with pride or make a grand speech. He just wants to be strong enough so that no one bullies Chun Xiaoman. That’s it. That’s the whole motivation. It’s so pure it hurts.

And then there’s Ji Lin. Her crash-out is the emotional gut-punch of the chapter. We first met her on a lighter, more hopeful note during the Shangsi Festival—a young woman trying to survive a game she was forced to play. Now she’s a hollow, screaming wreck who murdered her entire family for a throne that was never hers to keep. Her suicide on the white steps isn’t just a tragedy; it’s the thesis statement of the novel. In the Dao-Twisted World, the higher you climb, the more it costs you—and sometimes, the price is everything you are.

Pay attention to how Li Huowang handles this. He doesn’t pity Gao Zhijian or try to talk him out of it. He lays the truth flat, watches his brother walk into the fire, and then quietly offers Ji Lin a hand when no one else will. He’s becoming a different kind of leader—one who carries the weight of his choices without flinching.
