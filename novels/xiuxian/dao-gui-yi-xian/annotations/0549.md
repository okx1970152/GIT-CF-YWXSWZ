## Chapter Overview
Chapter 548, “The Supervisory Heavenly Office,” is a masterclass in psychological horror through bureaucratic *reasonableness*. After the visceral battle of the previous chapters, Li Huowang finally gets his audience with the highest authority of the Supervisory Heavenly Office—but nothing is as straightforward as a plea for help. The Superintendent doesn't appear as an ally; he appears as an incomprehensible, reality-bending question mark who treats Li Huowang's desperate vengeance quest like a minor work assignment. The chapter pivots on a single, devastating twist: the entire conversation was an illusion. Li Huowang didn't walk into the Superintendent's camp; he was caught in a spell so seamless that he couldn't tell the difference between negotiation and hypnosis. And yet, a deal was still struck. It’s the kind of horror that doesn't come from a monster, but from realizing that a power so great it can fabricate entire conversations exists—and that it's on your side only as long as it’s convenient. Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this is how you do cosmic political horror.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang, surrounded by Office agents, demands to speak to the Superintendent directly, claiming his reflection will prove his truthfulness.
- The Superintendent finally reveals himself: an unnaturally tall, broad figure wrapped in a red Daoist robe with flowing, alien crimson characters.
- Li Huowang pitches his case: he can find the Dice, but he needs the Superintendent's power to finish the job.
- The Superintendent agrees in principle but says he has "more important things" to do first, ordering Li Huowang to infiltrate the Upper Pole Pouring-Pass again and bring back its secrets as a down payment.
- Before Li Huowang can question the logistics of this arrangement, the scene dissolves. He is left standing outside the Upper Capital’s gate, having never actually left.
- Zhuge Yuan confirms: the Superintendent’s “art of illusion” fabricated the entire conversation, down to the carriage tracks in the mud.
- Li Huowang processes the chilling implication: he was completely at the Superintendent’s mercy without even knowing it. But since the man agreed to the alliance, he resolves to trust the deal and immediately sets out to search the Upper Pole Pouring-Pass again.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Art of Illusion (幻化之术):** In xianxia and Chinese folk magic, “illusion arts” (幻术) are a standard power—but rarely at this tier. This isn't a parlor trick. The Superintendent created a full sensory world in real-time, with dialogue, physical sensation, terrain, and a timeline. This is the level of cultivation where the borders between “internal imagination” and “external reality” have become purely optional. For a Heart-Element like Li Huowang, that’s a terrifying mirror.

**Superintendent (司天监):** The head of the Supervisory Heavenly Office isn’t a bureaucrat with a stamp. The title *Sitian Jian* historically involved observing celestial omens, but in this novel, it implies a figure who *measures* heaven—who can reach into the fabric of reality itself. His red robe with flowing characters is a visual callback to the corrupted scriptures and forbidden texts of the Dao-Twisted World, marking him as a being who reads the world as if it were a book.

**The Translation of Strange Characters:** Li Huowang notes the characters on the robe are stranger than Nüshu (女书), a genuinely historical script used exclusively by women in parts of Hunan. This specific reference grounds the horror: the Superintendent isn't babbling nonsense; he's using *a complete, illegible system of meaning*, which is far more unsettling. It suggests a civilization of language that exists just beyond human cognition.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a *breather chapter in a dread suit*. There's no combat, no monster, no blood. But the conversation is one of the most unnerving in the arc, because the Superintendent treats Li Huowang’s grand revenge quest—the thing he’s sacrificed everything for—as a minor side task. The horror here isn’t a knife; it’s a polite smile and a deadline. Pay close attention to the way the Superintendent’s dialogue doesn’t have a gender or age—it’s a void speaking through a form. And that line, “You should be waking up,” lands like a sledgehammer because it reveals that Li Huowang was never in control of the scene. He was a dreamer inside someone else’s dream. If you’re a fan of horror that works through *tone* and *implication* rather than gore, this is your chapter. It also sets up a new arc: Li Huowang heading back to the heart of the mystery, not as a victim this time, but as an agent of a power he doesn’t fully understand.
