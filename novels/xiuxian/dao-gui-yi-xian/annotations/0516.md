## Chapter Overview

Alright, buckle up, because Li Huowang just decided that the "diplomatic" way of doing politics is for suckers. After a test-run assassination attempt that leaves him riddled with swords (and it barely phases him anymore), he crashes a gathering of the Emperor's so-called "own people"—a collection of useless sycophants, corrupt nobles, and pretty-faced schemers who think they're playing a game of court chess. Li Huowang's response? He pulls the one card they never saw coming: the brutal, wholly literal application of raw power. By the end, it's less "dinner party" and more "bloody reminder of who actually does the killing."

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang's ability to Cultivate the False has changed the power dynamics with his hallucinations; he can now give them physical form, making them vulnerable and subject to his will—a significant shift from his previous helplessness.
- He leaves Li Sui to guard the house and goes outside the city to practice this newfound power, realizing only a Heart-Element can wield it.
- On his way to the gathering, his sedan is ambushed and stabbed through by three blades. He survives and arrives at the mansion, immediately destroying a stone lion and wall with his sword as an entrance.
- Dong Zhongshou introduces his "own people": a collection of pampered nobles, a famed calligrapher, and a fellow strategist—all of whom look down on Li Huowang.
- Li Huowang realizes these people are all "consumers" of strategy and bloodshed, not "producers." They talk, plan, and enjoy the spoils, but none of them do the actual killing.
- In frustration, he calls out their uselessness. When Dong Zhongshou tries to smooth things over with a performative toast, Li Huowang finally snaps.
- He materializes Peng Longteng's physical form at the table, and she literally tears Dong Zhongshou apart in front of the entire assembly.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The term "School of Diplomacy" (纵横家, *Zongheng Jia*) refers to a real ancient Chinese philosophical and political school from the Warring States period. Their entire game was about "vertical and horizontal alliances" (合纵连横)—convincing weak states to gang up on a strong one (vertical) or convincing a strong one to ally with a weaker one to pick apart the other weak states (horizontal). Think Machiavelli meets ancient Chinese backroom politics. The fact that Dong Zhongshou and his ilk are described exclusively as "strategists" and "planners" tells you everything about the world's hierarchy: the *thinkers* eat first, and the *doers* (like a certain sword-happy military counselor) are supposed to sit at the back of the table. Li Huowang, of course, violently rejects this order.

The "Skin Cup" (皮儿杯) mentioned is a decadent, historically-grounded practice from ancient feasts where a courtesan would hold wine in her mouth, and the drinker would sip it directly from her lips. It's the kind of grotesque, sensual opulence that screams "decadent aristocracy who have forgotten that real power depends on steel, not silk."

## Reading Guide

This is a pivotal chapter for Li Huowang's transition from "survivor" to "kingmaker." He's no longer playing defense; he's actively deciding who sits at the table. Pay close attention to his frustration—it's not just anger at the insult, but a deep, strategic contempt for people who confuse political maneuvering with actual power. His final act here isn't just rage; it's a terrifyingly clear message: "I am not one of your scheming puppets. I am the knife you forgot to be afraid of." Expect the court to be *very* nervous about their new "Er Military Counselor" from now on.

Also, the side-note about Ji Lin and the "Gu King" is a massive red flag. Someone (likely a high-level player at this very table) is already evaluating the Emperor as a failed weapon. The political intrigue is thickening, and Li Huowang just threw a hammer into the gears.
