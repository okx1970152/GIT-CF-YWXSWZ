## Chapter Overview
In this breather chapter, Li Huowang faces a psychological adversary far more insidious than any demon: the rational, well-meaning psychiatrist. What begins as a stubborn defense of his dual-reality worldview spirals into violent confrontation and a haunting glimpse of his potential future—then abruptly snaps back to the Dao-Twisted World, where the consequences of his absence are weirdly domestic. The true horror here isn't in blood-soaked cults or writhing flesh, but in the quiet, devastating possibility that the Doctor might have a point.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang clashes with psychiatrist Yi Donglai, who systematically dissects his belief system using Li Huowang’s own logic.
- Li Huowang physically attacks Yi Donglai when pressed on why he fears the worlds touching—a violent overreaction that reveals deep-seated vulnerability.
- A patient named Wang Wei—revealed to be Li Huowang’s *former* attending physician, now himself institutionalized—urgently calls out to him.
- Yi Donglai shows his first genuine shock upon learning this; the implication is that a previous doctor fell into the same "madness" he is now treating.
- Li Huowang wakes in the Dao-Twisted World, finding Li Sui has been stuffing steamed buns directly into his stomach through a wound while he was unconscious.
- He learns from Zhuge Yuan that they are still in Great Qi, and that severing the dragon vein there will not harm Great Liang.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The "Rational Horror" at Play**  
This chapter weaponizes a terrifying concept: what if the insanity is *contagious* through logic? Wang Wei, Li Huowang’s former doctor, is now a patient—suggesting that engaging with Li Huowang’s worldview can break a normal person too. This subverts the typical psychiatric-trope where the doctor is always safe; here, the doctor gets infected by the illness he’s trying to treat. It’s a grim nod to how belief systems can become self-validating traps in the Dao-Twisted World.

**Dragon Vein (龙脉)**  
A concept in Chinese geomancy and political cosmology. The dragon vein is the underground energy channel—the lifeblood of a dynasty's fate and legitimacy. Severing or "breaking" a dragon vein is catastrophic for that kingdom, but the effect is localized. Li Huowang’s instinctive worry is not about metaphysics but about *collateral damage*—a sign he is learning to think in geopolitical terms, not just survival.

## Reading Guide
Okay, fellow Daoists of chaos—this one is a mind-bender. Forget the flesh Buddhas for a moment; the real monster here is a guy in a white coat with *facts*. Yi Donglai isn’t a villain, and that’s what makes him terrifying: he’s using Li Huowang’s own rules against him. The moment the doc says “I’m following *your* logic,” he’s already won the argument—because any answer Li Huowang gives proves the Doctor’s point.

The chapter’s emotional gut-punch isn’t the fight, though. It’s the reveal of Wang Wei—the former shrink who *became* a patient. That’s the novel whispering to you: *this madness is communicable by conversation.* Yi Donglai’s shock is the first crack in his clinical armor, and you can almost hear his confidence cracking.

And then we swing back to the filthy, broken world where Li Sui is stuffing buns into Li Huowang’s stomach like a creepy mom-bot. The tonal whiplash is perfect—from sterile psychiatric horror to grotesque domestic comedy in one paragraph. Classic *Dao Guiyi Xian*.

Keep your eyes on the prize: Li Huowang is slowly, stubbornly accreting power and knowledge. The hospital is his safe room. The Dao-Twisted World is his training ground. And his own mind? That’s the boss fight he still can’t see coming.
