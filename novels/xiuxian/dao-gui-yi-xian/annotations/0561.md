## Chapter Overview

Well, well, well. The chase is real now, and the big boys have finally stepped onto the board. Our favorite flesh-and-bone father-daughter duo, Li Huowang and Li Sui, are sprinting through the woods outside the capital, playing a deadly game of cat and mouse with some seriously skilled imperial hunters. Li Huowang's got tricks—Zuowandao decoys, a collarbone divination gambit, and his trusty spine-sword to dip into Great Qi whenever the heat gets too much. But then the sky goes dark, the rift gets *cut in half*, and in walks the Grand Preceptor himself: Huangfu Tiangang, wielding a constellation-long sword and absolutely zero patience for bullshit. This is the chapter where the Emperor's victory becomes official, Li Sui's true nature is called out to her face, and the cosmic cold war between the Grand Preceptor and the Superintendent starts to show its cracks. Get ready—the realpolitik of this twisted dynasty is about to get a lot more personal.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang and Li Sui are being actively hunted by a skilled team of imperial trackers dispatched by the new Emperor, Ji Lin. The pursuit is relentless and methodical, forcing constant relocation.
- Li Huowang uses a Zuowandao illusion technique—a corpse with a face-pasting talisman—as a decoy to buy time, showing his increasing reliance on the sect's tricks for survival.
- He performs a divination using his own collarbone, not to actually get a result, but to *signal* the Superintendent that his task is complete. Desperate, passive-aggressive communication at its finest.
- Shangguan Yuting is confirmed as a tangible illusion tethered to the Superintendent, but her exact nature remains ambiguous, and even the Grand Preceptor is stunned by her existence.
- Huangfu Tiangang confronts Li Huowang directly, bluntly states that Anping (Li Sui's vessel) is dead and that Li Sui is a "evil spirit" inside her skin, exposing the truth that Li Huowang has been trying to hide.
- The Grand Preceptor's response to Dice's conspiracy is markedly calm and indifferent—he is far more interested in the mechanics of Shangguan Yuting's eyes than in the political plot, mirroring the Superintendent's earlier detachment.
- The chapter ends with the imperial pursuers arriving, but they are immediately ordered to stand down by the Grand Preceptor, signaling a shift in the power dynamics of the confrontation.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Grand Preceptor (国师) as Political-Cosmic Authority:** In Chinese imperial cosmology, the *Guoshi* (National Teacher / Grand Preceptor) is not just a senior advisor but often a figure who holds semi-divine or cosmic authority—someone who acts as a bridge between the mortal court and the Heavenly Mandate. Huangfu Tiangang, with his star-woven sword and realm-shaking presence, perfectly embodies this archetype. His ability to *cut a dimensional rift in half* isn't just a flex; it's a demonstration of his metaphysical jurisdiction over the very fabric of the "Great Liang" timeline. When he says "stand down," reality itself obeys.

**Divination via Self-Mutilation (锁骨干/卜卦):** Li Huowang's method of using his own collarbone for a divination spell is a brutal xianxia classic. Flesh-and-bone divination trades on the principle that one's body is the most direct conduit to fate. The pain is the price, the bone is the medium, and the act of *sending a signal* rather than getting an answer is a clever tactical twist on the trope—he's weaponizing the *concept* of divination as a communication device.

**The Void Nian & the "Unspeakable":** The chapter's tension is driven by a being so cosmic it doesn't even appear—the entity that *cut Li Huowang's rift*. This is the *Kong Nian* (Void Nian), a corrupted beast that feeds on yang-life, or perhaps something older: the "Unspeakable" (Shuo Bu De), a void-born entity that cannot be named or written. In Chinese mythology, naming a spirit often gives it power over you. To encounter something that inherently *cannot* be named is to face a horror beyond classification—a truly anti-linguistic, Lovecraftian threat.

**Lies, Mandates, and the Price of a Throne:** A major subtext here is the nature of the "Mandate of Heaven." In this story, it's not a divine gift but a parasitic burden—a stolen fragment of power used to prop up reality itself, maintained only through the self-slaughter of a single family. Ji Lin's victory is hollow because he has inherited a throne built on sacrifice and cosmic debt. Huangfu Tiangang and the Superintendent's indifference to "politics" reveals that they are servants of a much grimmer, more systemic horror than any mortal coup.

### Void Nian (虚年)

A corrupted Nian beast that drains yang life on contact; named to distinguish it from the traditional folkloric Nian.

### Mandate of Heaven (气运)

In this novel’s context, the Mandate is a stolen fragment of divine power that holds up reality; it can only be supported by a family willing to commit self-slaughter, making it a curse as much as a blessing.

## Reading Guide

This is a "power check" chapter. Li Huowang has been running on ingenuity and stolen tools, and now the top-tier NPCs are *looking* at him. Pay close attention to how Huangfu Tiangang doesn't even raise his voice—his threat is absolute and silent. Also note the difference in attitude: the Superintendent (from earlier) was cryptic and slow-moving; the Grand Preceptor is direct and terrifyingly correct. This isn't good news for Li Huowang. His alliance with the Superintendent is the only thing keeping him alive, and even that feels fragile. The fact that Huangfu Tiangang is more interested in *Shangguan Yuting's mechanics* than in Dice's plot tells you everything about the scale these beings are operating on—they are already playing a game that transcends mortal politics.

## Deep Dive: The Wu and the Stolen Mandate

This chapter alludes to a terrifying piece of backstory that deserves its own spotlight. The **Wu (巫)** are ancient, pre-human entities who once *farmed mortals as livestock*. The ancestors of humanity didn't become civilized—they *escaped* a farm, and they did so by stealing a fragment of a god's power. That stolen power is the "Mandate of Heaven" (气运). Every dynasty that upholds it is not being "blessed" by heaven; they are actively holding up a stolen reality, and the price is paid in blood—specifically, the blood of the imperial family, who must commit self-slaughter to maintain the illusion. This reframes everything: the Empress' death, Ji Lin's hollow throne, and the indifference of the Grand Preceptor and the Superintendent. They don't care about who wins because they know *everyone* loses in the end.
