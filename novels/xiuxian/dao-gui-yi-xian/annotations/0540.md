## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, because Chapter 540 is a masterclass in *uncomfortable* horror. We open not with our protagonist, but with Qiu Chibao—a starving woman boiling river stones into “soup” to cure her baby, burning human bones for fuel under a cracked bowl. This isn’t the flashy, grotesque horror of a Flesh Buddha. This is the slow, grinding horror of mundane desperation, of people who have been hollowed out so completely that they can no longer tell the difference between a magic ritual and a delusion. When the Faith followers rise up against the “red-robed Daoist” who killed their shaman, Qiu Chibao charges with a rusted knife, clutching a dead infant she believes is still alive. Li Huowang kills her without a second thought. But the scene lingers. It *sticks* to him, and to us, as a visceral reminder that in the Dao-Twisted World, the line between enemy and victim has been erased.

## Key Plot Points

- The chapter opens through the eyes of Qiu Chibao, a starving mother boiling river stones to make “stone soup” for her sick baby, using human bones as fuel. This establishes the brutal, unsustainable reality of the Faith followers’ existence.
- The Faith followers rise up against Li Huowang, mistaking him for the killer of their shaman. The uprising is chaotic, fueled by desperation and misplaced faith.
- Li Huowang, in the midst of his own life-or-death escape, summons Peng Longteng, whose bloodlust is only barely restrained by his threat to re-incorporate her. She carves a path of absolute carnage.
- Li Huowang has a moment of clarity: the Faith followers aren’t just crazed cultists. Their bellies are full of *chalk clay*—a filler eaten by the starving to stave off hunger. They are also eating “human flesh” (米肉, mǐ ròu). This re-contextualizes them as victims of systemic collapse, not just monsters.
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang realizing that hidden practitioners are among the mob; they have been tracking him through the dirt, closing in as he fled.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Chalk Clay (观音土, *guānyīn tǔ*):** Also known as “Guan Yin clay” or “kaolin,” this is a white, chalky mineral that poor Chinese peasants historically ate during famines. It expands in the stomach to create a false sensation of fullness, but it provides zero nutrition and eventually kills the eater through intestinal compaction. The novel uses it here as a stark, *factual* indictment of the systemic horrors the Faith followers endure. They are not just fanatics; they are victims of a world that has eaten them alive.

**Human Flesh / “Rice Meat” (米肉, *mǐ ròu*):** In the context of the Dao-Twisted World, this is a horrifying reification of cannibalism justified as divine sustenance. The term “rice meat” (米肉) plays on the idea of human flesh as a sacred, staple food—a corruption of the act of eating into a religious duty. This ties back to earlier chapters and the long-running theme of cannibalism as a grim necessity in this broken world.

### Rice meat (米肉)

A term used in the Dao-Twisted World for human flesh consumed as food, often framed as a divine or sacred substance; a grim corruption of ritual cannibalism.

## Reading Guide

Alright, fellow Daoists, let me just say: this chapter hurts. It’s a *hard* reset after the high-octane battles of the siege. The horror here isn’t a monster with a thousand arms—it’s a woman named “Eat Full” who has never been full. It’s a baby dead in its mother’s arms while she believes they’re both on the verge of becoming immortals.

The genius of this chapter is that it doesn’t *excuse* these people. The Faith followers *are* murderous raiders. They *do* burn and loot. But Li Huowang, in this rare moment of exhausted clarity, realizes that their fanaticism isn’t born of evil—it’s born of a world that gave them nothing else. And that doesn’t change what he has to do. He can *understand* them and still crush their skulls to escape.

Pay attention to the way the narrative shifts between Qiu Chibao’s perspective and Li Huowang’s. The first segment reads like a folk horror tragedy; the second is a raw, bloody war zone. The collision between these two tones is what makes *Daoguai Yixian*’s horror so effective—it forces you to feel pity for the people your protagonist has to kill.
