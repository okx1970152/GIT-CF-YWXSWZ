## Chapter Overview

Get ready for a gut-punch, fellow travelers. Li Huowang just couldn’t swallow that “kind lie” pill, could he? This chapter is the brutal, tear-soaked aftermath of the Niuxin Village massacre. Having just confessed to his pursuers that he, in his own insanity, burned Bai Lingmiao’s entire clan to death, he now has to face the woman he loves with the truth. There are no monsters here, no rituals, no Wandering Lords—just two broken people in a quiet room, the sound of rain, and the shattering of a fragile dream. It’s a devastating masterclass in how the novel’s real horror isn’t just cosmic terror, but the human cost of a mind that can’t tell fire from comfort.

## Key Plot Points

- The Red Zhong (a Zuowandao trickster) pressures Li Huowang to lie to Bai Lingmiao, framing it as a "white lie" for a good cause. The monk, sighing, pulls him away, disgusted by the suggestion.
- Li Huowang, in Bai Lingmiao’s room, asks her innocent questions about her family. Her naive, happy descriptions of the Bai clan make the coming confession all the more painful.
- Li Huowang makes the fateful choice to tell the truth: “Miaomiao… I’m sorry. They’re all dead. I killed them.” The confession is stark and final.
- The reaction is visceral: Bai Lingmiao freezes, then screams, beats him, and finally bites him through his scabs before collapsing into muffled sobs on the floor.
- Li Huowang declares himself the one who must leave, reaffirming his core belief: “A lie is a lie! There is no good or evil in it!”
- He storms out into the rain, violently pushing away his followers with a bitter, cruel speech, framing them as burdens. This is a self-imposed exile to protect them from himself.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter dives deep into a core philosophical conflict: **the ethics of deception**. It’s a central theme in the novel’s world, perfectly embodied by the Zuowandao. Hongzhong represents a pure, almost moral-relativist approach to lying: a lie’s value is judged by its consequence, not its nature. This directly mirrors ancient Chinese philosophical debates, particularly the tension between the “School of Names” (名家, Mingjia) which argued that reality is tied to the correct names of things (and thus, lying is a corruption of reality) versus more pragmatic thinkers like Han Fei, who saw deception as a necessary tool of statecraft. Li Huowang, however, is a man of the Dao-Twisted World’s *visceral* truth; for him, a lie is a cancer that corrupts from within, regardless of its outcome. His roar—“A lie is a lie! There is no good or evil in it!”—is his final, desperate attempt to cling to a stable moral axis in a universe where nothing is real. He chooses the pain of truth over the poison of a comfortable delusion.

### Zuowandao

A secret society in the Dao-Twisted World that worships the concept of deception; its members treat lies as a sacred art and a source of power and pleasure.

## Reading Guide

This is a chapter you feel in your bones, not one you analyze. The novel has spent hundreds of chapters building the tender bond between Li Huowang and Bai Lingmiao—a rare oasis of warmth in a desert of blood and madness. Watching that bond shatter is the true cost of Li Huowang’s incurable condition. The horror here isn't a tentacle or a flesh Buddha; it’s the look on Bai Lingmiao’s face when the man she trusted reveals himself as the murderer of her entire family. Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s final, bitter monologue as he leaves. He doesn’t frame himself as a tragic hero. He frames himself as a “unknown madman” who has become a “madman with unfathomable abilities.” It’s a chilling self-diagnosis and a perfect capstone to his character arc—from passive survivor to active monster.
