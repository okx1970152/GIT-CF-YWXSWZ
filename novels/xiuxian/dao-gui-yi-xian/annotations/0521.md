## Chapter Overview
Hold onto your dantians, fellow travelers, because this chapter is a masterclass in tactical misdirection and bloody resolve. Our favorite walking disaster, Li Huowang, finds himself cornered in a burning temple, facing down not one but *two* terrifyingly capable opponents: a blazing high-rank Ao-Jing cultivator and a scheming royal princess who’s been hiding her martial prowess from the very start. The hunter-prey dynamic flips so fast it’ll give you whiplash. But while the situation looks hopeless, Li Huowang’s brain is working overtime. He doesn’t just fight—he *calculates*. And in a world where pain is a currency and your own body is a weapon, his final, desperate act is less a surrender and more a terrifying declaration of intent. Get ready for a finale that’s equal parts gruesome and brilliant.

## Key Plot Points
Li Huowang’s assassination attempt on the Eldest Princess goes completely sideways when he’s intercepted by a high-ranking member of the Ao-Jing Sect, Zu Yi, who is revealed to be a monstrously powerful cultivator composed of two fused bodies. Recognizing he can’t win a prolonged fight without failing his mission, Li Huowang uses a clever diversion. He sends Li Sui, disguised as himself under a cloak, into a burning temple while he goes invisible to close in on his true target. The plan nearly works, but the Eldest Princess reveals she’s not a helpless damsel; she uses supernatural ribbons to nimbly dodge his final strike and corner him alongside Zu Yi. Surrounded by troops and facing two deadly foes, Li Huowang makes a stark choice—not to escape, but to fight to the death. In a final, shocking act of self-mutilation, he tears open his own chest, exposing his organs to the world, preparing for a last-ditch gambit.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Ao-Jing Sect**: A unique fictional fusion in this novel, blending elements of Zoroastrianism (as seen in their "cross-shaped" or *xiao* 袄-shaped clay idols) and Nestorian Christianity, hence the name *Ao-Jing* (袄景). Their path is one of excruciating pain, believing that the ultimate suffering grants the ultimate power. Don’t be fooled by their self-destructive vibe—their combat strength is terrifyingly effective, as Zu Yi demonstrates by weaponizing molten gold from a Buddha statue. This is a sordid inversion of the normally pure, golden Buddha into a tool of fiery death.

**The “Fusion” Cultivator**: Zu Yi's form—two heads, four arms, three legs stitched together—is a nightmarish take on esoteric cultivation. Unlike the orthodox Nascent Soul stage where the inner spirit is singular and pure, this suggests a heretical fusion of two or more cultivators into a single, more powerful entity. It’s a ghastly union, prioritizing raw, unnatural power over the elegant transcendence of traditional Daoism. He literally embodies the Ao-Jing Sect’s twisted philosophy: to gain power, you must be willing to destroy the very form of your humanity.

## Reading Guide
This chapter perfectly illustrates a core theme of the novel: for Li Huowang, a normal fight is a luxury he can’t afford. He’s up against the elite guard of a power-hungry princess and a flesh-fusing pyromaniac. His only advantage is his ruthless, tactical cunning. The “swap” with Li Sui is a brilliant bit of scene choreography, showing how their bond has evolved into a deadly fighting partnership. Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s final decision. He knows the Eldest Princess wants him alive for interrogation (to “not scatter his Three Souls and Seven Poppers”). By tearing open his own chest, he’s not just preparing a desperate attack—he’s tearing up the enemy’s script. It’s a macabre form of nihilistic agency. This is Li Huowang at his most dangerous: calculating, cornered, and completely willing to burn his own flesh to ruin his enemy’s plans.
