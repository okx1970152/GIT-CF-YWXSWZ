## Chapter Overview
We step away from the visceral body horror for a chapter of quiet, transactional world-building, and honestly? It's a breath of relief and a gut-punch of realism all at once. Li Huowang, having just survived the Black Tai Sui and acquired a terrifying new lead on the Supervisory Heavenly Office, now has to deal with the very mundane problem of what to do with his traveling opera troupe. Lü Zhuangyuan, ever the pragmatist, sees an opportunity and executes a masterclass in folk survival: he sells his entire troupe to Li Huowang—hook, line, and sinker—in exchange for a loan to rebuild their gear. The real drama isn't in the negotiation, but in the quiet, bitter argument between a father who has survived everything and a son who dreams of a different life.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang, while eating alone, continues to strategize about how to infiltrate the Supervisory Heavenly Office, worrying about whether his copper coin mask can protect his Heart-Element identity from all supernatural detection.
- Lü Zhuangyuan approaches Li Huowang with an IOU, asking for credit to repurchase the troupe's costumes and instruments. In exchange, he offers Li Huowang ownership—making him the "Patron" of the Lü Family Troupe.
- Li Huowang initially refuses, offering to forgive the debt entirely, but Lü Zhuangyuan insists on a proper contract, knowing that an unpaid debt is a dangerous imbalance.
- Li Huowang accepts the arrangement, mentally noting that he now has a "hen that lays eggs," however lean and irregular the income might be.
- After sealing the deal, Lü Juren confronts his father privately, expressing his desire to go back home and stop singing opera. He feels humiliated by being "sold."
- Lü Zhuangyuan delivers a bitter, experienced lecture on the realities of war and poverty: that calamities always come in pairs (war then plague), and that without a trade like opera, they'd be starving in the dirt.
- The chapter ends with Lü Zhuangyuan's sharp retort to his son's complaint about being a servant: "Since when do servants get to eat meat every day?"

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Patron-Opera Troupe Relationship (东家制度)**  
In historical Chinese folk society, an "East Patron" (东家, dōngjiā) was a wealthy household or individual who sponsored a performing troupe. It wasn't indentured servitude but a form of debt-bound patronage. The troupe gained capital and protection; the patron gained a share of future earnings and, more importantly, social face (面子, miànzi). It's a deeply practical, feudal-era business arrangement that binds loyalty, debt, and survival into one pragmatic contract. Lü Zhuangyuan's insistence on a formal IOU with a thumbprint, despite his illiteracy, is a perfect example of how the poor used ritualized legal forms to protect themselves in a world without state safety nets.

**Calamities Come in Pairs (祸不单行)**  
Lü Zhuangyuan's speech about war leading to plague leading to famine is not just folk pessimism—it's a brutally accurate description of pre-modern Chinese cycles of disaster. Generals conscripted farmers, battles destroyed crops, corpses piled up unburied (leading to plague), and the survivors, penniless and starving, resorted to selling children or land. In such a world, a skilled trade like opera—however lowly—was literally a lifeline. This historical reality gives weight to his argument: he's not trying to control his son; he's trying to prevent him from starving.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a quiet gem that does three things perfectly. First, it shows Li Huowang's evolving mindset: he's no longer a desperate survivor clinging to life but a calculating leader starting to think in terms of assets—"a hen that lays eggs." Second, it delivers a masterful character moment for Lü Zhuangyuan, transforming him from a sycophantic old man into a hardened, wise patriarch who has survived every disaster the world can throw at him. The father-son argument is heartbreaking because both are right: the father knows the cruelty of reality, and the son dreams of dignity beyond poverty. It's a tiny, human drama set against the cosmic horror of the Dao-Twisted World, and it reminds us that sometimes the most chilling monster is a system that grinds people down until selling yourself feels like a promotion.
