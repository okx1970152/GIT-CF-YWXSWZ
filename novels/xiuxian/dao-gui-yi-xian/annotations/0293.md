## Chapter Overview

Grab your stomach, folks—this one hurts. Li Huowang, having consumed the Black Tai Sui to gain an edge, unleashes the devastating *Ripping Flesh and Pulling Bone* technique, linking his agony to everyone around him. The move breaks the enemy siege but leaves him a hollow, barely-living shell. The chapter then pivots sharply to a cold dawn in a small town, where the limping, scheming Gouwa is already measuring Li Huowang’s corpse for a coffin and negotiating funeral extras. The result is a staggeringly bleak, darkly comic meditation on aftermath and abandonment.

## Key Plot Points

- The Zuowandao impostor, his face skinned by the Black Tai Sui, screams in both pain and humiliation at being outplayed.
- Li Huowang swallows the Black Tai Sui–wrapped skin and eyeball, then draws his copper coin sword, wounding the Zuowandao fatally before collapsing.
- On the brink of death and surrounded by clay figures, Li Huowang activates his agony-link technique, *Ripping Flesh and Pulling Bone*, causing the entities to flee and the villagers to collapse in shared torment.
- His last conscious sight is Bai Lingmiao running toward him, before he sinks into darkness.
- Time jump: At daybreak, Gouwa (still recovering from his own injuries) tends to chores, then eats a leisurely breakfast of tofu pudding and crullers in the marketplace.
- He visits a coffin shop, where he haggles aggressively over the price of a premium red pine coffin, eventually securing a discount and a free burial shroud.
- Gouwa orders a grand funeral with full trimmings—including caterers, opera performers, and paper maids—and reveals this isn’t his first time making these arrangements for a dead "shixiong."

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **Breakfast Culture (豆腐脑 & 油条):** Gouwa’s morning meal of sweet tofu pudding and fried crullers is classic northern Chinese street fare. *Doufunao* (tofu pudding) can be served sweet or savory; sweet versions are topped with sugar, sesame, and red beans. Crullers (*youtiao*) are typically dipped in soy milk or porridge. The scene highlights the relative prosperity of Great Liang towns where even non-farming folk eat elaborate breakfasts.
- **Coffin Shop Haggling & Funeral Bureaucracy:** In pre-modern China, coffin shops were often the center of a web of death-service professionals, including chefs (for funeral banquets), opera troupes (for mourning performances), and the *Eight Immortals* (八仙, a folk term for eight coffin bearers). The shopkeeper’s role as a coordinator for “white affairs” (白事) was essential, as a proper funeral required orchestration of many parties.
- **Paper Effigy Custom (纸扎):** Gouwa’s request for “several paper maids” (纸丫鬟) is rooted in traditional Chinese burial customs—burning paper effigies of servants, houses, and money ensured the deceased had company and comfort in the afterlife. This grim but normal transaction acquires a deeply macabre tone when the “deceased” hasn’t actually died yet.
- **Ripping Flesh and Pulling Bone (裂肉抽骨):** A self-sacrificial agony-magic technique from Li Huowang’s repertoire. It weaponizes his own intense physical suffering as a shared experience, forcing all nearby beings to feel the same pain. This ties into the novel’s core rule: power isn’t free—it’s paid for with visceral, non-transferable torment.

## Reading Guide

Okay, let’s talk about that tonal whiplash. We open with Li Huowang literally ripping his own guts out to save everyone, then we cut to a sharp-faced goon eating crullers and dickering over coffin paint. It’s a masterclass in how *Dao-Twisted World* sells despair: not with an endless crisis, but with the quiet, bureaucratic normalization of death. Gouwa isn’t being cruel—he’s practical, maybe even kind in his own way. He’s the guy who steps up when everyone else is too busy crying. But every detail—the shave on the coffin price, the request for paper maids, the casual admission that he’s done this before—is a nail in the mood. Also note: the chapter title is “Death.” But whose? Li Huowang’s? Someone else’s? Or the death of hope that a community bred on violence can ever stop planning each other’s funerals? Dark stuff, dear readers. Dark stuff indeed.

## Why Gouwa Matters (Character Spotlight)

Gouwa (狗娃, “Dog Boy”) is often a background whiner—lazy, scheming, self-interested. But this chapter is his finest, most chilling hour. He’s not just surviving; he’s *operating*. He knows the town layout, the best breakfast stalls, and the local coffin market well enough to haggle with professional confidence. His tone is pragmatic, almost bureaucratic, as he negotiates funeral logistics for a man who isn’t even clinically dead yet. This is the heart of *Dao-Twisted World* horror: the mundane machinery of death keeps turning even while the heroes are still breathing. Gouwa’s efficiency is a form of survival, but it’s one that treats relationships as overhead and grief as someone else’s job.
