## Chapter Overview

Well, well—Li Huowang finally catches a break! After all that high-octane horror, our favorite mentally-compromised Daoist gets to do something refreshingly simple: rob a family of con artists blind. That's right, this chapter is a heist comedy nestled inside a cosmic horror novel. Watch as Li Huowang uses his shiny new phantom-walking ability—acquired from Danyangzi's corpse, of all things—to phase through walls and clean out a nest of professional liars. It's a palate cleanser of a chapter, but don't let the lighter tone fool you: the seeds of the next disaster are already being planted between the lines. And that book he snags? Let's just say it's going to hit a little too close to home.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang uses his newly acquired phantom-walking ability to spy on a family of con artists named Wu. The father is training his son in the *Art of Deception*, boasting that he even tricked his wife into marriage.
- While invisible, Li Huowang discovers a hidden stash of gold and silver—clearly stolen goods—and pockets it all to solve the group's food shortage.
- The group enjoys a rare, luxurious meal, and morale briefly improves.
- Li Huowang steals the family's *Art of Deception* manual as punishment (and out of spite), erasing one of their most valuable possessions.
- While idly reading the manual, Li Huowang realizes with growing horror that the verbal manipulation techniques described in the book are the same ones used by the Zuowandao on him. The truth was always hidden in plain sight, mixed in with the lies.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Phantom-Walking (穿墙术):** This isn't your average xianxia teleportation skill. In the Dao-Twisted World, abilities don't come clean. Li Huowang's new power is a grim inheritance from Danyangzi's alchemical feast—a parasitic ability that lets him hide his physical body in walls while his phantom roams. No golden light or mystic chants here; just a creepy, gradual sinking into solid matter. It's a power born from corruption, and it feels exactly like that.

**The "Art of Deception" as a Genre (骗经):** Yes, there are actual textbooks for lying in this world. The novel draws from the real-world Chinese *pianjing* (骗经) tradition—historical manuals that dissect the art of the con. But here's the twist: unlike the real versions, this fictional *Art of Deception* is treated as a family heirloom, a serious trade passed down through generations. The father's proud boasting about "tricking a wife" echoes real historical anxieties about false promises and marriage fraud in pre-modern China, but it's played for dark comedy because—well, his wife is right there in the other room.

**One Truth, Nine Lies — The Mechanics of a Good Con:** The father's lesson—"a true con man's words must contain truth"—is a cornerstone of real-world psychological manipulation. It's the principle that makes scams so hard to spot: the victim trusts the 10% truth, and that trust carries the 90% lie. Li Huowang's dawning horror comes from realizing that Zuowandao operated on exactly this principle. Every time they spoke, a sliver of truth was hiding inside the nonsense. This isn't just a tactical epiphany—it's a fundamental erosion of his ability to trust anything anyone says.

### Phantom-walking (穿墙术)

An ability inherited from Danyangzi's alchemical feast, allowing Li Huowang to phase his physical body into walls while his phantom roams freely. It feels more like a parasite's gift than a clean power.

## Reading Guide

Okay, let's talk about that monk, shall we? That old nag who pops up every time Li Huowang is about to do something morally dicey? The sudden reappearance of the "moralizing hallucination-monk" is a crucial mental health barometer. He's not a ghost; he's Li Huowang's conscience, forced into a separate character by the sheer strain of the trauma he's endured. The monk's pleas aren't divine intervention—they're the last shred of Li Huowang's own decency trying to talk him down from becoming Danyangzi 2.0. Pay close attention to *when* this voice appears and *when* it stays silent. Its presence means Li Huowang is still fighting. Its absence would mean something far, far worse.

Also, can we appreciate the heist comedy for a second? Our protagonist, a man who has literally eaten human flesh to survive, is now stealing pickled vegetables from a con artist's basement. The tonal whiplash is deliberate—it's the novel reminding you that even in a world this broken, people still need to eat, still get petty, and still find joy in a full stomach. But don't get too comfortable. That little book in Li Huowang's hands is a loaded gun that's about to go off.
