## Chapter Overview
Brace yourselves, fellow travelers—the cracks are starting to show again. Just when Li Huowang thought he had his mind under control with the Black Tai Sui, a stray auditory hallucination shatters the fragile peace. This chapter is a quiet, atmospheric horror piece disguised as a festive night out. The beautiful sight of Kongming lanterns drifting over Yinling City is undercut by Li Huowang’s dawning dread as a single, garbled voice worms its way into his ear. By the end, he’s standing alone in a stable at the fourth watch, staring at a bound monster—and discovers someone has been watching *him*.

## Key Plot Points
- During a celebratory dinner in Yinling City, Li Huowang hears a faint, fragmented voice—"Hu—n—gry… wood…"—that triggers a violent startle reaction, nearly spilling soup over the table.
- He quickly covers it up as his "old problem," but Bai Lingmiao notices his hand trembling.
- After returning to the inn, Li Huowang waits until the dead of night to sneak to the stable where the Black Tai Sui is stored.
- He attempts to communicate with the Black Tai Sui, first calling it "Mom," then addressing it as "Dr. Wang Wei," asking for medication. There is no response.
- Frustrated, he screams at it to either show him the hospital reality or stop playing games. His outburst yields nothing.
- After hours of waiting in the dark, the night watchman’s call snaps him out of his trance. He eats more Black Tai Sui flesh to stabilize himself.
- A sudden wave of grief overwhelms him as he admits to himself how much he still longs for the other world to be real.
- Just as he calms down, he senses a gaze on him. He spins around to find a hooded figure wearing a big-headed doll mask, delivering a cryptic message about the Supervisory Heavenly Office.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Kongming Lanterns (孔明灯)** are small hot-air balloons made of paper, traditionally released during festivals or as memorials for the deceased. Named after the legendary strategist Zhuge Liang (courtesy name Kongming), they were supposedly used as military signal devices. In this chapter, the surnames painted on the lanterns—"Fu," "Miao," "Zhao," "Li"—reflect a funeral custom where families send the lanterns upward to guide the spirits of the departed toward the heavens. The act of releasing them while wiping tears turns the night sky into a transient, beautiful graveyard.

**Night Watchman (打更人, gengfu)** was a real profession in pre-modern China. Armed with a bamboo clapper (梆子) and a gong, the watchman would patrol the streets at fixed intervals, announcing the watch (更, roughly two hours per watch) and shouting warnings like "Beware of fire!" or "Be cautious with candles!" The fourth watch (四更, around 1–3 AM) is historically considered the deepest part of the night, when both human energy and supernatural activity are at their most intense. In horror fiction, the clatter of the watchman’s clapper often marks the boundary between safety and the unknown.

**Heart-Element (心素)** is a novel-specific term for a rare human constitution that makes the bearer a walking paradox: they cannot tell reality from hallucination, yet their beliefs can reshape both worlds. Li Huowang’s auditory hallucination (“Hu—n—gry… wood…”) is a jarring reminder that his condition is never truly cured by the Black Tai Sui—only suppressed. The moment his mind wavers, the barrier between worlds thins again.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in quiet, creeping dread. There’s no monster attack, no chase scene, no ritual gone wrong. Instead, the horror comes from a single, faint voice in a noisy restaurant and the slow unraveling of a man who thought he was finally stable. The real knife twist is Li Huowang’s confession: *he wants that world to be real.* After weeks of insisting the modern reality is the "lie," his sudden, raw moment of grief hits like a punch to the gut. And just when you hope the chapter will end on that somber note, the big-headed doll mask appears in the dark, whispering of the Supervisory Heavenly Office. The world is watching him again—and it’s not done with him yet.

## Unmasked — The Big-Headed Doll Mask
The mysterious figure at the end wears a **大头娃娃 (big-headed doll mask)**, a cultural artifact you might recognize from Chinese traditional festivals and folk dances. These masks are often used in celebratory performances like *Da Tou Fo* (Big-Headed Buddha) dances, where performers mimic the laughing, wobbling gait of a jovial deity. But in the Dao-Twisted World, this cheerful icon is weaponized into an anonymous, sinister calling card. The figure’s first words—linking Li Huowang to the Supervisory Heavenly Office—are a stark reminder that even the most innocent folk symbols can harbor something cold and calculating underneath. *Nothing* is sacred here; everything can be hollowed out and turned into a mask for something far less amusing.
