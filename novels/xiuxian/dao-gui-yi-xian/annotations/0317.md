## Chapter Overview

After a long, grinding journey by cart, Li Huowang finally gets the chance to try out that dark-blue talisman manual… and the results are *spicy*. This clever weapon’s speed boost is as brutal as it is convenient—but true to form in this world, the "free" power leaves a trail of literal blood-sweat. Our favorite paranoid Daoist then strikes up a conversation with a local peddler, probing for intel on the next nightmare zone: Cangshui County. What he gets back is a wonderfully folkloric ghost story about the Ghost Festival running overtime. But this is a world where "the King of Hell forgot to close the gate" could be a *conservative* take. Li Huowang is here to untangle the *real* mess beneath the spooky gossip.

## Key Plot Points

The chapter opens on a scene of banal but gritty travel. Li Huowang, now driving a cart, manhandles the excitable dog Mantou onto the wagon and gets a chance to test out a newly acquired talisman for the first time. The manual explains that these talisman-scripts are based on imitating the patterns of nature—a Daoist philosophy of origin-tracing applied as a practical, if risky, exploit.

After successfully powering the horse into a frantic, vein-bursting charge, Li Huowang confirms the talisman’s potency (and its *very* visceral cost). He wonders if the red, oil-like sweat seeping from the horse’s back is a sign of a hidden price being paid directly by the creature. This nuance is crucial—it frames the talisman not as a simple buff, but as a biological transaction.

The main event of the chapter is the ensuing conversation with a traveling peddler. Li Huowang attempts a classic intel-gathering maneuver: buy snacks, build rapport, and then steer the conversation towards the local danger. The peddler, initially cagey, eventually spills the beans. He claims that Cangshui County is haunted because the King of Hell left the ghost-gate open after the Ghost Festival. Li Huowang, cynical and world-weary, sees this folk explanation as the pat, sanitized version of a far uglier truth. The chapter ends with him pressing for the *real* story, leaving the reader anticipating the reveal.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Spell-Chants (符咒) vs. Talisman Scripts (符箓)**: The manual Li Huowang is reading makes a fascinatingly technical distinction. **Spell-chants** are sounds (*zhou*) derived from imitating natural cries, while **talisman-scripts** are visual traces (*fu*) mimicking the patterns of starlight, insect trails, and natural phenomena. In real-world Daoist tradition, these are often used together, with the incantation activating the written symbol. This chapter unpacks a bit of that metaphysical logic—the "Heavenly Mechanism" of the cosmos is believed to be encoded in the ordinary world, waiting to be read and repurposed.

**The Ghost Festival (七月半/中元节)**: The peddler mentions the **Ghost Festival**, or Zhōngyuán Jié. In Chinese folk religion, it’s believed that on the 15th day of the 7th lunar month, the gates of the underworld are thrown open, allowing ghosts and spirits to roam the living world. Families make offerings to their ancestors and to placate wandering souls. The peddler’s story is a classic folk-horror trope: "What if the door got stuck open?" In the Dao-Twisted World, this innocent folk belief is almost certainly a terrifying reality.

**The "Three Corpses" (三尸) and "Three Pure Ones" (三清)**: Li Huowang dismissively lumps these two distinct concepts together. The **Three Pure Ones** are the highest deities in Daoism (the Celestial Worthy of Primordial Beginning, etc.). The **Three Corpses** are parasitic internal demons that dwell in the head, chest, and abdomen, trying to hasten a person’s death. The fact that he names them in the same breath shows how the novel blends high theology with visceral horror, treating both as equally suspect and equally dangerous data in his survival logbook.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a classic *Daoyi Guiyi Xian* "breather episode" that isn't really a breather: it's a lull in the supernatural violence, but a peak in the psychological grinding. Here’s what to watch for:

- **The Tao of the Talisman:** Pay attention to how Li Huowang approaches the talisman. He doesn't see it as a victory unlock. He sees it as a filthy, exploitable bug in the system. His internal monologue reveals he’d believe in the Jade Emperor himself if it helped him survive—he doesn't care about the *source* of the power, only its *cost* and *effect*. This is pragmatism weaponized as a philosophy.

- **The Silent Scream of the Horse:** That detail about the red, oily sweat oozing from the horse's skin is *pure* *Daoyi* horror. The novel doesn't show the talisman as a clean "buff." It shows the biological toll on a living creature. The horse isn't just moving fast; it's being *driven* fast by a force that makes it bleed from the pores. This is how you know the system demands payment, even for small favors.

- **The Peddler’s Prologue:** The conversation with the peddler is a masterclass in Li Huowang’s social survival skills. He buys food not for hunger, but as a social lubricant. He lets the peddler's fear do the talking. And he immediately dismisses the folk-spooky explanation ("Hell left the door open!") as the shallow version of the truth. He knows that in this world, the *real* monster is always stranger and more systemic than a simple ghost story suggests.
