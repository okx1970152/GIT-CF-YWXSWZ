## Chapter Overview

In this quiet, gut-wrenching chapter, we follow Li Huowang and Bai Lingmiao through their last day of market fun before the group hits the road. It’s a bittersweet breather episode, but the *Dao-Twisted World* never lets you have nice things. Beneath the surface of this leisurely date, our boy Li Huowang catches a whiff of something rotten—and I don’t just mean the sheep manure. A single human skull carried by Mantou the dog unravels a thread of suspicion that points directly at the weakest, most hopeful member of the party. The chapter nails the art of showing tension through domestic quiet: a gentle hand pulled away from a dog’s tongue, a kick that sends evidence flying, and the hollow comfort of “let me describe the world to you because you can’t see the horror.” Get ready, folks—it’s the calm before the storm, and it smells like betrayal.

## Key Plot Points

- Mantou brings Li Huowang a human skull, confirming that the previous bone it found was also human. Li Huowang tests the dog’s loyalty by offering it his own fresh flesh; Mantou ignores the meat, proving it’s only trying to please its master, not feed on people.
- Li Huowang immediately shifts his suspicion from the dog to Sun Baolu, who had previously lied about the bone being from a horse. This sets up a creeping distrust of the only normal member of the group.
- Li Huowang takes Bai Lingmiao on a careful tour of the market, deliberately steering her away from anything grim. He describes an exciting, vivid world she can’t see, creating a poignant contrast between his effort and her disappointed reality.
- At dusk, the pair returns to camp and runs into Sun Baolu, who has successfully purchased a herd of sheep. He acts cheerful, innocent, and eager to go home.
- The chapter ends on a quiet cliffhanger: Sun Baolu, walking back to his tent, suddenly finds Li Huowang blocking his path. The look in Li Huowang’s eyes is no longer friendly.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Human Skull & Folk Omens:** In Chinese folk belief, a dog bringing a human skull to its master is a profoundly bad omen. Dogs are thought to perceive *yin* energy (ghosts, malevolent spirits), and carrying human bone is often interpreted as a warning about a hidden threat or an unclean presence nearby. Li Huowang’s reaction—offering his own flesh as a test—draws on a brutal pragmatic logic: a dog that chooses living meat over dead bone is a predator, not a companion.

**The Art of “Describing the World” (说世界):** This chapter contains a powerful, subtle example of Chinese narrative technique where a character’s lively spoken description replaces the reader’s direct perception. Bai Lingmiao is blind, so Li Huowang builds a fictional market for her with his voice. This is a deep-rooted literary tradition in Chinese fiction, where the power of *telling* is often prized over *showing*, especially in moments of emotional protection. Here, though, it’s a tragedy: the real world is cold, dirty, and menacing, and the painted world Li Huowang creates is temporary and fragile.

**The Threat of the “Normal” Person:** Sun Baolu is the only physically unmarked member of the group. In xianxia logic and folk-horror fiction, a character who is “too normal” in a world of grotesque curses is often the most dangerous. His lack of visible damage isn’t a sign of innocence—it’s a red flag. The trope flips expected sympathy: we start fearing for the one we should pity.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in *atmospheric dread without a monster attack*. If you came here looking for Flesh Buddhas and screaming spirits, you might feel let down—but don’t be fooled. This is the kind of quiet chapter where the author sharpens the knife before the next horror. Pay close attention to the small gestures: Li Huowang pulling Bai Lingmiao’s hand away from the dog, the casual kick that sends the skull flying, the repeated “don’t worry, nothing is wrong.” Every single one of these is a flag. Also, keep an eye on Sun Baolu. His sudden freeze at the end, when he sees Li Huowang’s gaze, is worth more than any screaming battle scene. Something is about to break—and it’s not going to be pretty.
