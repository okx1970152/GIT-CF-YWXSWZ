## Chapter Overview

This chapter is a masterclass in quiet horror—two seemingly mundane conversations, one in the Dao-Twisted World and one in a hospital room, that collide into a single, devastating existential punch. Yang Xiaohai plays the junior detective, grilling a little beggar and piecing together the terrifying truth: there’s a war on, and it’s spilling refugees into their path. But the real nightmare comes in the second half, where Li Huowang, lucid and calculating, conducts a dangerous experiment with a jade pendant. What he gets back isn’t an answer—it’s a duplicate, a physical contradiction that shatters any neat theory of “real vs. fake.” And then come the voices. Get ready, because this one doesn’t let go.

## Key Plot Points

- Yang Xiaohai, showing growing initiative, steps outside to question a young beggar. He learns that the beggars flooding the town are refugees from the east, driven west not by drought or flood but by an escalating war.
- Gao Zhijian corrects Yang, stammering that this isn’t just war—it’s a “routed army,” implying the front has collapsed and soldiers are running loose, pillaging without command.
- Gouwa’s casual suggestion to “buy” Yang Xiaohai is deflected with dark humor, but it underlines the ever-present threat of human trafficking in this world. The little beggar’s envy of Yang Xiaohai’s clean shoes is a brutal reminder of how fragile one’s position is.
- In the hospital hallucination, Li Huowang tests Doctor Li’s counter-hypothesis: instead of bringing objects *from* the hallucination *to* reality, he tries sending a real-world jade pendant *into* the Dao-Twisted World.
- The experiment yields a duplicate—two identical pendants exist simultaneously, one in each world. This shatters Li Huowang’s simplistic “one is fake, one is real” framework.
- Exhausted and confused, Li Huowang falls into a nightmare where Yang Na and Bai Lingmiao fuse into a single nightmarish composite entity, forcing him awake in cold sweat.
- The chapter ends on a poison-tipped question: *Was it really just a dream?*

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The *Lotus Song* (莲花落, *Lianhua Luo*)**: This is a traditional Chinese folk song form historically performed by beggars to solicit alms. Think of it as a busker’s hustle with centuries of cultural weight—part performance art, part desperate plea. In the Dao-Twisted World, hearing it from a crowd of refugees isn’t just background noise; it’s a reliable signal that these people have been displaced long enough to *professionalize* their misery.

**Routed Army vs. War Refugees**: Gao Zhijian’s stammered correction carries a terrifying tactical distinction. A “routed army” (溃军, *kuijun*) is worse than an invading one. Disciplined soldiers take what they need; routed soldiers take *everything*, because they have no chain of command, no supply line, and no fear of punishment. The horror here is that the front has not just moved—it has *dissolved*, turning armed men into roaming predators indistinguishable from bandits.

**Duplication as Existential Horror**: Li Huowang’s experiment produces a duplicate, not a transfer. In Chinese philosophical terms, this mirrors the old puzzle of the “broken mirror”—if a mirror splits, does each shard reflect the truth, or only a fragment? The Two Truths Doctrine in Mahayana Buddhism also haunts this moment: conventional truth (the hospital) and ultimate truth (the temple) are both valid, both empty, and both real. Li Huowang is being forced to live in *both* at once, and reality refuses to collapse into a single narrative.

### Heart-Element (心素)

A rare human constitution in the Dao-Twisted World that makes the bearer a valuable alchemical ingredient; Li Huowang is identified as one.

### Duplicate

In Li Huowang's experiment, an object sent from one world to the other produces a second copy rather than moving the original, creating two identical objects that exist simultaneously in both realities.

## Reading Guide

This is one of those chapters where nothing “happens” in the action-hero sense, yet everything shifts. Pay close attention to the jade pendant experiment—it is the *only* direct, intentional test Li Huowang has ever run between the two worlds, and its result (a duplicate, not a false copy) is a major update to the novel’s metaphysical rules. No, he still doesn’t know which world is “real.” But now he has proof that when he *tries* to cross-contaminate, reality *splits* rather than replacing one with the other.

The dream-composite of Yang Na and Bai Lingmiao is also worth sitting with. These two women represent his two anchors: Yang Na is his modern-world lifeline (romance, trust, the promise of sanity), while Bai Lingmiao is his Other-world tether (loyalty, tenderness, shared horror). That they merge into a single screaming horror is not random—it means his core emotional bonds are fraying, and he can no longer keep his two lives emotionally separate. The question at the end isn’t rhetorical. It’s a crack in the dam.
