## Chapter Overview

Alright buckle up, fellow Daoists, because this chapter is a quiet, character-driven breather that packs a deceptively heavy punch. Our boy Li Huowang, finally free of Danyangzi’s parasitic grip, makes a pilgrimage up the mountain to Anci Nunnery to pay his respects and, more importantly, call in a long-standing debt. What he gets isn’t just a cryptic conversation or a belly full of mung-bean cakes—it’s a **sword**. Not just *any* sword, either, but a military-grade killing blade soaked in so much murderous intent it nearly makes him lose his mind just by looking at it. But beneath this simple transaction lies something far more unsettling: Abbess Jingxin, the blind, toothless, grotesque Buddhist nun, is playing a longer game than she lets on. Between the casual price tag of thirty thousand taels of gold and a quiet request buried in fine print, this chapter sets up new stakes, new gear, and a new kind of emotional weight. It’s short, but every line matters.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang climbs Mount Henghua to visit Abbess Jingxin at Anci Nunnery, bringing a three-and-a-half-jin bundle of mung-bean cakes from Fushouzhai as a thank-you gift. His dog Mantou tags along, unimpressed by the mountain’s terrible trails.
- The nunnery remains exactly as before: filthy, foul, and decaying. The half-rotted food swamp in the courtyard is mostly gone—eaten or rotted away, no one can say.
- A brief encounter with nun Miaoyu establishes the nunnery’s atmosphere of casual, greedy pettiness; she demands a pastry as a toll for information.
- Abbess Jingxin has physically recovered from her previous emaciated state, now back to her normal obese and wrinkled appearance, but remains blind and toothless. She devours the mung-bean cakes with desperate, animalistic hunger.
- The chapter’s central emotional beat: Jingxin’s request for Li Huowang to return was not about the cakes. After confirming that Danyangzi is truly gone, she offers to *sell* him a weapon—a longsword of military origin, saturated with *sha qi* (killing intent).
- Li Huowang draws the blade slightly and is overwhelmed by a surge of murderous energy that turns his vision red and makes him tremble uncontrollably. He barely manages to resheathe it.
- Jingxin explains the sword’s nature: it’s a soldier’s blade that would devour an ordinary wielder, but as a Heart-Element, Li Huowang’s inherent “bewilderment” makes him immune to its corruption. The sword can kill both men and spirits.
- The price is set: thirty thousand taels of gold, payable via IOU with interest. But the real price is hidden in the small print: if Li Huowang ever finds a way to cure his Heart-Element condition, he *must* tell her.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

### The Nüshu Script (女书)
The gate of Anci Nunnery bears a couplet written in nüshu, a unique syllabic script historically used exclusively by women in the Jiangyong region of Hunan province. In a xianxia setting, this is not just flavor text—it signals that the nunnery is a space operating under a different, female-coded set of rules and secrets. The *Dao-Twisted World* often weaponizes real historical practices, and nüshu here might hint at hidden texts or forbidden knowledge passed down among the nuns.

### Military Swords and Sha Qi (煞气)
The sword Jingxin offers is described as a “military blade” (*bingjia de dongxi*). In Chinese martial and xianxia tradition, weapons used in war absorb the *sha qi*—a form of killing intent or violent energy—from countless battlefield deaths. Such blades are considered wild, dangerous, and prone to “devouring” their wielder if the user’s will is not strong enough. This is a classic trope: only a protagonist with a unique constitution (or in this case, a Heart-Element’s ontological bewilderment) can safely wield such a cursed weapon. The fact that Li Huowang’s own madness cancels out the sword’s murderous lust is a grimly poetic match.

### The Honest Price of a Sword
Thirty thousand taels of gold for a blade like this is almost absurdly cheap—this is not a market transaction but a symbolic transfer. Jingxin’s real price is the promise of information: if Li Huowang finds a way out of the Heart-Element condition, he must share it. This echoes a key theme of the novel: knowledge about the Dao-Twisted World’s rules is the most valuable currency, and everyone is hoarding and trading fragments of it.

### Sha Qi (煞气)

Killing intent or violent energy accumulated by weapons, especially military blades used in war. Such swords are considered dangerously wild and can “devour” a wielder who lacks a strong will or special constitution.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in how *Dao Gui Yi Xian* builds its world through character interactions rather than exposition dumps. The relationship between Li Huowang and Jingxin has evolved from transactional wariness to something almost familial—she scolds him for buying too few cakes with the casual authority of a cranky grandmother. But don’t let that warmth fool you. Jingxin’s motives are never purely sentimental. She asked him to come back specifically to check if Danyangzi was truly gone, and her “just in case” clause is a quiet trap of obligation.

Pay attention to how the chapter contrasts the grotesque physicality of the nunnery (the filth, the rotting food, Jingxin’s blackened fingernails) with the sudden, sharp *cleanliness* of the sword’s killing intent. The blade is dangerous, but it’s also *honest* in its violence—unlike the nunnery’s surface piety, which masks rot and hunger. This sword is a significant upgrade from the *Thousand Greats Record*, which demanded direct self-harm. The blade externalizes violence while still carrying its own cost.

Also, note the parallel construction of “price.” Jingxin’s mock IOU is meaningless; her real price is a promise. This is how power works in this world—every gift is a loan, every favor a debt with invisible interest. Li Huowang now owes her a truth he hasn’t even discovered yet.
