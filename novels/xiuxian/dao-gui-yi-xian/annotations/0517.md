## Chapter Overview

Alright, fellow daoists, buckle up—because this chapter is where Li Huowang stops playing small and starts playing for keeps. After the visceral, blood-soaked spectacle of Peng Longteng's rampage (seriously, did Dong Zhongshou even have a whole skeleton left?), our favorite red-robed madman delivers a brutal political ultimatum that would make Machiavelli blush. Then comes the real twist: Ji Lin finally shows he's not just a weeping, corpse-eating emperor—he's been listening. The assignment is a family affair of the most vicious kind, and Li Huowang has just volunteered to be the family butcher. Get ready for a chapter that's equal parts gangster monologue, court intrigue, and a cold, creeping sense that the game board is tilting.

## Key Plot Points

- **The Aftermath of Dong Zhongshou's Death**: The chapter opens with the gruesome remains of the strategist still in mid-air—Peng Longteng, headless and ecstatic, grinds his jawbone to dust. She is in full berserker mode, craving slaughter. Her invulnerability (no fatal weak point) makes her an unstoppable weapon, and she revels in finally getting her fix.
- **Li Huowang Declares Total War**: After the fighting ends, Li Huowang addresses the assembled Ji Lin loyalists and fence-sitters. He explicitly rejects traditional political maneuvering—no alliances, no trades, no diplomacy. His message is brutal: this is a Gu-raising contest, and everyone except the one on the throne must die. The previous emperor's iron law (left unspoken) backs his position.
- **The Message to Ji Lin**: Li Huowang speaks to the absent emperor directly, coldly, in front of everyone. He calls himself a "sword" that Ji Lin has been leaving in the sheath. The pressure is on: stop wasting time, or the Ling Nie you ate was for nothing.
- **The Imperial Decree**: The next day, an old eunuch delivers an oral decree—not a direct order, but a piece of intel: the Empress Dowager and the Changning Princess will leave the city tomorrow. Li Huowang immediately understands the subtext: kill them both. The eunuch's story about Ji Lin's childhood (receiving less coal money, being the unloved son) explains the depths of the emperor's cold resentment.
- **Bargaining and New Alliances**: Li Huowang tries to negotiate for future favors, but the eunuch dismisses him. More importantly, Li Huowang notices the eunuch's supernatural ability to vanish, recognizing that Ji Lin's court is not filled with weaklings. He begins to calculate how to use these powerful eunuchs against the Die.
- **Domestic Interlude & Preparation**: Li Sui doesn't understand the politics, but she enjoys her goose. Li Huowang sends her to prepare a dirt-filled vat and retrieves a sealed iron box from his carriage—something he is so terrified of escaping that it's packed in wax.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Military Path Cultivators (兵家)**: Peng Longteng is described as a "military path" cultivator "without a fatal weak point" (没有死穴). In the Dao-Twisted World, this is a terrifying combatant archetype—essentially a living siege engine powered by killing intent (杀气). Unlike Daoist or Buddhist cultivators who refine internal energy, military path cultivators are forged through battle and massacre, often at the cost of their own humanity. Their invulnerability comes not from divine protection but from the sheer density of death-will they've absorbed. This makes them nearly unstoppable in direct combat but also deeply predictable: they exist to kill, and they love it.

**Gu-Raising (炼蛊)**: Li Huowang explicitly frames the succession struggle as a Gu contest. In classical Chinese mythology, Gu is a venomous poison-bug created by sealing multiple insects in a closed container and letting them devour each other until only the strongest, most toxic one remains. By using this metaphor, Li Huowang is rejecting all civilized rules of succession and declaring that the throne belongs to whichever candidate survives the wholesale slaughter of all others—including their supporters. It is the most naked, Hobbesian interpretation of imperial politics possible.

**The Eunuch's Disappear (消失)**: The old eunuch's ability to vanish without a trace reveals that the palace bureaucracy is itself a supernatural ecosystem. Eunuchs in Chinese palace history were often associated with secret networks, hidden knowledge, and (in fiction) forbidden cultivation arts. Their physical castration was sometimes believed to open their bodies to different forms of spiritual power. Here, the eunuch is not just a messenger but a potential asset—and a reminder that Li Huowang is not the only apex predator in this court.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is *The Godfather* meets *Dao-Twisted World*—and I mean that as the highest compliment. Li Huowang's monologue to Ji Lin's retainers is one of the coldest political declarations in the entire novel. He's not asking for permission, not seeking allies, not even threatening. He's stating a law of physics: in this war, everyone but the final winner dies. There's no negotiation because there's nothing to negotiate *with*. The only question is who dies first and who dies last.

The emotional core, though, is the subtext about Ji Lin. The eunuch's casual story about the winter coal money is devastating in its mundanity. Ji Lin isn't just a power-hungry emperor; he's a child who was systematically unloved, a boy who learned that winter means being cold while others are warm. That sting has never left him. Now he's ordering his mother and sister killed—not because they're threats, but because they chose someone else over him. Li Huowang recognizes the cold logic even as he executes it.

And that sealed iron box? Whatever's inside it, Li Huowang has gone from reactive survivor to proactive schemer. He's gathering tools, building contingencies, and preparing for the Die. The sword is being sharpened.
