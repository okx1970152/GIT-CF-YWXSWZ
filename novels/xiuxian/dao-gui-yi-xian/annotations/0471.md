## Chapter Overview

New chapter, new destination! After the intense battles of the last few arcs, our favorite traumatized couple is heading to the capital. Wait—did I say *couple*? Well, they're traveling together, so close enough. But as usual, the Dao-Twisted World isn't about to give Li Huowang a single peaceful night's sleep. The chapter opens with a deceptively quiet journey, but the moment they roll into a seemingly empty village, you *know* some folk-horror nonsense is about to kick off. Because when has a village full of cheerful, arm-band-wearing, chore-dancing peasants ever meant good news? Get ready, folks—it's time to see what brand of weekly nightmare the local god is cooking up tonight.

## Key Plot Points

*   Li Huowang, Bai Lingmiao, and Li Sui travel toward the capital, seeking treatment for Bai Lingmiao's eyes, which have turned a worrying pinkish-white.
*   They stop for the night in a village that shows clear signs of recent death and loss from the Heavenly Calamity.
*   Li Huowang uses a small "recognition" trick on an old villager to secure lodging, revealing his growing pragmatism.
*   Bai Lingmiao's casual comment about the dead villagers causes Li Huowang to look toward Zhuge Yuan, whom he suspects blames himself for the world's suffering.
*   In the middle of the night, Li Sui wakes Li Huowang to witness the villagers performing a strange, ritualistic dance and chant under the banyan tree.
*   Li Huowang decides to observe rather than strike, uncertain whether the villagers are a threat, but confident in his own ability to handle them.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter introduces the "Child-God" (于儿神) mentioned in the villagers' chilling chant. This isn't a random invention; it's a direct reference to a figure from the ancient Chinese mythological text, the *Classic of Mountains and Seas (Shan Hai Jing)*. In that text, the Yu'er God (于儿神) is described as having a human head and two snake bodies, dwelling in a deep abyss, and being associated with thunder and wind. The *Shan Hai Jing* is a treasure trove of bizarre creatures and gods, and "Dao Gui Yi Xian" loves to pull from this wellspring of weirdness. By invoking this name in the context of a desperate, plague-ridden village ritual, the novel is grafting an ancient, obscure deity onto a fresh, immediate horror—a reminder that the gods of old might still be listening, and they might not have good intentions.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in setting, pacing, and atmospheric dread. It's the calm before the storm, the quiet rumble before the folk-horror jumpscare. Watch how the world-building does the heavy lifting here: the black armbands (a real historical mourning custom in China), the crumbling houses, the casual reference to the Heavenly Calamity—these details paint a vivid picture of a society broken by forces beyond its control. Li Huowang's own characterization shines through his actions: his gentle care for Bai Lingmiao, his small deceptions for practicality, and his cold confidence in his own combat ability. The real treat is the final scene: a dance in the dark. Who's this "Child-God"? What are they asking for? The chapter ends with a masterful hook, leaving you itching for the next chapter. This is the novel at its most subtle and effective—letting a single, eerie ritual tell a whole story of desperation.

## Minor Character Spotlight: The Funeral Bands

The *black armband* is a culturally loaded detail that deserves its own moment in the sun (or, more fittingly, the moon). In traditional Chinese mourning rites, the color and style of mourning attire are strictly graded by one's relationship to the deceased, with immediate family wearing the coarsest, most unhemmed fabrics. A simple black armband like the one worn by the villagers is a sign of a looser, more collective mourning—not for a specific parent or child, but for any relative or, in this case, neighbor. Seeing an entire village wearing them tells you that the Heavenly Calamity didn't strike a few unlucky families; it struck *everyone*. It was an equal-opportunity disaster, and this shared grief is what has bound the survivors together into their strange, desperate covenant with the Child-God.
