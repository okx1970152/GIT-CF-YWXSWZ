## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang finally arrives at Pi County, and the place is exactly as foreboding as its name suggests. After prying information from an old wheat harvester about the town's strange habits—people staying indoors, children crying behind windows, and a general pall of unease—he steels himself for the worst. The chapter is a masterclass in dread-building: Li Huowang doesn't find a monster waiting at the gates; he finds a town that feels *wrong* in every quiet, domestic detail. The real horror is the waiting, not the attack.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang grills an old harvester about Pi County; learns that locals stay indoors, leave payment and food outside, and seem to be in poor health.
- The old man mentions hearing a child being beaten through a window, which Li Huowang immediately interprets as a potential red flag for sacrificial or ritualistic abuse.
- The group's journey to Pi County is uneventful except for the eerie lack of bandits, which feels more ominous than welcome.
- Upon arrival, Pi County is described as shaped like the character "pi"—a deliberate, unsettling square town under a gray sky.
- The streets are empty; only a few hurried pedestrians pass by. Li Huowang feels many pairs of eyes watching from behind windows.
- A haggard woman clutching a crying child hurries past the group. Li Huowang catches the scent of blood and rust on her—and his dog Mantou confirms it.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Pi County (匹县):** The town is named after the visual shape of the Chinese character “匹,” which resembles a square with an extra stroke. This isn't just a cute naming quirk—it's an example of how Chinese place names can literally be pictographic. For English readers, imagine a town shaped like a capital “L” being called “L-Town.”

**Hired Wheat Harvesters (麦客, Maike):** These are seasonal migrant workers who follow the wheat harvest across northern China, traveling from village to village. In the pre-industrial world of the novel, these men are the bottom rung of the agricultural ladder—landless, itinerant, and utterly dependent on the goodwill of landlords. The novel uses them as a perfect barometer for local normalcy: if a harvester thinks something is off, you know it's bad.

**Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司):** This is a government-sponsored supernormal law enforcement body, believed to be under the imperial board of war. They track and deal with rogue cultivators, supernatural disasters (called *Heavenly Calamities*), and any other phenomena that threaten the stability of the realm. Li Huowang's entire strategy for Pi County hinges on attracting their attention—he wants the problem to be big enough to bring them in, but not so big that it kills him first. It's a grim cost-benefit analysis, but that's his life now.

**Folk Taboo against Beating Children:** In traditional Chinese folk belief (and in many modern rural contexts), beating a child is seen not just as discipline but as a potential supernatural red flag. Crying children are believed to attract certain types of hungry spirits or even to be a sign of possession. The fact that the old harvester specifically mentions the crying as something that “made my heart ache” is a cue for the reader to suspect this is more than just harsh parenting.

## Reading Guide

Welcome to Pi County, where the streets are empty, the windows are full of eyes, and the local mom smells like a crime scene. Li Huowang is playing a dangerous game here: he needs the problem to be big enough to warrant an official response, but not so big that his group gets eaten before the cavalry arrives. Classic Dao-Twisted World logic. The best part of this chapter is how the horror is built from *ordinary* details—a crying child, a woman who avoids eye contact, a dog that whines at nothing. You know something is very wrong, but you can't point to a single monster. That's the kind of dread this novel does best. Also, points for the dog being a reliable supernatural detector. Mantou is the MVP of this arc.
