## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow travelers — this one’s going to sting. After the explosive ritual that freed Bai Lingmiao from the spirits that controlled her, our protagonists don’t get a triumphant victory lap. Instead, they get the silent, crushing aftermath. Lian Zhibei limps away with her payment, Li Huowang stares at a blood-stained veil, and the quiet that follows is louder than any explosion. This is a chapter about collateral damage — not the physical kind, but the emotional wreckage that settles when the right thing costs something you never expected to pay. And if you thought Li Sui was just a creepy little sidekick, wait until you see her waiting for someone who will never come home.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang compensates Lian Zhibei for the plan’s failure, handing over the jade token for yang-life as agreed. Their transaction closes cleanly, but the emotional bill hasn’t arrived yet.
- Bai Lingmiao embraces Li Huowang in silence. The Second Spirit is gone, leaving only a pair of blood-red shoes and a soaked bridal veil. She tells him she wanted to say it earlier but couldn’t.
- That night, neither Li Huowang nor Bai Lingmiao can sleep. She confesses she misses the Second Spirit, who was like a sister to her. Li Huowang wrestles with guilt, asking himself if he would have made a different choice if he had known.
- At dawn, Li Huowang finds Li Sui in the main hall, waiting for “Second Mother” with half a wild dog she saved. Li Sui doesn’t understand that the disappearance is permanent — she remembers the Second Spirit coming and going before. Li Huowang hugs her, unable to explain.
- Meanwhile, the Bai family compound is running low on grain. Gao Zhijian pushes the village militiamen through brutal training with fresh-wood halberds, demanding they keep up. The men complain but endure for the white-flour buns at noon.
- A logistics dispute arises among the disciples: Zhao Wu wants to wait for the autumn harvest to buy grain cheap, but Yang Xiaohai and Gouwa worry they won’t last that long. Chun Xiaoman settles it for now with Li Huowang’s remaining gold.
- Gao Zhijian stops Chun Xiaoman afterward. Flustered and red-faced, he tells her he has money from “the past” — and when she asks if he was a rich landlord, he can’t explain. He crouches down and starts drawing on the ground instead.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The chapter’s emotional core hinges on the **Second Spirit (Er Shen)** — a concept rooted in the folk mediumship tradition of Northeast China. In a *tiao dashen* (跳大神) ritual, the First Spirit is the lead medium who chants and drums, while the Second Spirit is a silent partner, often acting as a vessel or spiritual assistant. But here, the Second Spirit isn’t just a tool — she’s a person, a sister, a presence with her own memories and attachments. Her loss is felt not as a ritual cost but as a death in the family.

Li Huowang’s late-night reflection also touches on a classical Chinese moral framework: the tension between **righteous duty (大是大非)** and **human sentiment (人情)**. In xianxia morality, saving the world from a Heavenly Calamity is obviously right, but the emotional gray zone — where the “right” choice hurts the people you love — has no clear verdict. This is the novel’s quiet acknowledgment that cultivation isn’t just about power; it’s about living with the scars of your own decisions.

### Fresh-wood halberd (湿木戟)

A training weapon made from newly cut wood, still heavy with moisture. These are far heavier than dried or seasoned weapons, designed to build raw strength through pure physical punishment.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in *show, don’t tell* grief. Notice how the author uses objects — a pair of shoes, a raw wild dog, a silent embrace — to do the emotional heavy lifting instead of any dramatic monologue. Li Huowang’s inability to answer Bai Lingmiao’s unspoken question is more devastating than any confession. And Li Sui’s innocent hope that the Second Spirit will “come back” like she always did? That’s a kid processing loss in real-time, and it hurts exactly as much as it should.

Also worth flagging: the way Gao Zhijian’s stutter and frustration are handled. In a less careful novel, his backstory would be a punchline. Here, it’s treated as a genuine mystery — and his refusal to say more, just drawing on the ground, feels like a deliberate choice to protect a past he can barely articulate. Keep an eye on that thread.

For new readers: if the emotional weight of this chapter feels heavier than expected, that’s intentional. *Dao Gui Yi Xian* doesn’t let its victories go unpunished. The cost is rarely gold or blood — sometimes it’s just the sound of someone’s voice you won’t hear again.
