## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, fellow Daoists, because Chapter 468 is one of those rare, gut-punching breathers where the horror isn’t teeth and tentacles—it’s a mother’s forced smile. Li Huowang shuttles back to White Tower Psychiatric Hospital for a single meal with Sun Xiaoqin, and the whole scene is a masterclass in mundane dread. He uses cheap mental tricks to make her happy, promises her a future of video games and skydiving, and all the while, his brain is coldly calculating opportunity costs. Then he blinks back to the Dao-Twisted World, his vision restored, and he has to verbally wrestle his own daughter into believing he just ate *egg custard*, not pocket-lint. It’s quiet, it’s domestic, and it’s devastating.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang’s hospital visit opens with both maternal and filial performance: Sun Xiaoqin hides her anxiety behind a smile, and Li Huowang detects the lie immediately.
- He explicitly rejects the idea of spending more time with her, deeming it “wasting time” on cultivation goals. This is a crucial character beat—his pragmatism has calcified into coldness.
- To cheer her up, he employs “small Zuowandao tricks,” painting a vivid fantasy of post-discharge life: video games, world travel, bungee jumping, and winning back Yang Na.
- His internal monologue reveals a telling dissonance: he is only 18 but feels ancient, and he speculates that the two-hour time difference between worlds might explain his age gap, raising a new bracket of truth/illusion theory.
- He cuts the visit short (“in three days”) and returns to the dark, blind Dao-Twisted World.
- Li Sui hints that he was eating something off the ground; Li Huowang forcefully corrects her (“I just ate egg custard!”), dictating reality by sheer will.
- The chapter ends with his vision restored—the gauze comes off, and he finally gets a full look at Li Sui’s new form.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**“Heavenly Calamity” vs. Mundane Horror:** This chapter is a textbook example of the novel’s unique dread taxonomy. The real horror isn’t a screaming demon—it’s watching an ordinary woman force a smile because her son is in a mental ward. The text lingers on the *details* of that lie: the cracked-screen phone, the reflexively defensive “I’m not worried,” the bargaining through promises of VIP subscriptions. Xianxia usually sells you transcendence; *Dao Gui Yi Xian* sells you the guilt of choosing it.

**Zuowandao and Emotional Manipulation:** Li Huowang calls his tactic a “small Zuowandao trick” (坐忘道的小手段). This is a loaded term. Properly, *Zuowandao* is a secret society named after a Zhuangzi meditation concept, “sitting in forgetfulness,” a state of pure, empty awareness. The novel twists it into a religion of lies and cognitive disruption. By using their methods to comfort his own mother, Li Huowang is normalizing the very weapon that shattered his mind—a deeply unsettling form of self-mutilation.

**Bungee Jumping as a Counter-Icon:** The casual mention of extreme sports (蹦极) is a deliberate tonal shock. This isn’t a cultivator offering spirit stones; it’s a Chinese mother’s real-world anxiety (“I can’t even watch the videos on Douyin!”). It grounds the metaphysical horror in a recognizable, modern Chinese domestic reality, making the return to the dark, sticky world of Li Sui’s claw-hand all the more jarring.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a trap. It feels gentle, even sweet, but it’s actually a catalog of Li Huowang’s erosion. Pay close attention to the speed of his thoughts: he spots his mother’s fake smile, files it as a data point, and immediately rejects the *human* solution (spending time with her) for the *efficient* one (a quick lie). That is not the logic of a healer. That is the logic of a man who has learned to treat love as a variable to be optimized. Also, the detail about his eyes recovering is not a happy ending—it is the removal of a vulnerability, which in this world, is usually the signal for something worse to come.
