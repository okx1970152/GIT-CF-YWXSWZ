## Chapter Overview

Fellow Daoists, grab your emotional support... well, everything, because Chapter 308 drops us straight into the raw, shivering core of Li Huowang's worst-case scenario. We're not in the Dao-Twisted World fighting monsters or scheming against cults. We're *stuck* in the modern-world hallucination, and it's trying to kill him with the most mundane, terrifying weapon of all: exposure. This is a masterclass in how the novel weaponizes the ordinary. No flesh Buddhas, no Wandering Lords—just a rotting orange, a cold rain, a broken lighter, and the crushing realization that he might have been abandoned by everyone he loves. The horror here is deeply personal, profoundly lonely, and absolutely chilling.

## Key Plot Points

- The chapter opens with Li Huowang eating a rotten orange in the pouring rain, his anchor to the modern-world side of his split existence. He's starving and desperate, having just tried to bluff his way through a deadly confrontation with the Bai family in the Dao-Twisted World.
- Frantically, he calls out for Bai Sai, trying to reinforce his lie that he is "family" and that Bai Lingmiao is pregnant with his child, hoping it will buy him a reprieve from execution.
- But the hallucination-world doesn't cooperate. Instead, he only encounters indifferent, frightened strangers who see him as a dangerous madman to be avoided.
- The physical toll of the hallucination becomes terrifyingly real: he collapses from cold, suffers from violent shivering, and begins to feel the onset of a high fever.
- He finds shelter under a bridge tunnel and manages to scavenge discarded plastic bottles and a lighter to build a small fire, a grimly practical act of survival.
- A young girl appears at the tunnel entrance—the same child he once took hostage, now a first-grader. Her fear of him is a brutal mirror of how his "sickness" has isolated him from the innocent world.
- She drops a piece of chocolate, which triggers a painful memory of Yang Na, the woman he longs for but cannot reach.
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang crouched by his poisonous, plastic fire, tasting the bitter dregs of his own despair, accepting his abandonment with the hollow mantra: "It's just a hallucination anyway."

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Hallucination as Survival Metaphor** — This chapter is a brilliant inversion of the classic xianxia "secluded cultivation" trope. Instead of a serene cave where a hero meditates for power, Li Huowang is in a filthy, rain-swept bridge tunnel, burning toxic plastic just to stay alive. His "cultivation" here is pure, grinding endurance of psychological and physical misery. The novel constantly reminds us that for a Heart-Element, the mind is not a sanctuary—it's a battleground where the boundary between "real" and "fake" is a lethal weapon.

**The Horror of Abandonment** — In Chinese folk and literary tradition, being cast out by one's family or community is a profound form of spiritual death. Li Huowang's muttered "I've been given up on by my family" taps into a deep cultural anxiety. The novel uses this to twist the knife: even the "safe" hallucination of the modern world provides no shelter. The strangers who recoil from him are not enemies, but their fear is its own kind of torture.

**The Chocolate Connection** — The Dove chocolate is not a random detail. It directly links Li Huowang's present despair to his past with Yang Na, the anchor of his "normal" life. In the Dao-Twisted World, he is a monster and a liar. In the modern world, he is a patient and a burden. The chocolate is a tiny, sweet reminder of a self he can no longer access, making his loneliness feel absolute.

### Heart-Element

A rare human constitution highly valued by alchemists; being a Heart-Element makes Li Huowang's mind a battleground between reality and hallucination.

## Reading Guide

Get ready for a chapter that doesn't move the plot forward at all, but instead plunges us *downward* into Li Huowang's psyche. This is a classic "breather chapter" in the worst possible way—a pause for emotional devastation rather than plot progression. The key here is the *sensory detail*. Pay attention to the cold, the stench of the rotten orange, the acrid smoke of the burning plastic. The author is forcing you to *feel* Li Huowang's physical misery without any supernatural filter. This is the rawest, most human he has been in a long time, and that vulnerability is the true horror. The appearance of the first-grader is a gut-punch callback to a much earlier arc, reminding us that his sickness has touched and terrified the innocent. The chapter is a quiet, lonely howl in the dark, preparing us for whatever desperate act he will commit next.
