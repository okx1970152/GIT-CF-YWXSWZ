## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang hits rock bottom—and then somehow keeps digging. Chapter 377 delivers the emotional aftershock of Bai Lingmiao’s corruption, lets the former softest character walk out with a spiteful one-liner, and then drags our favorite madman to the very doorstep of institutional power: the *Supervisory Heavenly Office*. Between hangovers, internal parasites, and the crushing weight of an unchangeable past, Li Huowang musters just enough spite to keep moving. And for the first time in a long while, the world of the Dao-Twisted World shows him something he *kind of* recognizes: the Mohist school. But nothing here is ever quite what it seems.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang finally lets go of the old Bai Lingmiao—literally. He accepts her under the influence of the Purple-Tasseled Sword, but she rejects *him* with brutal clarity, questioning whether he loves *her* or just a docile fantasy.
- Bai Lingmiao storms off, declaring she’ll marry another man, leaving Li Huowang alone with the Second Spirit for a brief, quiet moment of comfort—then complete solitude.
- Wallowing in despair, Li Huowang drinks himself into a stupor, only to wake up and discover Li Sui’s tentacles are visibly surfacing on his hand. The parasitic takeover is accelerating.
- His response is not surrender but defiance—he shakes his fist at the sun, swearing to overturn fate. Classic Li Huowang, just with more hangover.
- He heads to the Supervisory Heavenly Office, first walking into the astronomy bureau by mistake before finding the correct building—a strange, empty room where invisible gazes track his every move.
- A hidden passage reveals his guide: Sima Lan, a man from the **Mohist school** (Qi branch), who speaks through a blind black bird. Li Huowang learns there are two Mohist traditions: Qi Mo (acceptable) and Liang Mo (heretical).

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司, Jiān Tiān Sī)** is a fascinating piece of worldbuilding. In imperial China, the *司天监* (Sītiān Jiàn) or Bureau of Astronomy was the official body responsible for calendar-making, astronomical observation, and interpreting celestial omens—tasks of immense political significance, as the Emperor’s mandate from Heaven was read in the stars. Here, it’s been twisted into a *supernatural law enforcement agency*, fusing bureaucratic structure with investigative occultism. The line of characters Li Huowang sees on the wall—warning that privately printing calendars is a crime punishable by relocating the offender’s entire clan—is actually a real historical decree. The novel retains the *form* of imperial control over celestial knowledge while filling it with very different content.

**The Mohist School (墨家, Mòjiā)** is one of the major philosophical schools of the Hundred Schools of Thought period. Mohism emphasized universal love, meritocracy, thrift, and—importantly for this chapter—advanced engineering and defensive warfare. Mohists were legendary architects of siege defenses and builders of mechanical devices. The split into *Qi Mo* (齐墨) and *Liang Mo* (梁墨) is a fictional invention by the novel, but it draws on the historical fragmentation of Mohism after the Qin dynasty. Qi was known for its cosmopolitan, scholarly culture; Liang was associated with militarism and brute force. In the Dao-Twisted World, this translates into a divide between “allowed” and “heretical” lineages. For Li Huowang, hearing “Mohist” is a rare moment of grounding—it’s one of the few classical Chinese schools he actually *knows* from the real world.

### Two-Layer Heart-Turbid Trap (一生一死心浊)

A containment paradox where a dead Heart-Turbid conceals a living one; victims are trapped in a fabricated karmic obstacle layer, unable to perceive reality.

### Face-Changing (变脸)

A traditional Sichuan opera technique where performers rapidly switch between painted masks; in this novel, it triggers Li Huowang’s paranoia, associating any change of face with Zuowandao deception.

## Reading Guide

If you thought Bai Lingmiao’s corruption arc couldn’t get any sharper, brace yourself. This chapter doesn’t just let her walk away—it puts a dagger in the heart of Li Huowang’s self-deception. Her accusation—“do you love me, or just a concubine who obeys you?”—is one of the most emotionally surgical lines in the entire novel. It’s not about the sword; it’s about whether Li Huowang ever truly saw *her* as a person, or just as a devotion-shaped hole in his broken world. And the worst part? He doesn’t have a good answer.

On the action side, we get our first real look at the Supervisory Heavenly Office in the capital, and it’s everything we hoped for: bureaucratic horror, invisible watchers, hidden corridors, and institutionalized strangeness. The Mohist introduction is a quiet lore bomb—finally, a school of thought that Li Huowang can connect to his own lost world. But in true *Dao Gui Yi Xian* fashion, even that comes with a catch (heretical Liang branch, anyone?). Get ready, fellow travelers—this is the calm before a very stormy investigation arc.
