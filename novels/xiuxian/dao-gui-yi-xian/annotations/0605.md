## Chapter Overview
Move over, boring naval battles. In this chapter, Li Huowang takes "boarding an enemy vessel" to a brilliantly grotesque new level. After a forced march across the desert, he finally catches up to the enormous ritual ship carrying the Heart-Pan of Doumu—the man who, along with Huangfu Tiangang, tried to ambush and kill him. With his body held together by Li Sui’s writhing black tentacles (which he now treats as his own limbs), he infiltrates the ship under cover of darkness. But the real threat isn’t the crew, who are mysteriously absent. It’s the ship itself: a monstrous gallery of animated woodcarvings, tree-root statues of the Eight Immortals, and one very disturbing wall of screaming, eyeball-packed faces. *Daoguixian* asks the question: what’s scarier, a ghost ship or a *ship that carves its own gods*?

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang arrives at an enormous wooden ship just before sunset, after a frantic journey to intercept the fleeing Heart-Pan. His body is severely damaged; three of his four limbs are gone and replaced by Li Sui's tentacles.
- He waits for complete darkness before boarding, using his invisible, misaligned body to scout undetected.
- The ship's foredeck is empty but covered with altar items: a massive cauldron, three human-high incense sticks, and offerings (pig, ox, sheep heads). The crew is nowhere to be seen.
- Inside the cabin, Li Huowang finds the walls covered in a hyper-detailed, three-dimensional woodcarving of a bustling city street—a "Along the River During the Qingming Festival" display.
- The wooden people come alive the moment Li Huowang draws his weapon, pointing and staring at him. He can't hide from them. Peng Longteng tries to smash them but is instantly turned into wood herself.
- After cutting a rift in the wall, Li Huowang catches a nightmarish glimpse of a giant, wood-textured eye socket packed with dozens of eyeballs staring back at him.
- He cuts a hole in the floor and drops down to the next level, which is pitch black. There he discovers: giant root carvings of the Eight Immortals. Li Tieguai is a bloated mass of preserved tree burls; Lü Dongbin is a hollowed-out, insect-eaten shell.
- As Li Huowang lights the area, the remaining Six Immortals appear in their own grotesque wooden forms. The chapter ends on this chilling tableau.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
The "Eight Immortals" (八仙, *Ba Xian*) are a beloved pantheon in Chinese folklore and Daoist mythology: figures like Li Tieguai (Iron Crutch Li), Lü Dongbin, and He Xiangu who each achieved immortality through a unique path. They're usually portrayed as benevolent, eccentric, and wise—the kind of sages you'd want at your banquet. In *Daoguixian*, however, they've been twisted into something *sui generis*. These aren't graceful statues; they are **root carvings**—artifacts made by following the natural, gnarled growth of a tree root rather than imposing an idealized human form. Li Tieguai is swollen and lumpy with preserved burls; Lü Dongbin is a hollow, worm-eaten husk. This is a classic *Daoguixian* move: take a familiar, comforting cultural symbol, strip away its beauty, and leave behind something that feels less like art and more like a biological specimen preserved in wood.
The "tree root carving aesthetic" here is especially important. In Chinese art, following the root's natural form (随形) is a sign of mastery—harmonizing the human design with nature's chaos. But in this chapter, the root's chaos *consumes* the human form. The Immortals aren't carved *from* wood; they *are* wood, in all its lumpy, worm-eaten, cancerous entropy. That's the horror: the divine, in this world, isn't transcendent. It's material, organic, and decaying.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in building tension through *locale alone*. Notice how Li Huowang, even with his supernatural senses and Li Sui's terrifying assistance, is a stranger in this boat. The ship isn't just a vehicle; it's a reliquary. The total absence of a crew suggests that the boat itself *is* the defense system. The root-carved Eight Immortals aren't guardians waiting to ambush him—they're horrifying scenery, which might be worse. Pay close attention to the wall of tiny, animated city people. They point and stare but don't attack. Are they servants? Sensors? A warning? And the image of the massive, wood-grained eye socket packed with too many eyeballs—that's not a monster you fight; it's the ship's *consciousness*, maybe. The chapter ends on a classic *Daoguixian* freeze-frame: Li Huowang, surrounded by giant, grotesque gods made of tree tumors and insect holes, with no clear path forward. Brilliantly unsettling.
