## Chapter Overview
Get ready, fellow Daoists—this chapter is a masterclass in worldbuilding delivered through a grumpy, obese, wisdom-dispensing nun. Li Huowang finally gets some straight talk about the Dao-Twisted World’s real rules, and the picture is not pretty. Abbess Jingxin of Anci Nunnery shatters any lingering illusions Li Huowang might have about trusting people, hiding his Heart-Element nature, or joining a sect for protection. The core lesson? “Run.” But the real bombshell comes when she warns him about a group she almost forgot to mention: the Zuowangdao. These aren’t your run-of-the-mill bandits or power-hungry cultivators. They are gamblers, tricksters, and reality-bending con artists who lie for fun, have fooled actual gods, and once talked an entire city into self-castration for a laugh. For a man who can’t tell truth from lies, they are the worst possible enemy. Li Huowang ends the chapter with a name he can’t place, a sword he can’t pay for, and a dread he can’t shake.

## Key Plot Points
1. **The Sword is a Loan, Not a Gift**: Abbess Jingxin corrects Li Huowang’s effusive gratitude—the blade costs 30,000 taels plus interest, to be paid back in installments via courier. This grounds the helping hand in pragmatic, worldly economics.
2. **The Heart-Element Survival Guide**: The abbess delivers the brutal truth: as a Heart-Element, Li Huowang is a walking treasure. No sect will take him in out of kindness. His only reliable strategy is “fight or flee.” Trust is a luxury he cannot afford.
3. **Anci Nunnery’s Secret Skill**: Li Huowang asks how the nuns cultivate their powers. The abbess deflects—the technique can only be written in Nüshu (female script) and cannot be taught to a man, closing off yet another potential path.
4. **The Zuowangdao Introduction**: In the chapter’s critical turn, the abbess warns Li Huowang about a group called the Zuowangdao—a band of gambler-con artists who use bizarre supernatural abilities and flawless illusions. They are not driven by profit but by amusement, making them unpredictable and terrifying.
5. **They Fooled a God**: The abbess drops the haunting detail that a century ago, a Zuowangdao cell spent thirty years and successfully tricked the “Wedding Joy God” (囍神), a deity distinct from the simpler “Joy God” (喜神). This establishes the Zuowangdao as a force capable of targeting entities far above mortal scale.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Zuowangdao (坐忘道): The Way of Sitting in Forgetfulness** – The name is a direct and malicious hijacking of a genuine Daoist meditative state. In classical Daoism (notably in the *Zhuangzi*), “zuowang” describes a state of profound stillness where one forgets the self, the body, the intellect—a path to mystical unity with the Dao. This novel takes that serene, quietist ideal and corrupts it into a philosophy of compulsive deception. The Zuowangdao do not seek enlightenment through stillness; they seek it through chaotic, performative falsehood. Their power lies not in swords or spells, but in the systemic erosion of certainty.

**Nüshu (女书): Female Script** – This is a real-world phenomenon: a phonetic syllabary that was historically used exclusively by women in Jiangyong County, Hunan Province. Passed down through generations, Nüshu was used for personal letters, poetry, and lamentation songs—a secret language in a patriarchal society. In the Dao-Twisted World, it becomes a gatekeeper for certain esoteric powers, introducing a gendered barrier to knowledge that even the most desperate male protagonist cannot cross.

**The Two Joy Gods: 喜神 vs 囍神** – A vital distinction. **喜神 (Xi Shen)** is a generic term for a “God of Joy” or a “Deity of Happiness,” often associated with good fortune and auspicious events. **囍神 (Xi Shen)** uses the doubled “囍” character, which is the traditional Chinese emblem for *double happiness* and is exclusively associated with weddings. The Zuowangdao allegedly fooled this specific marriage deity, a trick that implies they manipulated the very fabric of matrimonial destiny and ritual. The nuance suggests the novel’s world has a granular, bureaucratic pantheon where each character variant corresponds to a distinct divine jurisdiction.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a “quiet info-dump done right.” There are no monsters, no fights, no bloodbaths—just a fat, scratching nun and a protagonist standing in a doorway. But the tension here is entirely conceptual. Li Huowang’s world just got much, much larger and more terrifying, not because something tried to kill him, but because he now understands that the rules he thought he knew are laughably incomplete.

Pay special attention to the abbess’s phrasing. She doesn’t say “they are dangerous.” She says they “*even dared to try and trick the gods*.” That’s not fear of a threat; that’s respect for a precedent. The Zuowangdao aren’t just criminals—they are ontological hazards. For a man like Li Huowang, whose survival depends on accurately reading reality, the introduction of a faction whose entire purpose is to *make reality unreadable* is arguably the most dangerous development yet.

Also note the micro-detail about the last Zuowangdao intruder at the nunnery: *“he saw the error of his ways and converted to Buddhism.”* The abbess says this almost as an afterthought, but it carries a chilling implication. Did he genuinely repent? Or did the nuns *fix* him? In a world where nuns cultivate with Nüshu and gods can be conned, “converted” might be a much more violent verb than it sounds.
