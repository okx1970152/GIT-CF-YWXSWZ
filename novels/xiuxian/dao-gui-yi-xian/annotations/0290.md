## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this chapter is a masterclass in paranoia backfiring. Li Huowang, still shell-shocked from his last run-in with the Zuowandao, confronts the blind fortune-teller Chen with extreme prejudice. What starts as a brutal interrogation and face-peeling (yes, you read that right) quickly turns into a gut-punch realization: Chen might actually be telling the truth. And the real enemy? That friendly big-headed doll from the Supervisory Heavenly Office who offered him a job. The chapter closes with the creepiest possible party crashers—the Mud Bodhisattva and the temple’s Eighteen Arhats, whose mud-shells are cracking open to reveal compound eyes inside. The horror shifts from *“who can I trust?”* to *“it doesn’t matter—they’re coming either way.”*

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang suspects Blind Chen is a Zuowandao trickster lying about not having called for reinforcements. He refuses to be led into another trap and attacks.
- The ensuing fight is fierce but short: Blind Chen uses a Bagua mirror that emits a purifying light beam to destroy the Wandering Lords. However, Li Huowang’s group reinforcements (Bai Lingmiao, Gao Zhijian, Chun Xiaoman) quickly overwhelm and capture him.
- Li Huowang performs a gruesome “face peel” interrogation—cutting open Blind Chen’s face layer by layer to search for a second mask—but finds nothing. This is the hard evidence that Chen is *not* a Zuowandao shape-shifter.
- Realizing his error, Li Huowang frantically has Bai Lingmiao invoke the White Immortal to heal the old man and apologizes. But Blind Chen is furious and terrified; he believes this was all a Zuowandao scheme to take him out.
- The chapter ends with an ominous interruption: the Mud Bodhisattva has been watching them, and the temple’s Eighteen Arhats are emerging with cracking mud shells and compound eyes beneath.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

Let’s talk about **the Eighteen Arhats (十八罗汉)** and **the Mud Bodhisattva (泥菩萨)**. In standard Buddhist temple layout, the Eighteen Arhats are enlightened disciples of the Buddha, often depicted as fierce protectors of the Dharma—guardians, not aggressors. But in the Dao-Twisted World, every bit of “sacred” iconography gets inverted. These Arhats aren’t stone statues anymore; they’re alive, their bodies made of cracking clay with compound eyes hiding inside. That chilling detail—*compound eyes*—is no accident. It leans into the “insectoid” horror the novel loves, suggesting these things aren’t divine guardians but parasitized shells. As for the “Mud Bodhisattva,” a *pusa* (bodhisattva) is a being on the path to enlightenment who delays their own nirvana to help all sentient beings. But a “mud bodhisattva” is something else entirely—a folk term for an idol that’s been sitting in a shrine so long it’s just part of the wall. Here, it has become a silent, patient *watcher*, and its mere presence signals that the temple belongs to something older and far less merciful than any Buddhist hall should be.

### Bagua Mirror

A bronze or metal mirror engraved with the Eight Trigrams, used in Daoist ritual to purify, repel, or exorcise entities.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a textbook example of why Li Huowang is both tragically sympathetic and terrifying to be around. His trauma with the Zuowandao has made him *too* good at seeing lies where there are none. The face-peeling scene is graphic but narratively essential—it’s the only way he can *know* for sure. And the irony is crushing: he tortures an innocent man for proof, gets it, and immediately has to patch him back up while the real threat watches from the shadows. For first-time readers of *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, this chapter demonstrates the novel’s core tension: the protagonist’s survival mechanism (extreme, violent paranoia) is also his greatest moral failing. Pay close attention to how the temple scene is framed—the Mud Bodhisattva isn’t attacking; it’s just *standing there*, which is somehow worse. When the familiar horror of the Zuowandao is replaced by the silent, compound-eyed horror of corrupted temple guardians, the message becomes clear: in this world, even the “good guys” trust the wrong leads, and the statues are always watching.
