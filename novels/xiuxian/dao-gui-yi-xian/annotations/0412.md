## Chapter Overview

Oh, Li Huowang. Our poor, battered boy finally catches his white whale—Bei Feng, the Heart-Element he has been hunting across half the Great Nuo—and in the span of a single conversation, the man goes from salvation to salt in a wound. This chapter is a brutal masterclass in Zuowandao-style psychological warfare delivered via a mahjong metaphor. The high of victory curdles into confusion, then into the cold, grinding horror of realizing the prize is a fake in a much larger game. Get ready for a gut punch disguised as a discovery.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang identifies Bei Feng, hidden among the eunuchs, through Zhuge Yuan’s perception. His initial reaction is overwhelming, tearful relief.
- He confirms the man’s identity by peeling back his false skin to reveal the flesh-mahjong tile bearing the name “Bei Feng.”
- Li Huowang demands the method to suppress his hallucinations. Bei Feng acts tough but is receptive to negotiation, correctly inferring Li Huowang is a Heart-Element seeking him.
- The crushing revelation arrives: Bei Feng is NOT a Heart-Element. He is only one of four individuals sharing the same face and title within the Zuowandao. The real Heart-Element is “Old Four.”
- Zhuge Yuan confirms this truth with an awkward apology, devastating Li Huowang whose hopes are ripped away seconds after being fulfilled. The Zuowandao’s “four-of-a-kind” trick is explained.
- Enraged, Li Huowang nearly kills Bei Feng but is stopped, realizing the captive may hold other useful information. He heals him with the *True Sutra of the Fire Vestments*.
- Bei Feng remains provocatively defiant, taunting Li Huowang with the ultimate Zuowandao weapon: even if he tells the truth, how can Li Huowang ever trust a liar’s word?

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Mahjong Metaphor as Ontological Weapon**: The chapter’s title, “Mahjong,” is not casual flavor. A standard set contains 144 tiles divided into suits and honor tiles. The Winds (East, South, West, North—each with four identical copies) and the Dragons (Red, Green, White—each with four identical copies) are central to the game’s strategy of concealment and pattern-building. The Zuowandao’s use of this structure is terrifyingly clever: by having four people share one social identity (“Bei Feng”), they weaponize the game’s logic of identical copies. An outsider watches a single piece, never realizing there are three others lying in wait, shifting positions. This transforms a parlor game’s rule into a system for ontological fraud.

**The Horror of “Cultivate the False into Reality”**: This chapter fractures Li Huowang’s understanding of “finding” someone. The core conflict—the philosophical war between cultivating truth (修真) and cultivating falsehood into reality (修假)—is laid bare not as an abstract debate, but as a trap built into the world’s architecture. The Zuowandao don’t just wear masks; they *are* the mask, multiplied. Bei Feng’s taunt—”I dare you to believe me”—sums up the sect’s power: they don’t need to lie when they can make the concept of truth itself unreliable.

### Cultivate the True/False (修真/修假)

A central philosophical conflict: the xianxia ideal of “seeking truth” is opposed by the Zuowandao’s power of “cultivating falsehood into reality.”

## Reading Guide

Here is where the story’s philosophical dread tightens its grip. This is a classic “victory is worse than failure” beat. Watch how carefully the narrative pivots from tactile relief (the skin peeling, the tile being found) to cognitive collapse (the tile is a copy). Li Huowang’s emotional arc functions as a breath-taking seesaw: hope spikes, shatters, and relapses into grim pragmatism. The Zuowandao, once a street-level nuisance, are revealed to have a corporate structure that mirrors a game of pure strategy and deceit. And that final line from Bei Feng isn’t just a taunt—it’s an invitation to paranoia. Is he telling the truth now? Can he even afford to be trusted when he admits he can’t be trusted? That’s the Zuowandao’s real power.
