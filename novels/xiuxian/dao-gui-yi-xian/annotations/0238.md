## Chapter Overview

Whoa. This chapter is a masterclass in tension through configuration. Li Huowang is lost in the dark, eavesdropping on a philosophical argument between two unseen speakers, and the conversation is bonkers-level existential. They’re talking about how nothing is ours—pain, joy, even life itself is “on loan” from some higher owner. The goal? Strip it all off and become “The One.” It’s like a debt-collection agency for your soul. Li Huowang is rightly suspicious, comparing it to Danyangzi's crazy talk, but before he can puzzle it out, things get *very* physical. A spectral hand grabs his people, a frantic monk-debater crashes into their camp, and the chapter climaxes with Li Huowang staring an inch away from a hanged, red-dressed female corpse with a tongue that just keeps drooping down. It’s a classic Dao-Guardian recipe: metaphysical dread spiced with immediate, tactile body horror.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang overhears a debate between two voices, a man and a young girl. The man’s philosophy is radical: emotions (the Three Corpses), pain, and life itself are not our own—they are owned by external entities. The only way to true freedom is to discard all these “borrowed” things to become “The One.”
- Li Huowang connects this idea to his own world, wondering if his pain “belongs” to Bashe and his joy to the Double Joy God. He quickly shuts it down, reminding himself these are just unproven beliefs, no different from Danyangzi’s delusions.
- Sun Baolu panics, claiming a hand is grabbing his arm. Li Huowang touches it—a rough, cold hand that yanks him before he cuts it away with his sword.
- A young, gaunt-faced Daoist in a yellow robe appears, looking nothing like the haggard Danyangzi. He warns of an entity called the “Noose-Son” and leads the group in a desperate flight.
- The Daoist performs a unique, non-Daoist, non-Buddhist incantation to mask their presence. The effect is chilling: a pressure passes over them, and their body temperatures drop.
- A massive shape looms in the darkness. A rusted steelyard weight falls from above, attached to a rope that leads to a pair of cloth shoes… and then a swinging red-clad corpse with an unnaturally long tongue drops down, inches from Li Huowang’s face.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **Three Corpses (三尸)**: This is a heavy-hitter from Daoist internal alchemy. The “Three Corpses” are three parasitic spiritual worms that live in the human body (head, chest, belly), driving us toward base desires (greed, lust, hunger). The goal of cultivation is to “cut off” these corpses. The mad monk’s speech weaponizes this concept, framing everything we are as “owned” by something else.
- **“The Great Dao gives rise to fifty, Heaven uses forty-nine” (大道五十，天衍四九)**: This is from the *I Ching* and classic interpretations of the “Dayan Number.” It essentially means that the Dao contains infinite potential (50), but the manifested world (Heaven’s mechanism) only uses 49 of those threads, leaving one “empty” space. This space is the “One” that humanity can occupy to escape fate. The monk’s philosophy is a radicalized Xianxia take on this ancient concept.
- **Jumping out of the Three Realms (跳出三界外)**: A classic Xianxia and Buddhist concept of ultimate liberation. The “Three Realms” are the Desire Realm, the Form Realm, and the Formless Realm. “Jumping out” means escaping the cycle of reincarnation. The monk uses it as a promise of ultimate personal freedom after shedding all that is “owned.”
- **Corpse Solution (尸解)**: In Daoism, this is a method of transcendence where an adept fakes their death, leaving behind a token (a sword, a talisman) to confuse the celestial bureaucracy. The “Mushroom Corpse Immortal” mentioned in the incremental glossary inverts this beautifully—transcendence not through escape, but through embracing literal rot and decay. Chew on that for a second.

## Reading Guide

Alright, fellow seekers of the twisted, here’s the deal. This chapter is a classic “the monster isn’t the real threat” setup. The actual monster is *philosophy*—a very, very dangerously wrong philosophy. Pay attention to the discussion about “owners.” It’s the first time we’ve heard such a clear, theologically reasoned framework for what’s wrong with this world. It’s not just “bad things happen.” It’s that everything you are *belongs to someone else.* And the only road to freedom is to become a hollowed-out shell, “The One.” That’s terrifying on a level beyond any physical monster.

Also, don’t gloss over that “great cauldron” (鼎) reference. The novel is building a connection between these philosophical ideas and a specific power structure, maybe even a high-level faction using these cauldrons for something. And the final image? The hanged corpse with the steelyard weight? That’s pure *Dao Gui* grotesquerie. It’s not just a scary face; it’s a specific, culturally-loaded instrument of punishment (steelyards measure weight, a damn twisted punishment) turned into a haunting entity. Enjoy the nightmares!
