## Chapter Overview

Welcome to the Spirit Calling, fellow Daoists! Chapter 60 throws us headfirst into a genuine piece of Chinese folk horror: the *tiao dashen* ritual. Our favorite pragmatic madman, Li Huowang, gets a front-row seat to Li Zhi’s actual skills as the group stumbles into a village and discovers that their local “scandal” is, in fact, a full-blown possession crisis. This chapter is a masterclass in atmosphere—the transition from a mundane, gossipy village to the edge of the supernatural is seamless, and the ritual itself is described with loving, visceral detail. We get to see Li Zhi in his element, and the scene sets up a crucial test: is this guy the real deal, or just another fraud in a world full of them? Buckle up; the drums are beating.

## Key Plot Points

- The group arrives at a village where Li Zhi claims to have a contact. The village, however, is eerily empty of people.
- Li Zhi, like a supernatural gossip columnist, excitedly discovers what he thinks is a family scandal (“the old man rolling in the ashes”).
- The “scandal” is revealed to be a violent possession: a naked woman is contorting on a table, her eyes without pupils and with multiple eyelids, suggesting a serious supernatural infestation.
- Li Huowang observes the locals’ helplessness. He realizes that for common folk, dealing with such entities is a matter of luck—they either tough it out or find a wandering exorcist.
- Li Zhi volunteers to perform a *tiao dashen* (Spirit Calling) ritual. He negotiates payment with the family while maintaining his casual, chatterbox demeanor.
- The ritual begins. Li Zhi’s drumming and singing create a focused, almost oppressive atmosphere. The Second Spirit begins to tremble and quake, signaling the arrival of something.
- The possessed woman is repelled by the incense smoke from the ritual, confirming its supernatural efficacy.
- The chapter ends with the ritual in full swing, establishing Li Zhi as a genuinely competent folk practitioner, contrasting sharply with his usual gluttonous and talkative nature.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Tiao Da Shen (跳大神):** This is a central piece of Chinese folk religion, particularly in Northeast China. It’s a ritual where a medium (the Lead Spirit, 大神) and their assistant (the Second Spirit, 二神) use drumming, singing, and incantations to invite a spirit or deity to possess the Second Spirit’s body. The purpose is usually to diagnose and cure illnesses caused by spiritual affliction, to exorcise malevolent entities, or to ask for guidance. The “god” or “immortal” is often a respected ancestor or a powerful spirit like a fox (狐仙) or weasel (黄仙). In the context of the Dao-Twisted World, the ritual is depicted with a remarkable sense of gritty authenticity—the smoke, the drum, the shivering, the *hiccups*—all of which are classic signs of a successful invocation or mediumistic trance.

**Er Shen (二神):** The “Second Spirit” or assistant medium plays a crucial, terrifying role. In Li Zhi’s case, we have already seen his “wife” as the Er Shen. She does not speak but serves as the vessel. Her sudden trembling, hiccuping, and the rustling under her veil are classic signs that a spirit is “mounting” her (上身) or beginning to make contact. The Er Shen is not a mere puppet; as previous chapters have hinted, she may possess her own will and secrets.

**Incense Smoke as a Barrier:** Li Huowang notes that the incense smoke doesn’t disperse but forms a barrier that the possessed woman cannot cross. This is a genuine folk belief: incense smoke is considered a medium of communication with the spiritual world and can create a purifying or protective boundary. Its behavior in this scene is described with a tangible sense of physicality, reinforcing the visceral horror of the Dao-Twisted World.

**Folk vs. Formal Religion:** Chapter 60 contrasts the “high” Daoism of Danyangzi (false, corrupt, alchemical) with the “low” folk religion of Li Zhi. While Danyangzi hides behind holy titles and secret scriptures, Li Zhi deals with the common people’s everyday terror directly, using techniques passed down through generations, not written in corrupted heavenly texts. It’s a fascinating look at the novel’s class system within its supernatural framework.

### Tiao Da Shen (跳大神)

A Chinese folk ritual led by a medium (大神) and an assistant (二神) to invite a spirit to diagnose and cure possession or illness. Drumming, chanting, and incense are key components.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a fantastic character moment for Li Zhi. Up until now, he’s been the loud, gluttonous, and slightly annoying comic relief. But “The Spirit Calling” shows us his *professional* face. The transformation from a gossip-hungry village idiot into a focused, chanting ritualist is unsettling and impressive. For Li Huowang, this is a critical observation point. He’s evaluating Li Zhi’s power, and his decision to stay back and watch instead of interfering is very telling of his new, pragmatic, and calculating nature. The horror here isn’t just the contorting woman; it’s the normalcy of the supernatural. This is a village that *knows* what bad luck feels like and can only hope that a wandering spirit-dancer passes through. The chapter’s greatest strength is how it grounds the cosmic horror in a very real, very human desperation.
