## Chapter Overview
Hold onto your souls, fellow readers. This chapter isn’t about a big monster or a flashy ritual—it’s a raw, psychological gut-punch that takes place entirely within the sterile white walls of the modern world. We get one of the most poignant and desperate scenes in the entire novel: Li Huowang’s last-ditch, soul-crushing attempt to reconnect with Yang Na. It’s a chapter that weaponizes the novel’s core theme of reality’s fragility, using the mundane setting of a hospital courtyard to deliver a moment of pure, heartbreaking tragedy. The horror here isn’t a flesh Buddha; it’s the cold, clinical logic of a doctor’s explanation.

## Key Plot Points
- In a fit of wild, desperate hope, Li Huowang believes he can “cultivate” in the real world using his *innate pneuma* (先天一炁) and “wills” Yang Na to turn back and kiss him through the fence.
- Yang Na’s father physically carries her away, but the two share one last, heartbreaking kiss through the cold, diamond-shaped wire of the hospital fence. Li Huowang finally lets go when he sees her waist is about to snap from the strain.
- After Yang Na is gone, Li Huowang is confronted by Sun Xiaoqin and a new doctor. His immediate, paranoid reaction is to treat a hospital broom as a “sword” and count the people before him (five).
- The new doctor, a sharp-witted man with a toupee, doesn’t try to restrain Li Huowang. Instead, he uses a gentle, almost conversational approach to plant a devastating seed of doubt.
- The doctor presents a “subconscious” theory: the coincidences Li Huowang experiences when he wakes from his hallucinations are not supernatural but are self-created desires. He even confesses to a small experiment involving Yang Na’s call, confirming this chilling, mundane explanation.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter presents a fascinating and tragic twist on xianxia concepts. When Li Huowang says he can “cultivate” (修真) in this world, he is trying to use the high-stakes, reality-manipulating logic of the Dao-Twisted World to bend the modern, material world. The *Innate Pneuma*(先天一炁) is a core concept in Daoist internal alchemy (内丹) and qi cultivation—it’s the raw, fundamental energy before it divides into yin and yang. In any normal xianxia, “seeing” your pneuma is a major step. Here, Li Huowang tries to use it without even being able to sense it, driven by pure, mad love. The doctor’s counter-theory is brutal in its simplicity: he replaces the supernatural “coincidence” of the parallel worlds with the mundane psychological mechanism of the subconscious. This is the book’s signature move—offering two perfectly plausible and mutually exclusive explanations for the same phenomenon and letting the reader’s own sanity hang in the balance.

## Reading Guide
Brace yourselves: this is a *cruel* chapter. The high of Li Huowang’s “success” is brutally undercut by the doctor’s clinical, logical dismantling of his hope. Pay close attention to the prose here—the descriptions of the fence carving his face, the strained waist, the doctor’s ridiculous toupee. Every detail in a chapter like this is a calculated piece of atmosphere. The true brilliance of this chapter is that even as a reader, you start to doubt with Li Huowang. Is it magic, or is it just a schizophrenic’s desperate subconscious playing tricks on him? The novel has given you just enough evidence for both sides. The real terror isn’t the monster you can see; it’s the doubt that creeps in when you can’t tell if the world is broken, or if you are.
