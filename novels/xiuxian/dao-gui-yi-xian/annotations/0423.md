## Chapter Overview

The morning after his humiliating expulsion, a bruised and frozen Lü Xiucai drags himself back to Bai Lingmiao to beg for readmission. He has squandered his winnings, he has nowhere to go, and the prospect of his wife Tao’er being “leased” to another man is the final nail in his pride’s coffin. He swallows his resentment and calls her “Mistress.” But this chapter’s true pivot isn’t about a failed schemer returning to the fold—it’s about Bai Lingmiao purchasing a dozen starved children from a human trafficker and christening them her “god-children.” And, with a cold farewell to a slaver who doesn’t know he’s already dead, she signals that the gentle girl is learning to operate in a world where kindness and murder share the same breath.

## Key Plot Points

- Lü Xiucai, broke and injured, returns to beg Bai Lingmiao for forgiveness. He publicly acknowledges Li Huowang as his “Master” and her as “Mistress.”
- Bai Lingmiao notices a group of children being sold by a trafficker and, inspired by memories of her own childhood affluence under the Bai clan’s old system, buys them all.
- The trafficker insists that “slave-keeping is illegal under Great Liang’s imperial edict,” so he reclassifies the sale as “adopted sons and daughters,” complete with deeds.
- Chun Xiaoman is visibly shaken by the sight of human trafficking, recalling her own trauma. Bai Lingmiao comforts her and promises the trafficker won’t live until tomorrow.
- The Second Spirit is dispatched into the empty street as a silent, ominous prelude to an execution that occurs off-screen.
- Bai Lingmiao gives the children generous terms (60% of crops to keep) and a full bag of food, showing that her new hardness doesn’t erase her core kindness.
- Lü Xiucai tries to angle for authority over the group but is shut down when Bai Lingmiao correctly reads his hidden resentment. She then offers him a chance to cultivate a supernatural power, which he accepts—while swearing inwardly to surpass Li Huowang.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**“Leased Concubine” (典妾)** – This is not casual slang. It refers to a grim historical practice in which a poor husband temporarily “pawned” his wife to a wealthier family for the purpose of bearing a male heir. The woman returned after fulfilling the contract, but the transaction was profoundly dehumanizing. Lü Xiucai’s visceral rejection of this idea—even while starving—shows that his pride, however foolish, has a moral line he won’t cross.

**Spirit-Summoning Verse (搬兵决, Bān Bīng Jué)** – A ritual chant originating from northeastern Chinese shamanic (Chuma) traditions. It uses a rapid, rhythmic drumbeat to compel spirits into action. Here, Bai Lingmiao sings it as a dispatch order—the Second Spirit is sent into the empty street to execute the trafficker. The verse isn’t a spell she “casts”; it’s a protocol, an order whispered to a silent agent already bound to her.

**Adopted Children as Bought Labor** – The trafficker’s clever shift from “slave” to “adopted child” reflects a real legal fiction in certain periods. Imperial edicts forbade open slavery, but the poor could be sold under terms like “adopted laborer” or “servant for life.” The system was brutal even on paper, and Bai Lingmiao’s cold awareness—she knows they are *functionally* slaves—marks her growth from naive believer to pragmatic matriarch.

## Reading Guide

Here’s the quiet flex in this chapter: Bai Lingmiao buying twelve starved kids is not *just* charity. It’s a building project. She remembers how powerful the Bai clan used to be—how they had money, assets, a full manpower base—and she’s replicating that. The children are not cannon fodder; they’re *the start of a foundation.* But the *way* she does it—promising fair shares, feeding them immediately, executing the trafficker off-screen out of raw empathy for Xiaoman—tells you her power arc isn’t turning her cold. It’s turning her *competent.* She can be kind and lethal in the same morning.

And then there’s Lü Xiucai. He says all the right words, bows his head, accepts the “worthless” label. But the final line of the chapter—*“I’ll become stronger than Li Huowang!”*—is a mic-drop on his internal resentment. He’s not reformed. He’s *biding.* The schemer is alive and well, just buried under a polite bow.

Also, note the Second Spirit’s silent departure. No dialogue. No dramatics. Just a drumbeat and a walk into an empty street. That’s the Dao-Twisted World’s most efficient promise of death.

## Deeper Dive: The Legal Fiction of “Godchildren”

The trafficker’s line—“We don’t keep slaves in Great Liang, these are plainly your adopted children!”—is steeped in a real-world historical tension. Many pre-modern Chinese dynasties prohibited outright slave markets, but the poor could be “adopted” into de facto servitude. The term *yizi yinü* (义子义女) literally means “righteous son / righteous daughter,” a kinship fiction that allowed the wealthy to command the labor of the destitute without legally violating the ban. The documentary evidence for such “adoptions” is abundant in Ming and Qing legal archives. Bai Lingmiao’s decision to give them sixty percent of their own harvest is genuinely generous by historical standards—many such “godparents” took all the yield and called it *rent.* Her blend of cold pragmatism and leftover warmth is what makes her character arc in this arc so compelling.
