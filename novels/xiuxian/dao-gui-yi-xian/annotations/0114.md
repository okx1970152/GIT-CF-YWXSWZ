## Chapter Overview
Hold onto your scrolls, fellow Daoists, because this chapter is a masterclass in survival logic disguised as a domestic road-trip scene. After the raw terror of the border crossing, we pivot to the Lü Family Troupe—a bunch of traveling opera performers trying to figure out why the world is burning. Lü Zhuangyuan, the grumpy old patriarch with a brain sharper than his pipe, lays out his grand plan with a mix of street wisdom and old-ginger cunning. But beneath the talk of “fate” and “duck eggs” lies the quiet horror of a man who knows that the dynasty is crumbling, and the only safe path is to latch onto the weirdest, most terrifying person on the road: Li Huowang. Oh, and there are stinky nuns hauling silver. Lots of silver. This chapter is a slow-burn setup that trades blood for bureaucracy, and it’s *delicious*.

## Key Plot Points
– Lü Zhuangyuan calms his sons after the soldier robbery, explaining that the chaos signals a “change of dynasty”—a term that lands with cold, pragmatic weight.
– He reveals his sneaky survival strategy: he memorized Li Huowang’s map route (while pretending not to look) and plans to follow the little Daoist all the way to Liang.
– The “jar of salted duck eggs” is elevated from a comedic gift to a strategic investment; Lü Zhuangyuan intends to cash in that goodwill when he begs to travel with the group.
– The troupe’s mood lightens as they fantasize about fresh audiences and new coins, showing how quickly hope can be rebuilt even after trauma.
– They encounter a procession of fat, foul-smelling nuns hauling six heavily loaded wagons. Other locals are following them, suggesting a folk-logic rule: where the locals go, you go.
– After an hour of shared travel, Lü Zhuangyuan spots Zhao Wu—the scarred, leg-injured man from earlier—traveling alone toward Li Huowang.
– A heartwarming reunion turns sour when Lü Xiucai asks Zhao Wu about his homecoming; Zhao Wu’s face shuts down, revealing deep, unspoken trauma.
– The chapter ends on a grotesque punchline: a gust of wind blows open a nun’s wagon curtain, revealing a mountain of white silver inside.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**“Change of dynasty” in Chinese folk logic**: Lü Zhuangyuan isn’t being dramatic. In pre-modern China, a massive influx of defeated soldiers almost always heralded the fall of a dynasty. Common folks didn’t need court historians—they read omens in the road. Empty villages, abandoned fields, and armed bands moving east meant one thing: the Mandate of Heaven was shifting. “The world is going to be chaotic” is the peasant-meteorologist version of a dynasty collapsing.

**“Old ginger is the spiciest”**: A classic Chinese proverb, meaning experience trumps youth. Lü Zhuangyuan’s entire philosophy is built on this. He’s not a scholar or a cultivator—he’s a man who has survived by reading the room, by calculating favors, and by knowing that a jar of duck eggs is worth more than a gold ingot *if you use it right*. This is survival via social chess, not martial prowess.

**Fat, stinky nuns and silver**: In the Dao-Twisted World, religious figures are never just religious figures. These nuns are grotesquely obese, reeking and gluttonous—a visual shorthand for institutional decay. The fact that they’re hauling silver openly, and that locals follow them without question, suggests that this is a known caravan with a powerful patron. The silver is bait, but for whom? And what happens when the bait is seen by the wrong eyes? The curtain-blow scene is a classic “show, don’t tell” setup for future conflict.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a *breather* in the best sense—a transition chapter that lets you catch your breath while the author tightens the noose. Pay close attention to Lü Zhuangyuan’s logic: he is not a passive victim. He is a survivor who weaponizes connections, reads people, and treats “fate” as a tool rather than a mystic force. His plan to follow Li Huowang is both clever and chilling—because if a smart old man like him thinks *Li Huowang* is the safest person on the road, what does that say about the road?

Also, savor the domestic detail. The salted duck eggs, the pipe smoke, the family bickering—these moments make the world feel lived-in. The horror of this novel isn’t just in the flesh Buddhas and the Wenzi; it’s in the quiet realization that even a simple family trip has become a desperate gamble for survival. And that silver? Yeah, that’s not going to stay hidden for long.
