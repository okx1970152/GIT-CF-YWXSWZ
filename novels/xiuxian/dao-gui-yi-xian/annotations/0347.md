## Chapter Overview

Oh, Miao Miao. This chapter is a quiet gut-punch that swaps the usual flesh-sacrifice horror for the slow, creeping dread of a broken heart. We rejoin Bai Lingmiao and Chun Xiaoman on the road back to Niuxin Village. Bai Lingmiao is still performing her duties as a spirit medium—saving a dying child with a visceral, needle-and-scissors ritual—but her eyes are hollow, her smile is gone, and the black ligature mark on her neck is a constant, physical reminder of her trauma. Chun Xiaoman does her best to talk some sense into her, offering everything from blunt honesty about Li Huowang’s flaws to the practical option of becoming a “self-combed woman.” It’s a rare, slower moment that lets us sit with the emotional aftermath, and it hits harder than any monster attack.

## Key Plot Points

- Bai Lingmiao performs a successful exorcism/healing on a deathly-ill child, using drumming, spirit-summoning chants, and uncanny surgery to extract wriggling parasite-like matter from the boy’s palm.
- She refuses payment from the poor father, only accepting fifty coins after heavy insistence.
- Chun Xiaoman rides escort and watches over Bai Lingmiao with worried vigilance; the black scar on Bai’s neck is a persistent, unspoken marker of her trauma.
- At night, Chun Xiaoman tries to console Bai Lingmiao, arguing she needs to move on from Li Huowang’s loss. Her words are blunt, even cruel—calling Li Huowang “crazy” and “a doomed bachelor”—but driven by genuine love.
- Bai Lingmiao’s only response is to curl up and sob, revealing she is still completely consumed by grief.
- The next day, they reach Niuxin Village’s outskirts and encounter Lyu Zhuangyuan heading south for opera work; he asks after his missing youngest son.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Spirit-Summoning Chants (Bang Bing Jue / 帮兵决)**  
The rhythmic, almost hypnotic verses Bai Lingmiao chants belong to the northeastern Chinese *chuma* (出马) spirit-medium tradition. The “drum whip” is a literal ritual tool—a small drumstick tied with colored ribbons—and the entire cadence is designed to create a trance state. The rapid shift from chanting to violent action (stabbing the child’s palm with nail-guided precision) reflects the medium’s role as a channel for a spirit that heals through direct, often brutal, intervention.

**Self-Combed Woman (自梳女)**  
Chun Xiaoman’s suggestion that Bai Lingmiao become a “self-combed woman” references a historical practice from the Pearl River Delta region (especially Guangdong), where women would take a vow of permanent spinsterhood by combing their own hair into a bun in a formal ceremony. It was a way to reject marriage and gain a degree of autonomy, often supported by sisterhood networks. In this context, it’s a practical if blunt offer of a path forward.

**The Black Scar as Physical Trauma Marker**  
The lingering black ligature mark on Bai Lingmiao’s neck—visible even after healing—is a classic *Dao-Twisted World* touch. In this world, emotional and spiritual damage often leaves tangible, unhealing marks on the body. The scar isn’t just a bruise; it’s a physical score left by her ordeal, and Chun Xiaoman’s inability to stop looking at it mirrors the reader’s unease.

### Bang Bing Jue

A traditional northeastern Chinese spirit-summoning chant from the chuma (出马) medium tradition; its rapid, repetitive drum cadence is designed to induce a trance and call spirits into the medium’s body.

### chuma medium

A folk spirit-medium tradition from northeastern China, where the practitioner serves as a ‘mount’ for animal or spirit patrons; rituals often involve drumming, singing, and direct physical intervention.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a character-driven breather, so don’t expect combat or cosmic revelations. Instead, savor the slow burn of Chun Xiaoman’s well-meaning but painful attempt to drag her sister back to life. Her speech is raw, real, and deeply relatable—she’s not a therapist, she’s a survivor using the tools she has (tough love, practical suggestions, and a good arm to hold someone). Bai Lingmiao’s silence and eventual tears are devastating precisely because they’re understated. If you’ve ever watched a friend disappear into grief, this one will tug hard. Also, keep an eye on that brief mention of Lyu Zhuangyuan’s missing youngest son—a quiet seed for future trouble.
