## Chapter Overview

Alright, folks, welcome back to the Bai family's secret underground lair—which, surprise, is apparently bigger than the emperor's palace. This chapter is a slow, creeping descent into the past. Bai Lingmiao is forced to confront the fact that her family, and especially her beloved grandfather, were deeply entangled with a cult that most certainly did not have her best interests at heart. It’s not a chapter of monsters and fights; it’s a chapter of dread, memory, and the horror of finding out that the people you loved and trusted were not who you thought they were. Strap in, because this one digs into the mystery of Niuxin Village and the strange origins of the White Lotus Society right here.

## Key Plot Points

- The chapter opens with Chun Xiaoman interrogating Gao Zhijian about the texts he's been reading, which are the forbidden, disturbing scriptures of the White Lotus Society. Bai Lingmiao is shaken but silent.
- The group discovers that the underground network beneath the ancestral hall is a massive, multi-room complex, far too large for a simple village. It’s a hidden city of cult activity.
- They find a giant, lifelike ceramic horse, which triggers a buried childhood memory in Bai Lingmiao of her grandfather bringing her here to play, and of the horse flying into a painting.
- Bai Lingmiao, acting on a half-remembered instinct, discovers a wall painting that matches her memory. She begins to piece together that the Bai family was not a normal family, and that their secrets run very deep.
- The group lights a series of hidden oil lamps shaped like lotuses, revealing the full scale of an underground grand hall. The centerpiece is a massive mural of six black horses carrying an empty lotus, a symbol that is broken or incomplete.
- They see hundreds of unfinished ceramic statues, suggesting a grand, perhaps insane, project that was never completed. Gouwa is awed by the scale of it all, calling it fit for an emperor.
- The chapter ends with Bai Lingmiao having a poignant, painful memory of herself as a toddler, running into the arms of her loving grandfather in this very hall—a memory that now feels like a prelude to a lie.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The White Lotus Society (白莲教):** This is a huge one. In Chinese history, the White Lotus Society wasn't a single evil cult, but a long-lasting, secret religious tradition that kept popping up over centuries. It was a syncretic faith, drawing from Buddhism, Manichaeism, and folk beliefs. Its central tenants often revolved around an apocalyptic savior figure (Maitreya Buddha) and a Mother Goddess (Wusheng Laomu, who you saw earlier). For the *Dao-Twisted World*, this isn't a historical footnote—it’s the framework for the novel’s cosmic horror. The empty lotus in the mural and the talk of "no birth, no death" are direct references to White Lotus scripture, promising a paradise that is now twisted into a physically threatening reality.

**Ceramic Horses and Rites:** The massive, lifelike ceramic horse isn't just a fancy sculpture. In Chinese funerary art, tomb guardians and spirit animals were common to protect the deceased or escort them to the afterlife. The scale here, and the specific fact that Bai Lingmiao remembers *riding it into a painting*, suggests this was a ritual artifact. It may have been used for shamanic journeys or "opening the way" to a spirit world—a physical portal for a White Lotus rite that Bai Lingmiao was unknowingly subjected to as a child.

**The Empty Lotus and the Black Horses:** In White Lotus iconography, the lotus often represents the "true emptiness" (真空家乡) or the pure land. That it is empty on the mural is deeply significant. The six black horses, however, are a unique invention of this novel. In Chinese cosmology, six is a Yin number, and black horses are often associated with the underworld or with chaotic, untamed energy. This mural, when read as a whole, likely depicts a journey to this "empty land" via a dangerous path, escorted by demonic steeds.

## Reading Guide

This is an "atmosphere and discovery" chapter. Don't look for a big fight scene or a tactical solution. Instead, focus on how Bai Lingmiao’s memories are betraying her. Her grandpa was such a positive figure in her life, and now every happy memory she has is a clue pointing to a terrible truth. The slow reveal of the underground hall, with its meticulous detail and hypnotic scale, is designed to make your skin crawl. Pay close attention to the mural details—the hole in the lotus is not a mistake. It’s a sign of something forcibly removed or a section that was deliberately left blank, which in the Dao-Twisted World, is far more ominous than a completed prophecy.

## Digging Deeper: The Psychology of a Cult Basement

If you’re a fan of horror, you know that the most terrifying places aren’t always the ones covered in blood. They’re the ones that are *wrong*. This underground hall isn't a dungeon; it's a palace. It’s meticulously crafted, with art, complex lighting, and planned architecture. That’s what makes it so unsettling. It wasn't made by a madman in a fever dream, but by organized, systematic, and *committed* belief. The hundred unfinished statues aren't a sign of failure; they’re a testament to the sheer ambition of the cult. It shows you that the White Lotus Society here wasn't just a few fanatics in a cave; it was a full-blown, well-funded, and highly organized underground movement, and the Bai family was at its absolute core.
