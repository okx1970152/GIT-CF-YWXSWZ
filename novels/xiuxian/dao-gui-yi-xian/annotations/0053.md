## Chapter Overview
The chapter opens with our beleaguered, neck-stitched protagonist lying in a donkey cart, the creaking of the wheels underscoring a moment of forced stillness. After the visceral nightmare of Zhengde Temple, Li Huowang is forced to confront the aftermath: the sight of his own swollen belly and blood-soaked hands. The chapter is a masterful slow burn of self-examination, where our hero wrestles not with external monsters, but with the terrifying possibility that the monster *inside* might be the one calling the shots.

## Key Plot Points
Li Huowang is bedridden, his neck freshly sewn by Bai Lingmiao. He directs the simple maintenance of the cart, telling Gouwa to oil the wheels. The quiet, domestic moment is a direct contrast to the horror of the previous chapter. As he stares at the sky, he mentally dissects the truth of what happened at the temple. He realizes a critical schism: he saw it as a battle between the ascended Danyangzi and the Buddhas, but his companions saw *him*, wearing a terrifying expression, devouring the monsters. This is a profound crisis of identity. He tests his suspicion of being changed, asking Bai Lingmiao (who has bandaged her own eyes) if he seems more irritable. The group stops to camp; Chun Xiaoman quietly feeds him. The Lu family troupe finds them, with Lu Zhuangyuan confirming they are back on the road together. That night, as Li Huowang ponders the stars, a trembling body (Bai Lingmiao) seeks comfort in his arms. He offers reassurance, but the chapter ends with the heavy weight of his unresolved inner darkness.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter is a masterclass in post-horror psychology. In traditional xianxia, a protagonist absorbing a power often leads to a neat power-up. Here, the "success" (defeating the enemies) is presented as a psychological catastrophe. The novel uses a classic horror trope of the "unreliable witness"—Li Huowang cannot trust his own memory, and neither can his companions. His question to Bai Lingmiao about his own personality change is a quiet, chilling moment. He is not just afraid of Danyangzi; he is afraid of becoming a vessel that Danyangzi can use, his own body a suit of armor for a predator. The "Heavenly Scripture" is casually used as a pillow, showing its demotion from a mythical artifact to a mundane object of discomfort. The folk tradition of "Lesser Fullness" (Xiaoman) for naming a girl is a nice touch, grounding the world in earthy, rural authenticity. Finally, the chapter reinforces the "Home" theme from earlier: Li Huowang’s inability to define a safe "home" in either the real or the hallucinatory world leaves him in a state of permanent existential crisis, with only the immediate present as his anchor.

## Reading Guide
Welcome to the hangover chapter, fellow Daoists. After the fever-pitch action of the Flesh Buddha, we’re treated to a quiet, sinister character study. This is where *Dao Gui Yi Xian* really gets its hooks in you. The horror isn’t in a monster you can stab, but in an idea you can’t escape: “What if I’m the thing I’m running from?” The creaking cart wheel is a perfect metaphor for Li Huowang’s broken psychology. Pay close attention to the moment he asks Bai Lingmiao if his temper has changed. It’s a tiny question, but it carries the enormous weight of a man who fears he’s being rewritten. The quiet intimacy between them—her bandaged eyes, his stitched neck—is a fragile treaty in a war of one. Get ready for a chapter that trades screaming for seeping dread. It’s a masterful pause that builds the existential threat into something far more terrifying than any temple full of flesh monsters.

## Thematic Deep Dive: The Unreliable Self
This chapter is the novel’s clearest statement on its central theme: the fragmentation of the self. Li Huowang is trapped between two contradictory sets of evidence. His internal memory says one thing (Danyangzi acted), but the physical evidence (his hands, his belly, the testimony of his friends) says another. This is not just a matter of being wrong; it is a fundamental crisis of the "I." He can no longer trust the very process of perception. The novel brilliantly uses the mundane setting of a campfire and a meal to frame this cosmic doubt. It’s not a grand revelation from a deity; it’s a quiet, grinding realisation while being fed soup. This is the horror of the "Heart-Element" made personal: the truth is not found, but constantly re-made through belief and pain, and the one who believes might be the architect of his own nightmare.

## Character Spotlight: Chun Xiaoman
Chun Xiaoman is a quiet but important presence here. She, along with Bai Lingmiao, represents the human element in a world of monstrous choices. Her name, tied to a solar term, roots her firmly in a world that is lived in, not just feared. Her silent, methodical feeding of Li Huowang is an act of pure, non-judgmental care. She does not recoil from him, nor does she offer false comfort. Her simple action, "fur-covered, like a black gorilla," doing something so gentle and human, contrasts powerfully with the inhuman horror that Li Huowang fears is inside him.
