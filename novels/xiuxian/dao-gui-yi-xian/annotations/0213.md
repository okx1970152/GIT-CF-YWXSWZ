## Chapter Overview

Alright, buckle up, folks, because this chapter is *all about the consequences*. After the explosive battle, Li Huowang isn't celebrating—he's dealing with the fallout, both physical and philosophical. He firmly shuts down Bai Lingmiao's naive request for divine powers, revealing the horrifying truth of what it costs to gain Bashe's attention. But the real kicker? Decay has returned to the world. That's right, the body horror is back, baby! This isn't just a random event either; it sends our boy Li Huowang into a full-blown existential spiral, musing about cosmic entities and the nature of the world itself. He’s not just patching up wounds; he's making a permanent, life-altering decision about the *Thousand Greats Record*, ensuring his connection to suffering is literally welded to his flesh. It’s a quiet, gut-wrenching chapter that feels like the eye of the storm—tense, painful, and heavy with portent.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang refuses Bai Lingmiao’s request to learn the *Thousand Greats Record*, explaining the extreme physical and spiritual torment required to gain Bashe’s attention, and his fear that Chun Xiaoman wouldn’t survive the process.
- Li Huowang notices the smell of his own rotting flesh and realizes with genuine relief that "decay" has returned to the Dao-Twisted World, suggesting the world's twisted laws have momentarily normalized.
- This leads to a dangerous train of thought: if decay returned, could the Abbess Jingxin be revived? He then spirals into deeper questions about whether supernatural entities like Bashe, the Flesh Buddha, and a hypothetical "Decay Deity" are at war.
- Forcing himself to stop this dangerous speculation, Li Huowang uses the *True Sutra of the Fire Vestments* to summon a healing fire-slug, which begins treating his severe burns.
- Li Huowang makes a permanent choice: he has Bai Lingmiao press the *Thousand Greats Record* onto his raw, scabbed back, fully intending for his healing skin to embed the artifact permanently under his flesh.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **Truth of the Thousand Greats Record**: This chapter explicitly confirms that the power of an artifact like the *Thousand Greats Record* isn't a simple "skill book." It's a pact with Bashe, a primordial god of pain. Li Huowang's warning clarifies that "divine powers" are just the name for surviving extreme, impossible suffering. It’s not cultivation; it’s torture-as-gateway.
- **Divine Domains & the Pantheon**: Li Huowang’s wild theory is a fantastic peek into the novel's cosmology. He speculates about a deity of decay, connecting it to the same framework as the Flesh Buddha (flesh/desire) and Bashe (pain/suffering). This suggests a world ruled not by virtuous gods but by principle-entities that are the embodiment of abstract horrors. The "Black Fly Bodhisattva" he saw earlier hints that these entities can be layered or corrupted. This is "克苏鲁" (Lovecraftian) logic applied to Chinese folk deities.

### Mushroom Corpse Immortal

A corrupted Daoist concept where a cultivator's transcendence is achieved through rot, decay, and fungal parasitism, inverting the traditional "Corpse Solution" myth.

## Reading Guide

Ready for some *deep lore* and equally *deep wounds*? This isn't the chapter for flashy fights, but it's crucial. Watch how Li Huowang's mind works: he’s constantly oscillating between terrified deduction and deliberate self-censorship. When his thoughts start drifting toward the White Jade Capital, he forces himself to stop because he *knows* thinking about it is a trap. That’s a solid character beat showing his hard-earned wisdom. Also, chew on his decision regarding the *Thousand Greats Record*. It’s a brilliant tactical move—making the artifact part of his body for instant use—but it's also a symbolic act of consuming his own monstrous legacy. He's not just carrying the Record; he's *becoming* it. Pay attention to the quiet heartbreak in this chapter—the simple kindness of him protecting his friends from the same path he walks is a moment of pure humanity in a sea of gore.

## Deeper Dive: The Corpse Debate (尸解仙)

Li Huowang's fixation on the return of decay and Abbess Jingxin's death touches on a deep and morbid corner of Daoist mythology: the concept of the "Corpse Solution Immortal" (尸解仙, Shijie Xian). In traditional Daoist lore, this was a type of immortality achieved by faking one's own death. The practitioner would leave behind a "substitute corpse"—a sword, a talisman, a bamboo staff—that looked exactly like them, to trick the celestial bureaucracy and escape the registers of the dead. But in the twisted world of our beloved disaster-magnet, this concept gets the full *Dao Gui Yi Xian* treatment. What if the Abbess wasn't faking her death to ascend, but was *actually* broken by a conflict so vast it shattered the very concept of decay? Her "corpse" wasn't a clever disguise; it was the waste product of a divine war. The return of decay, then, doesn’t just mean the stench is back—it means the cosmic "game" is back on, and the pieces that fell off the board are being snapped back into place. It’s a terrifying thought, and it’s exactly the kind of cosmic body-horror this novel excels at.
