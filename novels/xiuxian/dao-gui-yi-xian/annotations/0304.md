## Chapter Overview
Li Huowang finally gets a chance to interrogate the Bai clan patriarch, Bai Sai—only to walk away with more questions than answers. The official story about a heated stone and a masked thief doesn’t add up, and Hongzhong’s spectral jab that “this kid is lying to you” sits uneasily in the back of his mind. Still, he swallows his paranoia and plays the part of the gracious benefactor, accepting the clan’s fawning hospitality. But when night falls and he closes his eyes, the true puppet master makes his entrance—not in flesh, but as a hanged white Wuchang in the landscape of Li Huowang’s dream.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang suppresses his reaction to Hongzhong’s warning about Bai Sai, consciously working to keep his face neutral in front of the crowd.
- He interrogates Bai Sai about the “heated orange stone” that supposedly started the conflict. Bai Sai claims it was a natural treasure stolen by a masked youth.
- Li Huowang’s tactical instincts kick in: he notes that the masked man’s behavior (driving captives in cangues instead of killing them) doesn’t fit the logic of simple robbery—suggesting a deeper motive.
- Bai Sai confirms the masked man uses a mental power that makes victims see horrifying illusions, matching Li Huowang’s earlier white Wuchang hallucination.
- The Bai clan fawns over Li Huowang, offering him the best meat and fanning him—a level of deference he finds deeply uncomfortable.
- During the night watch, Bai Sai reveals his understanding of “yang energy” is purely superstitious hearsay, disappointing Li Huowang.
- Li Huowang falls asleep and wakes in a misty dreamscape where a hanged white Wuchang accuses him of ruining its plans.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**White Wuchang (白无常)** is one of the two emissaries of the Chinese underworld, known as the “Heishi Baichang” or Heibai Wuchang (黑白无常). White Wuchang is the benevolent one, said to reward the virtuous and guide good souls to the afterlife. The novel subverts this entirely—here, the image is used as a terror tool and a dream-threat, stripping away its protective function and turning one of folk religion’s “kindly” spirits into a conduit of menace. Also of note: **the copper-coin mask**. As established earlier, this is a concealment artifact woven from old Chinese coins, a jianghu (river-and-lake underworld) method for hiding one’s identity from supernatural perception.

## Reading Guide
Fellow Daoists, strap in—because this chapter is all about *the itch*. You know the one: the feeling that something doesn’t add up, but you can’t quite prove it. Li Huowang has been in this game long enough to smell bullshit from a hot stone away, but he also lacks the information to call it. Bai Sai’s story is smooth, the clan’s hospitality is overwhelming, and even the young man’s hunting prowess is impressive—but Hongzhong’s one-liner hangs over the entire scene like a ghost that refuses to leave. And speaking of ghosts: the dream-sequence finale with the hanged white Wuchang is a masterclass in atmosphere. It’s not a *reveal*—it’s a *question*. “Why did you ruin my business?” Who is this entity, and what was its scheme? The chapter ends before we get an answer, leaving us to stew in uncertainty right alongside Li Huowang.

## Deep Dive — The Logic of Supernatural Bureaucracies
Here’s something worth chewing on: Li Huowang’s instinct to find the masked man’s motive *by looking at his methods* is a skillset born directly from his experience with the Supervisory Heavenly Office and other “official” supernatural bodies. In the Dao-Twisted World, organizations like the Office don’t operate on emotion—they follow procedure. They *stabilize*. So when a masked man drives a whole clan in cangues for hundreds of li despite having overwhelming power, Li Huowang smells a systematic purpose, not random cruelty. This is the kind of deduction that separates a survivor from a victim: he’s not looking at the horror, he’s looking at the paperwork logic *behind* it.
