## Chapter Overview

This chapter is a quiet, character-driven breather that lets Bai Lingmiao step into the spotlight—and it’s a treat. We watch her navigate a small-town exorcism case with a mix of rookie jitters and growing competence, as she faces a classic piece of Chinese folk horror: a reanimated corpse that comes back craving raw meat. The pacing slows down to let her process, plan, and even haggle. It’s a grounded, earthy horror vignette that reminds us how far she’s come, while also setting the stage for a night that promises to be anything but peaceful.

## Key Plot Points

- Bai Lingmiao, following the Second Spirit’s guidance, arrives at a poor farmer’s home in Qianjia Village to investigate a suspected possession case.
- Her initial knock is met with a slammed door after she bluntly asks if anyone is “possessed by an evil spirit,” revealing her social inexperience with such sensitive inquiries.
- After some internal debate with the Second Spirit, she successfully negotiates entry, quoting the standard rate of fifty coppers for a spirit-dance exorcism.
- The farmer and his son explain the horror in plain, dry language: their deceased matriarch, buried in haste after a mild “happy funeral,” returned from the grave the very next night.
- The reanimated corpse did not speak or curse—she simply demanded raw meat and devoured an entire cured pork leg like a starving animal, before promising to return the following night.
- Bai Lingmiao assesses the danger with the Second Spirit, judges it manageable, and volunteers to stay overnight to perform the exorcism.
- The chapter closes with Bai Lingmiao alone in the dark, lantern-lit hall, nervously awaiting the corpse’s return.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is steeped in specific Chinese folk belief and ritual practice. Let’s break down the key elements:

**The "Happy Funeral" (喜丧, xǐ sāng):** The old man mentions his wife’s death was a “happy funeral” because she lived past seventy and passed without prolonged suffering. In Chinese rural tradition, a *xǐ sāng* is not mourned with pure grief—it marks a life well-lived and a natural transition. The intense horror of the story comes precisely from this peace being violated: a “happy” death should not produce a hungry, violent corpse.

**Tou Qi (头七, First Seventh Day):** The farmer mentions the trouble began before the deceased’s *first seventh day*—the seventh day after death. In Chinese folk custom, the soul returns home on this day. The family keeps vigil to welcome it, often laying out food or scattering ash to see if the spirit leaves footprints. Here, the vigil is broken by knocking from inside the coffin, a classic Chinese zombie/jiangshi story beat—the dead should not be moving before their soul’s final visit.

**The Second Spirit as an Internal Advisor:** Bai Lingmiao’s mental dialogue with the Second Spirit is now fully integrated into her routine. She doesn’t just follow commands; they *discuss* danger levels and strategies. This reinforces her growth from a passive vessel into an active medium who collaborates with her spirit partner.

**The Price of Exorcism:** Fifty coppers for a spirit-dance is a fixed price, suggesting a standardized local economy for supernatural services. The father’s suspicion that she might “cheat” them because they’re poor hints at the precarious trust between rural villagers and wandering ritual specialists.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a wonderful change of pace after the intense, high-stakes horror of recent arcs. It’s *domestic horror*—the terror of someone you love coming back wrong. The prose is stripped down, focusing on the raw, almost mundane details: a knocked coffin lid, a gnawed pork leg, a pair of terrified farmers squatting in their own yard. Bai Lingmiao’s voice here is refreshingly human: flustered when rejected, quietly proud when accepted, and nervous as she waits for the night. The horror builds not through jump scares but through the simple, dreadful anticipation of a door opening after midnight. It’s a classic setup, executed with the novel’s signature folk-horror authenticity.
