## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang snaps awake from yet another encounter with the pursuing specter—except this time, he realizes the horror isn’t a wandering demon at all, but a *government agent*. In a chapter built on a single devastating rug-pull, the novel flips our understanding of the entire Bai family pursuit arc. The White Wuchang isn’t a folkloric nightmare; it’s a masked operative of the Supervisory Heavenly Office. The Bai family isn’t a band of pitiful refugees; they’re wanted heretics. And Bai Sai? That avuncular clan head who just gave Li Huowang his blessing to marry Bai Lingmiao? He’s the one who’s been lying this whole time. What starts as a tense chase through a muddy swamp dissolves into a crisis of trust—and a masterclass in how *Dao Gui Yi Xian* weaponizes bureaucratic horror over supernatural terror.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang snaps awake and immediately notices a toddler playing with his copper coin mask, grounding him back in the mundane morning routine.
- He privately warns Bai Sai that the White Wuchang is still watching them in his dreams.
- Bai Sai, visibly terrified at first, recovers after Li Huowang reassures him of protection and shifts the mood by openly acknowledging Li Huowang’s relationship with Bai Lingmiao.
- The group buys up livestock from a village, significantly speeding up their travel.
- Bai Sai leads the group off the path to Niuxin Mountain, toward a vast reed marsh he claims will shake any pursuit.
- The White Wuchang manifests in the mist as they enter the reeds; Li Huowang orders the group ahead and stands alone to face it.
- Remembering Tuoba Danqing’s advice, Li Huowang shouts “Supervisory Heavenly Office” as a threat.
- The White Wuchang *responds* as a person, revealing itself to be a fellow Office agent—one rank higher than Li Huowang.
- The agent explains that he and his senior brother were hunting the White Lotus Society, a sect of heretical cultivators colluding with evil; Li Huowang inadvertently broke their seal and freed them.
- Bai Sai, seeing Li Huowang’s Office token, turns cold and flees into the reeds, confirming his clan’s guilt.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The White Wuchang (白无常)** here gets a vicious subversion. In traditional Chinese folk religion, Black and White Wuchang are underworld messengers who escort souls of the dead. White Wuchang (the taller, tongue-lolling one) is typically associated with bringing blessings or fortune to the living. The novel weaponizes this familiarity: the reader and Li Huowang both assume this is a genuine supernatural entity, only for the mask to be pulled off—literally—to reveal a human bureaucrat underneath. It’s a horror of mis-categorization: the threat isn’t a god, but a man with a badge and a grudge.

**The Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司)** is depicted here with the cold logic of a state institution. It doesn’t care about good vs. evil in a moral sense; it cares about stability. The agent’s tone—casual, almost aggrieved (“you cut my seal!”)—is that of a professional annoyed that his paperwork has been disrupted. This bureaucratic framing makes the horror feel systemic rather than personal, which is often more unsettling.

**The White Lotus Society (白莲教)** reference is brief but loaded. Historically, the White Lotus was a long-standing secret religious society in China, often associated with millenarian rebellions and the worship of the Eternal Mother (Wusheng Laomu). In the Dao-Twisted World, calling them “heretical cultivators colluding with evil spirits” is effectively a death sentence from the Office—whether it’s true or just a convenient cover for state violence.

## Reading Guide

Hold onto your hats, folks—this chapter is a gut-punch that recontextualizes nearly everything that came before it. The big takeaway? **Li Huowang has been played.** Bai Sai’s earlier fear, his gratitude, even that warm “When will I drink at your wedding?” speech—all of it was a performance to secure Li Huowang’s protection against the Office. The moment Bai Sai saw the token, his mask dropped too. The chapter ends with the Office agent dropping a rhetorical bomb: “If they hadn’t killed so many people, would the higher-ups have sent two agents?” That’s not a question; it’s a challenge to Li Huowang’s entire moral compass. Who, exactly, has he been helping? The answer, as of this chapter, is: probably the wrong people. The subtle skill here is that the novel never tells us Bai Sai is guilty with certainty—the evidence is strong, but the doubt is planted beautifully. Li Huowang’s next decision will define his arc from here.

## Deeper Dive: The Art of the Bureaucratic Horror Reveal

This chapter is a textbook example of *Dao Gui Yi Xian’s* signature move: taking a perfectly scary supernatural setup and revealing that the *real* horror is human institutions wielding mundane power. The White Wuchang isn’t scary because it’s a ghost; it’s scary because it’s a man with a mask and a badge who can *legally* destroy the Bai family. The mist, the swamp, the eerie silence—all of it was stagecraft. The Office agent’s casual line, “I’m actually a rank higher than you,” is funnier and more terrifying than any monster’s roar. It tells us: you’re not fighting a god; you’re fighting the system. And the system has protocols.
