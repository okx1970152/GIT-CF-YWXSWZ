## Chapter Overview

We’re back in the aftermath, fellow Daoists—and the bill has come due. Chapter 294 opens with Gouwa’s grubby little hustle (haggling for a coffin he no longer needs) before dropping us straight into Li Huowang’s shattered bedside. Our boy survived the Zuowandao showdown, but the price tag keeps growing: Chun Xiaoman aged years from summoning Wandering Lords, the group is jumpy, and Li Huowang’s head is a haunted house he can’t evict. The chapter’s real meat? That gnawing, inescapable question: *Was the double-pupiled liar really lying about everything, or is there a pattern he can’t explain?* And then—because this novel hates letting us breathe—his hallucinations physically manifest inside his throat. Get ready for a quiet, vicious little chapter about what happens after the battle ends.

## Key Plot Points

- Gouwa’s character gets a sharp, comic sketch: transactional mourning, immediate regret over the sunk cost of a coffin, and carefully managed cowardice. The turmeric-under-the-eyes trick is pure theater.
- The group’s physical and metaphysical injuries are tallied: Chun Xiaoman’s accelerated aging confirms the brutal cost of Wandering Lord summoning (four of them cost a full year of life). Li Huowang offers her Lifespan Pills from his gourd, which she refuses.
- Li Huowang’s cognitive engine won’t shut off. He retraces the Zuowandao’s claim (being called “Red Center”) and lands on a chilling logical gap: why did the seventh liar use the *exact same* scheme as the first six? His conclusion—that the dead and living Zuowandao colluded across death—is both paranoid and disturbingly plausible in this world.
- Gouwa presents the spoils: a Bagua mirror, bloodstained gambling tiles. Li Huowang momentarily relaxes, misreading them as proof he passed the Supervisory Heavenly Office’s test and can now join. It’s a fragile, desperate hope.
- Then the hallucination returns—and it’s *worse*. The faceless Zuowandao is now part of his menagerie of ghosts (Peng Longteng, Jin Shan Zhao, the old monk). Li Huowang’s breakdown is sudden, violent, and physical: he shoves everyone away and starts vomiting black tentacles.
- The chapter’s final, gut-punch beat: Li Huowang deliberately pushes the tentacles *back* into his throat. The Black Tai Sui is not an invader to expel—it’s a survival tool he keeps on purpose.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Why the hell is Li Huowang swallowing the Black Tai Sui?** This is where the novel’s twisted internal logic shines. Black Tai Sui (黑太岁) is a grotesque parasitic entity, but remember: in the Dao-Twisted World, poison and remedy are often the same thing. Earlier in the arc, Li Huowang learned that the Black Tai Sui suppresses his hallucinations—it’s a biological cage for the madness. By swallowing it back, he’s not being disgusting for the sake of shock value; he’s making a cold, desperate choice to keep the *functional* parasite inside him so he can think straight. It’s body horror as self-medication. Also worth noting: traditional Tai Sui (太岁) is a revered folk deity associated with the yearly calendar and earth spirits—its corruption into a wriggling, flesh-eating substance is pure *Dao Gui Yi Xian* alchemy-as-horror.

**The Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司)** is also getting a quiet lore drop here. Li Huowang believes joining them will give him “backing”—protection, resources, a way out of blind guesswork. This is the first time we see it framed as a genuine hope for stability, not just a scary bureau. But after what we’ve seen of institutional piety in this novel… let’s just say we’re not holding our breath.

## Reading Guide

This chapter does something *Dao Gui Yi Xian* does brilliantly: it turns the post-battle lull into a psychological pressure cooker. There’s no new monster, no chase scene. Just Li Huowang’s broken body and his broken brain arguing over what’s real. Pay close attention to the hallucination lineup—it’s getting *crowded* in there. The faceless Zuowandao means this liar left a deeper scar than the ones before, and that open skull-grin is going to haunt you.

Also, the Gouwa material is a masterclass in dark comic relief. He’s a survivor—a weasel with a budget—and his panic over the coffin money is both funny and quietly tragic. This is a world where even grief has a price tag, and Gouwa’s transactional view of death is just another form of the novel’s core horror: normalizing the abnormal until it’s just another line item.

And that final image—Li Huowang on his knees, cradling his own parasitic tentacles like treasure, shoving them back down his throat—is the chapter’s thesis statement. Survival in this world means learning to keep the horror *inside* you, on purpose. It’s not pretty. It’s not clean. But it’s how you live to see the next chapter.
