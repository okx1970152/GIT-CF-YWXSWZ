## Chapter Overview

This chapter is a quiet, character-driven breather after the storm, but its emotional weight hits just as hard as any monstrous battle. We find our survivors catching their breath in the palace, only to realize that victory comes with a new kind of battlefield—the human heart. Chun Xiaoman gets a taste (literally) of royal bird’s nest porridge and finds it underwhelming, but Gao Zhijian, now Emperor of Great Liang, has a much heavier dish to serve. His confession to Chun Xiaoman is raw, stuttering, and heartbreakingly sincere—only to be met with her furious, ironclad refusal. It’s a soul-crushing moment of “right person, wrong world,” and it sets the stage for Gao Zhijian’s grim acceptance of his imperial duty to produce heirs. Meanwhile, our three stooges—Gouwa, Zhao Wu, and Lu Xiucai—provide some much-needed comedic relief by being caught red-handed spying by a floating, creepy eunuch. The chapter ends with Gao Zhijian’s lingering terror: is the Dice truly gone, or is it just sleeping inside him?

## Key Plot Points

- **Gao Zhijian’s Confession and Rejection**: Gao Zhijian finds Chun Xiaoman alone in the imperial kitchen and confesses his love for her, stuttering in a way that harkens back to his simpler days. He admits he wants her to be his Empress, but he cannot bear the thought of their potential children being forced into a murderous succession war. Chun Xiaoman, fiercely independent, rejects him outright, threatening even to sever their sect-sibling bond if he persists.
- **Chun Xiaoman’s Hidden Pain**: After storming out, Chun Xiaoman immediately drops her tough facade, sliding down a wall and burying her head in her knees, her eyes red. It’s a powerful reminder that her refusal isn’t born from cruelty, but from a deep, pragmatic sadness. She then runs after Bai Lingmiao.
- **The Three Stooges Get Caught**: Gouwa, Zhao Wu, and Lu Xiucai, hiding behind a pillar, watch the whole confrontation. Zhao Wu hilariously misinterprets Chun Xiaoman’s rejection as a sign she might be into him. Their cover is blown when Gao Zhijian approaches, and they are escorted away by a gloomy eunuch who had been silently listening to everything.
- **The Imperial Burden**: Alone, Gao Zhijian gives in to the pressure from his advisors. He orders the selection of an Empress and imperial concubines, fully aware that he is sacrificing his own desires for the “Dragon Vein” and the future of his dynasty.
- **The Lingering Fear of the Dice**: Gao Zhijian asks the Chief Astrologer a terrifying question: is he truly cured of the Dice’s possession? The Astrologer cannot give a definitive answer, pointing to Li Huowang as the only one who might know, leaving the reader with a chilling thread of uncertainty.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **The Mandate of Heaven (气运) as a Curse**: This chapter gives visceral weight to the novel’s twisted take on the “Mandate of Heaven.” In this world, it’s not a celestial reward but a parasitic, transactional burden. Gao Zhijian isn’t ruling; he’s being *used* by the Dragon Vein, forced to produce heirs who will likely kill each other to keep the dynasty going. It’s a grim inversion of the standard xianxia trope of imperial glory.
- **Selection of Empress and Concubines (选皇后 / 选秀女)**: This is a real historical practice. The process of selecting an Empress and “show maidens” for imperial consorts was a massive political and ritual event. Gao Zhijian’s resigned order is a heavy moment, as it signifies the end of his personal freedom and his total absorption into the machinery of the state.
- **The Chief Astrologer (司天监)**: The *Sitian Jian* was a real historical office responsible for astronomy, calendar-making, and interpreting celestial omens. In the Dao-Twisted World, this role has been merged with that of a fate-weighing mystic who casually divines imminent disaster (“next year will likely be difficult”). The contrast between his profound duty and his mundane, physical description (taller than Gao Zhijian) is a classic Dao-Twisted touch.
- **Imperial Kitchen (御膳房) vs. Folklore**: The presence of a vast imperial kitchen with wok-tall chefs is a nod to the sheer scale of courtly logistics. Chun Xiaoman’s casual “invasion” of this sacred space is a character beat showing her lack of awe for tradition. The mention of “bird’s nest porridge” (edible swiftlet nests) is a real, luxury Chinese health food, grounding the fantastical palace in tangible culinary culture.

### Mandate of Heaven (气运)

In this novel's context, the Mandate is a stolen fragment of divine power that holds up reality; it can only be supported by a family willing to commit self-slaughter, making it a curse as much as a blessing.

### Selection of concubines (选秀女)

A historical imperial practice where the court would select young women from noble families to serve as potential consorts; in the novel, it represents the ultimate loss of personal autonomy for the Emperor.

## Reading Guide

Get your emotional armor ready, fellow Daoists. This chapter is a masterclass in “after the victory, the real war begins.” Gao Zhijian’s confession is a brutal case of “right love, wrong time.” He’s finally the Emperor, but that power only makes his inability to protect his own happiness more agonizing. Chun Xiaoman’s rejection is a stunning display of self-respect. She knows her own worth, and she refuses to be a pawn in a dynastic tragedy, no matter how much she might care for him. Her hidden tears are the real gut-punch.

Also, keep an eye on that last exchange with the Chief Astrologer. The chapter ends on a note of deep, unresolved dread. Gao Zhijian’s terror of the “Dice” is completely justified. The novel is reminding us that even in moments of apparent peace, the metaphysical horror is never fully gone. It’s a quiet cliffhanger that promises the calm won’t last.

And for some lighter fare, enjoy the trio of Gouwa, Zhao Wu, and Lu Xiucai fumbling their spy mission. It’s a welcome dose of levity that makes the surrounding tragedy hit even harder. This is a chapter about the cost of victory, and it’s paid in loneliness.
