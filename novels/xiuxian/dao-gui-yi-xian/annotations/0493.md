## Chapter Overview

Alright, fellow Daoists, buckle up—this chapter is a full-scale battle royale for the soul of Niuxin Village. Gao Zhijian goes full one-man-army with his military halberd, Chun Xiaoman unleashes the Wandering Lord on a budget (three years of life, ouch), and a mysterious Cult of the Yu’er God launches a night raid with disturbing fervor. The tone is pure desperation: farming tools versus flesh-cutting weapons, divine possession versus shadow-stabbing assassins. This is not a heroic last stand—it’s a bloody, messy, close-call survival fight where every victory costs flesh, years, or both. Get ready for some hard-hitting folk-horror combat that shows exactly how far these villagers have come—and how much further they’ll have to go.

## Key Plot Points

- The cult followers, draped in black cloth, attack Niuxin Village at night with farm tools and fanatical zeal. They chant about reincarnating as emperors, showing a distorted blend of folk religion and imperial fantasy.
- Gao Zhijian, fighting without Peng Longteng’s armor, holds the line alone with his giant halberd but starts to get overwhelmed by sheer numbers.
- A needle with white fuzz strikes a cultist, causing his features to melt off his face—an early sign of supernatural intervention from the villagers’ side (Gouwa’s *ruyi*).
- Chun Xiaoman activates the Wandering Lord (three-year lifespan cost) to mass-disable cultists, then pivots to a Spirit Invocation (Divine Strike / Shén Dǎ) ritual alongside Lü Xiucai and four others, making them temporarily invulnerable.
- The tide turns in the villagers’ favor, but an old woman in a tattered kasaya, with a “巫” (wu) character on her face-covering, emerges from Gao Zhijian’s shadow and stabs him with a black jade dagger.
- The old woman is fast and lethal: she kills a Spirit-Invoked girl with a single neck slash, proving the dagger bypasses the ritual’s protection.
- Chun Xiaoman pays three more years of life to multiply the Wandering Lord to ten entities, forcing the old woman out of hiding.
- Gouwa uses the Heart-Element tongue on his *ruyi* to disorient her, and Gao Zhijian crushes her with his halberd, ending the immediate threat.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Shén Dǎ (神打 / Divine Strike / Spirit Invocation)**  
This chapter features a vivid display of *Shén Dǎ*, a folk martial-arts and religious practice where the practitioner invokes supernatural protection—usually from a deity or a master—to make their body invulnerable. The ritual here follows a distinct formula: the practitioner chants, cuts their own brow to draw blood, and visualizes armor covering every part of the body. In real-world Chinese folk religion, *Shén Dǎ* is associated with groups like the Boxers and various secret societies; the incantation often reinforces a sense of collective invincibility. The novel weaponizes the practice by showing its limits: the black jade dagger bypasses the protection entirely, adding a layer of horror that even faith has a counter.

**The Yu’er God (于儿神 / Yú’er Shén)**  
This name comes straight from the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* (*Shan Hai Jing*), one of China’s oldest mytho-geographical texts. The Yu’er God is described as having a human head and two serpentine bodies. In this chapter, the cult followers chant about reincarnating as emperors under this deity, suggesting a local, corrupted variant of the myth, used as a justification for suicidal attacks. The novel grounds its supernatural horror in real classical mythology, which makes the weirdness feel gritty rather than abstract.

**Invulnerability and Its Counter**  
The concept of a “copper skin and iron bones” body is a staple in Chinese martial arts fiction. Usually, it’s a high-level Qi Refining or Foundation Establishment technique. Here, it’s a desperate, low-level ritual that works – until it encounters a weapon specifically designed to break it. The black jade dagger with the “巫” character implies a specialized artifact for countering divine protection, possibly tied to a rival occult tradition (the Cult of the Yu’er God’s “witch” / *wu* specialist).

### Classic of Mountains and Seas (山海经, Shān Hǎi Jīng)

An ancient Chinese compendium of mythic geography, fantastical creatures, and folklore; a primary source for many monsters and deities in the Dao-Twisted World.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a perfect example of how *Dao Gui Yi Xian* handles group combat: not with rigid formations or glorious strategies, but with desperate, scrappy, cost-driven tactics. Gao Zhijian’s brute strength, Chun Xiaoman’s lifespan-burning commands, and Gouwa’s opportunistic artifact use all feel earned because the novel has shown us their costs. The Wandering Lord, for instance – Chun Xiaoman pays *three years of life* for the multiplication, and the narrative doesn’t sugarcoat it. That’s a recurring theme: power in this world is never free, and the characters grow by learning which prices they’re willing to pay.

Also, watch how the cult’s fanaticism is depicted. They’re not cartoon villains; their chant about reincarnating as emperors reveals a genuine belief system, twisted but internally coherent. This makes them scarier than mindless zombies – they *want* to die for their god. The contrast between their fervor and the villagers’ grim, pragmatic survival instinct is the emotional core of the battle.

Finally, note the “巫” character on the old woman’s face-covering. In ancient China, *wu* referred to spirit-mediums or shamans, often female. The novel uses this historical marker to signal that she is a *wu* practitioner, not a generic cultivator—a reminder that the Dao-Twisted World draws power from multiple, ancient traditions, not just one system.
