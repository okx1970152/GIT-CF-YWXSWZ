## Chapter Overview
Welcome back, fellow Daoists, to the hangover after the ghost opera. Last night’s terror is over—the monsters are dead (or banished, or both)—and Li Huowang is sitting down to a fat, greasy meal with the Lü family troupe. But while everyone else is busy wolfing down braised pork and thanking their lucky stars, Li Huowang is stuck in a funk. His master’s voice whispered in his ear during that ritual, and now he can’t stop asking the one question that terrifies him more than any monster: *Did Danyangzi actually ascend? And if not an immortal… what did he become?*

This chapter is a quiet breather—food, laughter, shared relief—but underneath the surface, Li Huowang is drowning in the novel’s core existential dread. He’s not sure if he can trust his ears, or his eyes, or even his own diagnosis. The chapter ends with him pocketing his silver and making a pragmatic decision: head to Xijing City and find out what those Buddhist monks know. Sometimes a feast is just a feast. But for Li Huowang, even a good meal tastes a little like uncertainty.

## Key Plot Points
- **The Lü family feasts like royalty.** After last night’s supernatural showdown, the troupe is treated to a lavish spread by the Hu family. Lü Zhuangyuan bosses his sons around, Lü Xiucai eats himself stupid, and the whole table is vibrant with the crude, loving chaos of a family that just survived something terrible.
- **Li Huowang is stuck on a single detail.** The voice he heard during the ritual was unmistakably Danyangzi’s. It was calling him *wa*—*lad*—the only term Danyangzi ever used for him. This tiny fact drives him into a spiral of self-doubt: *Is it a hallucination from skipping the Black Tai Sui? Or is Danyangzi actually back, changed into something worse than an immortal?*
- **Li Huowang’s internal logic hits a wall.** He remembers that the ascension method and the inner pill were complete fabrications—lies he made up on the spot. If those lies can’t produce a real immortal, then Danyangzi’s transformation after death must be something else. The possibility is both a logical puzzle and a personal horror.
- **Lü Zhuangyuan collects payment and splits the silver.** After the employer leaves, Lü Zhuangyuan hands Li Huowang six of the ten silver ingots, honoring their deal. It’s a small but meaningful moment: Li Huowang is now integrated into the troupe’s economic survival, not just their supernatural bodyguard.
- **Li Huowang commits to his next destination.** He decides to head to Xijing City and investigate the temple there, hoping the monks might shed light on what Danyangzi has become. It’s a pragmatic step forward, but the chapter ends with him still carrying the weight of unanswered questions.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
- **Lü Zhuangyuan’s house rules.** The argument over offering chicken to a Daoist guest touches on a real cultural distinction: Buddhist monks (和尚) are the ones who forbid meat, while lay Daoist priests (道士) may or may not observe dietary restrictions depending on lineage. Lü Zhuangyuan’s sharp retort (“*Fuck your mother’s fart!*”) is both a character beat and a folk-knowledge moment.
- **Memorial tablets falling as a sign.** In Chinese folk religion, memorial tablets (牌位) are physical anchors for ancestral spirits. If they fall or move without visible cause, it’s often interpreted as a supernatural signal—a presence, a reaction, a message from the other side. Li Huowang’s focus on this detail shows that even a *xinsu* is still subject to the folk logic of the Dao-Twisted World.
- **Ten silver ingots.** A silver ingot (元宝) of this size is a significant sum in a pre-modern economy. Ten taels could support a small family for months. This feast and payout represent a major windfall for the Lü troupe, raised from a single night’s harrowing work. It underlines the economic desperation that drives these opera families into the arms of dangerous patrons.
- **Xi Shen’s disappearance.** Last chapter, a terrifying entity called Xi Shen appeared and vanished. Li Huowang’s uncertainty about whether it was *real* or a *hallucination* is—if you’ve read this far—the entire thesis of the novel. The line between the two worlds isn’t just blurry; it’s actively maintained by Li Huowang’s own broken perception.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is the quiet after the storm, but don’t skim it—the silence is *loaded*. Li Huowang is not chilling out; he’s recalibrating. The fact that he hears Danyangzi’s voice *at all* is the most dangerous plot development in the book so far. Is it relapse? Is it ascension? Is it a new kind of haunting? The answer is probably all three, because *Dao Gui Yi Xian* hates clean answers.

Pay close attention to how Li Huowang talks himself in circles. *“I’m a mental patient… but what if?”* That oscillation—between self-diagnosis and existential terror—is his core voice. He uses his own illness as a tool to doubt reality, but also as a shield to avoid facing something worse. And the way Lü Zhuangyuan treats him… not as a patient, not as a god, but as a *valuable partner* in survival… that’s new for Li Huowang. It’s almost normal. Almost.

Also, the food. The novel is about horror, yes, but also about *living*. The scene where the family tears into a roast chicken is a rare moment of warmth in a world full of mutilated vase-girls and flesh Buddhas. Let yourself enjoy it. Then remember that the voice in Li Huowang’s ear isn’t done with him yet.
