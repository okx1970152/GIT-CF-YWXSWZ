## Chapter Overview

Poor Li Huowang. No sooner does he catch a whiff of the temple’s rotten underbelly than Abbot Xinhui drags him back to the same carving yard and shows him… perfectly ordinary stone statues. Lions, qilins, even a serene Buddha—all solid rock. Xinhui even chops off a stone lion’s head to prove it. Our schizo-detective is left clutching the piece, wondering if his own mind has betrayed him again. Is the Black Tai Sui wearing off? Or is the temple gaslighting him on an industrial scale? In true *Daoguai Yixian* fashion, the chapter forces both Li Huowang and the reader to sit with that agonizing uncertainty—and then twists the knife with a four-stick incense offering that feels like walking into a predator’s mouth. The payoff comes when he’s finally alone: that wall-punching rage and the defiant whisper that the Buddha *is* a lie. This is Li Huowang at his most paranoid and most lucid—a man learning to trust his sickness.

## Key Plot Points

- Xinhui brings Li Huowang and the hostile monks to the sculpture yard, where the earlier writhing flesh-monsters have reverted to unfinished stone statues. Li Huowang is stunned.
- Xinhui severs a stone lion’s head with Li Huowang’s own sword and hands it to him. The weight and texture are undeniably real.
- Li Huowang frantically touches every statue—qilins, the great Buddha in the hall—and they are all solid stone. He cannot reconcile this with his earlier vision.
- Xinhui calmly tells Li Huowang he is very ill, implying the horrific visions were hallucinations. The monks whisper “madman” among themselves.
- Li Huowang clutches his head, wondering aloud if the Black Tai Sui he consumed has worn off, causing fresh delusions.
- Xinhui lights *four* sticks of incense and orders Li Huowang to make amends to the Buddha. Li Huowang’s instincts scream that the Buddha was a parasite-monster; he hesitates.
- Under Xinhui’s displeased gaze, Li Huowang forces himself to approach, bow three times, and insert the incense. Nothing transforms.
- Walking back, Li Huowang engages Xinhui in polite conversation: he learns about the Heaven–Earth–Profound–Yellow tier system, Xinhui’s teleportation trick (“a small art”), and that Danyangzi was only “mid-tier.”
- Back in his room, Li Huowang drops the mask. He slams the wall and declares that the stone carvings were all illusions—his senses were right all along. The temple is hiding something.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The **Heaven–Earth–Profound–Yellow** (天地玄黄) tier system is a classic cultivation ranking borrowed from the *Thousand Character Classic*, used in many xianxia works to loosely grade power. Xinhui dismisses it as “bored people’s invention,” which is both a humble-brag and a signal that the temple operates outside such frameworks. **Four sticks of incense** (四根香) carry a superstitious sting: in Chinese folk practice, odd numbers (especially three) are standard for worship; four is associated with death and offerings to spirits—a subtle, unsettling detail in an already tense scene. The **Black Tai Sui** (黑太岁) is a parasitic entity Li Huowang has been consuming to suppress his mind; its failure serves as the plot’s mechanics for doubt. Finally, the temple’s apparent ability to turn fleshy horror into stone suggests a **large-scale illusion or reality-manipulation** skill, tying into the novel’s core theme: the Dao-Twisted World’s high-end actors can bend perception at will.

## Reading Guide

Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s internal monologue—it’s a masterclass in forced calm under institutional pressure. He plays the cooperative patient, asks the right questions, and even jokes about his madness, but the moment the door shuts, the mask shatters. This is a key character beat: he is learning to trust his “illness” as a superior sensor. Also note Xinhui’s teleportation trick—a casual display of power that dwarfs Danyangzi’s alchemy. The chapter asks you to decide: is Li Huowang being expertly gaslit, or is he truly slipping? The ambiguity is the point. After this, every subsequent scene at Zhengde Temple should be read with heightened suspicion toward every kindly monk and every smooth stone surface.
