## Chapter Overview

Fellow Daoists, strap in—this chapter is a masterclass in political tightrope walking, where Ji Lin reveals himself as a far more dangerous predator than our good friend Li Huowang anticipated. Our boy's original plan of using a puppet emperor just got a reality check the size of a dragon throne. With the Dice still out there and Bai Lingmiao's life on the line, Li Huowang pivots hard: forget the princess, he needs to find the Si Tian Jian, the celestial monster-hunting bosses of the capital. But just as he's about to make his move, the familiar chaos of the Lü troupe rolls back into his life like a manure cart hitting a pothole, dragging nostalgia and trouble right to his feet.

## Key Plot Points

Li Huowang delivers his report on the Prince of Ren's death, only to realize Ji Lin has been playing a much faster and deadlier game than he thought. The emperor has been systematically eliminating his siblings with alarming speed—and he expects Li Huowang to finish the last one, Princess Anping. The brief exchange in the throne room is a cold chess match: Ji Lin flexes his newly acquired power, including the defection of the Prince of Ren's former asset, the Wen Cai Shen. Li Huowang takes the edict but immediately decides to stall. He has no intention of killing the clever princess—she's using a body double, which buys him time. On the third day, a carrier pigeon brings a lead from Liu Zongyuan: the Si Tian Jian will be leaving through the East Gate on the twenty-third. Li Huowang drops everything and races to the gate, but fate sends the Lü troupe crashing into his stakeout, forcing him to juggle his old responsibilities with his new, high-stakes objective.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The Wen Cai Shen (文财神), or Civil God of Wealth, is a figure from Chinese folk religion. In the novel's world, it's not just a statue—it's a supernatural asset that can be seized along with a defeated rival's court. Ji Lin inheriting this entity from the Prince of Ren is a major power signal; it implies he has control over wealth-channeling rituals or divination assets that go far beyond simple money.

The Si Tian Jian (司天监)—literally "Supervisor of the Celestial Observatory"—was a historical position in ancient Chinese courts responsible for astrology, calendar-making, and interpreting omens. In this novel's twisted cosmology, they have been elevated to monster-hunting imperial enforcers with jurisdiction over supernatural disasters. Two of them in the capital means two walking nuclear options.

The Yue Liang Men (月亮门), or Moon Gate Sect, is a classic jianghu intelligence network trope. Using "moons" as communication tokens is a neat callback to the secret-society practice of tally systems, where a physical token authenticates a message between trusted agents. Li Huowang retrieving the moon from his own flesh is a reminder of just how much of his body has been turned into a supernatural toolbox.

## Reading Guide

If you're feeling the pacing shift from wall-to-wall horror to political maneuvering, that's intentional—and it's done with surgical precision. The horror here isn't a monster; it's the cold realization that Ji Lin has been outgrowing his cage faster than Li Huowang could update his threat assessment. The best part is how our protagonist adapts: he doesn't panic, he doesn't rage—he recalculates. Li Huowang's choice to help the princess rather than kill her is a quiet, clever flex. He's learning that survival in the Dao-Twisted World isn't just about sharp swords and sharper talismans—it's about knowing when to *not* make a move. And that final scene with the Lü troupe? It's the chaos of home crashing into a war zone. Their arrival is comic relief with a knife hidden in the sleeve—old faces mean old debts, and in this world, debts have a way of getting paid in blood.
