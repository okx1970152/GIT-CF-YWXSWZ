## Chapter Overview

The long-awaited exorcism of Danyangzi is about to begin—but before a single prayer bead is spun, this chapter serves up a masterclass in grotesquery. Abbess Jingxin, a gigantically obese, toothless nun, grafts a pair of blood-red eyeballs into her own sockets like she’s popping in contacts. The entire Anci Nunnery staff has to demolish a wall just to get her outside. Six ancient, bloated abbesses are wheeled out on rickety wooden carts to face a writhing black statue made of billions of flies. The atmosphere is less “holy ritual” and more “eldritch nursing home field trip.” Li Huowang, bruised but bizarrely calm, musters his nerve while Bai Lingmiao quietly questions the sanity of trusting these people. It’s tense, ugly, and deeply unsettling—and that’s before the chanting starts.

## Key Plot Points

- **Jingxin’s New Eyes**: Abbess Jingxin places the blood-red eyeballs into her own empty sockets, restoring her sight and confirming her willingness to use forbidden, visceral methods. Her lack of eyelids on the new eyes creates immediate unease.
- **An Unlikely Alliance**: Li Huowang confirms the deal: Anci Nunnery will perform the ritual to expel Danyangzi. He suppresses his disgust and pragmatic fears, trusting that the deal will hold despite the nunnery’s alarming appearance.
- **The Six Abbesses Emerge**: Jingyuan and four other equally obese, aged nuns are wheeled out from deeper within the nunnery. Their physical decay mirrors the institutional corruption of rural Buddhism, but Li Huowang has no other option.
- **The Fly Buddha**: The ritual site is a low hollow containing a ten-meter-tall black Buddha statue that is not stone or wood—it’s a writhing mass of green bottle flies. The buzzing statue serves as both an altar and a horror set piece.
- **Li Huowang’s Self-Interrogation**: A moment of shared grief occurs between Li Huowang and Jingxin when they talk about her son, who is a withered, mindless human ingredient. Li Huowang reflects on the weight of choice: “To live muddled like that… is that really better?” He decides he must try, even if the odds are slim.
- **The Ritual Begins**: Jingxin tosses Li Huowang a string of prayer beads. He begins reciting a difficult sutra, marking the start of the exorcism.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **The Thousand Greats Record (《大千录》)**: This red bamboo slip is the foundational artifact of a suffering-cult. Its methods involve trading extreme agony to the entity *Bashe* for supernatural power. Li Huowang keeps his hand on it as a survival reflex—it is his only guarantee of emergency violence in a world where trust is a rare, fragile currency.
- **Heart-Element (心素)**: A rare human constitution that makes the bearer an object of desire for alchemists and cultivators. Jingxin’s warning—“You’re still a Heart-Element. That’s the big one.”—underscores that expelling Danyangzi solves only one of Li Huowang’s existential threats; his fundamental nature will continue to attract predators.
- **Rural Buddhist Corruption (Anci Nunnery)**: Unlike the polished, ceremonial evil of Zhengde Temple, Anci Nunnery represents a vulgar, decaying form of institutional religion. The abbesses are obese, filthy, gluttonous, and indifferent to spiritual duties. Their physical grotesqueries mirror the hollowing out of faith—what remains is only the shell of ritual, animated by base human instinct rather than enlightenment.
- **The Fly Buddha**: Buddhist iconography is deliberately perverted in the Dao-Twisted World. A Buddha statue made of flies is the ultimate inversion of sacred purity—flies feed on decay, death, and filth. The image suggests that what these nuns worship is not transcendence but rot. In Chinese folk religion, flies are also associated with corruption and the restless dead, making this “Buddha” a node of concentrated impurity.
- **Sutra Recitation as Action**: In Chinese Buddhist and Daoist ritual, chanting sutras is not passive prayer—it is a concrete, performative act that generates power, aligns karma, or compels spiritual forces. Li Huowang’s murmured chant marks the boundary between preparation and consequence.

## Reading Guide

First things first: yes, the scene where an elderly nun rams two raw eyeballs into her face happens on page one. Welcome to the Dao-Twisted World, where the body horror budget is unlimited and nobody flinches. What makes this chapter stand out is the *mundane dread* packed into every detail—the creaking cart wheels, the moldy rice cake, the casual mention of a son who is kept alive as a mindless vegetable because “as his mother, the best I can do is keep him alive.” That line hits harder than any jump scare.

This is a chapter about *trusting monsters*. Li Huowang knows Anci Nunnery is corrupt, disgusting, and possibly dangerous. But they’re the only option he has. The narrative forces him—and the reader—to sit with that discomfort. When Jingxin tells him to “stop stalling,” he stalls. When she throws him the beads, he catches them. The ritual is starting, but the real tension is whether any of these broken, bloated figures can actually do what they promised.

Also, keep an eye (ha) on the Fly Buddha. In this world, nothing is merely decorative.
