## Chapter Overview
Well, well, well—Li Huowang finally catches up to the smell. That nagging sense of "I've been here before" finally clicks, and guess who's written all over it? The Zuowandao. This chapter is a glorious, paranoid unraveling of an entire town's collective delusion, where our favourite paranoid protagonist uses cold logic, a sword, and a very large coffin to prove that the monster under Pi County's bed was never real. But here's the kicker: the monster was just a story spread by people who really, really wanted you to be afraid. Buckle up for a masterclass in folk-horror debunking, Daoist-style.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang interrogates the townsfolk and the magistrate, systematically dismantling their belief in the "ghost that steals the root." He forces them to admit none of them have actually witnessed the phenomenon firsthand—it's all hearsay.
- Piecing together the absurdity of the "cure" (silver locks, chili powder) and the conveniently timed death of the original source, Li Huowang has a flash of recognition: this is Zuowandao work.
- In a rage, he attacks Magistrate Yang Hongzhi, checking for a second face (a classic Zuowandao trick), but finds none. The townspeople are victims, not perpetrators.
- Following the magistrate's lead, Li Huowang has the coffin of the deceased "spirit-dancer" exhumed. Inside: no corpse, only a mahjong tile (six wan)—a signature taunt left behind by the tricksters.
- Realizing the Zuowandao have long since abandoned Pi County like a broken toy, Li Huowang calms down and shifts his focus. He gathers the townspeople to explain the truth: the "shrinking" was a mass delusion fueled by a deliberate rumor.
- He returns to the inn to reassure Gao Zhijian and privately reframes the entire incident as a strategic opportunity—solving this problem for the Supervisory Heavenly Office will force a contact.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Absurdity of "Shrinking" (缩阳/缩阴)** – This is a real, documented phenomenon in Chinese folk belief, often called *suoyang* (shrinking penis) or *suoyin* (shrinking vagina). It’s a culture-bound syndrome where individuals (usually men) believe their genitals are retracting into their body, often accompanied by a panic that death will follow if it fully disappears. In rural folklore, it was "treated" with folk remedies ranging from pulling the affected area (yes, really) to applying chili paste—meant to scare the ghost away. The novel brilliantly weaponizes this genuine folk fear, showing how easily a fabricated supernatural "rule" can grip an entire population when it taps into an existing anxiety. Li Huowang’s insight—that there’s no ghost at all, only a story repeated until it became "truth"—is a clinical-level deconstruction of how mass hysteria operates in a pre-modern setting.

**The Zuowandao's Signature Taunt** – The mahjong tile (六万, six wan) left in the empty coffin is not just random trash. In the context of the Zuowandao, everything they do carries a performance. A mahjong tile—a gaming piece—is a perfect calling card for a sect that treats reality as a game. "Six wan" (十 thousand) might subtly mock the weight of the case they left behind: six times ten thousand grains of dread, all created from nothing but a lie.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a huge breather for Li Huowang's sanity, and you can feel the tension leave his shoulders when he realizes there's no ghost. But don't let that calm fool you—this is also a brilliant piece of strategic repositioning. Li Huowang isn't just solving a crime; he's building a resume for the Supervisory Heavenly Office. Pay attention to how quickly he switches from "vengeance mode" to "public relations mode" once he realizes the real Zuowandao agent is long gone. That's a man who knows his priorities. Also, the little detail of Gao Zhijian trembling at the inn? Pure *Dao Gui Yi Xian*—even the 'safe' moments come with a reminder that his companions are traumatized by the same world he's learned to navigate. Great setup for the next move.
