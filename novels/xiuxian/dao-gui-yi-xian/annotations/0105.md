## Chapter Overview
Alright folks, strap in, because things take a deeply uncomfortable turn. This chapter is all about a morning-after reckoning—and not the fun kind. Li Huowang has put the pieces together: the warmth in his bed last night, the crimson veil, the wrong body, the wrong *feel*. It was Bai Lingmiao’s Second Spirit (二神). Now he has to confront the girl he cares about and ask the questions he really doesn't want the answers to. The atmosphere is thick with suppressed rage, creeping horror, and the kind of intimacy that feels more like a trap than a comfort. Get ready for a slow-burn interrogation that peels back another layer of the Dao-Twisted World’s most unsettling rule: the things that wear familiar faces are never just innocent.

## Key Plot Points
1. **The Morning After Investigation**: Li Huowang reviews the evidence from the night before—the bite mark on his finger, the extinguished candle, the unfamiliar shape in the dark—and concludes the “bride” in his bed was not human. Or at least, not Bai Lingmiao.
2. **Confronting Bai Lingmiao**: Li Huowang questions her about her Second Spirit. He learns the crucial truth: every Lead Spirit has a Second Spirit that is a perfect physical copy of the Lead Spirit. Li Zhi’s “wife” was actually his own Second Spirit, not a separate person.
3. **The Unspoken Secret**: Bai Lingmiao can’t explain how the Second Spirit first appeared—only that it “came in a dream” when she became a chuma. Li Huowang sees she’s genuinely unable to articulate it and stops pressing.
4. **The Veiled Face-off**: Li Huowang summons the Second Spirit and confirms, through clothing and silhouette, that she was indeed the figure in his bed. He forces himself to swallow his confrontation in front of Bai Lingmiao, unwilling to reveal what truly happened.
5. **The Departure**: The group leaves Wu Family Village and heads for Mount Henghua. Bai Lingmiao insists on accompanying Li Huowang this time, declaring she is no longer a burden now that she can use the spirit dance.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Second Spirit (二神, Er Shen)** – In the chuma (出马) tradition, the Lead Spirit (大神, Da Shen) is the primary medium, and the Second Spirit (二神, Er Shen) is the designated “vessel” for the possessing immortal. The Er Shen is typically a living human who has been spiritually bound to the Da Shen, enabling the immortal to inhabit a recognizable form. What the novel does with this concept—making the Er Shen a perfect physical duplicate of the Da Shen—is a horrifying twist. It means the face you trust is not a separate person but a *mirror*, a copy that shares your form but may harbor its own agenda. The “dream appearance” Bai Lingmiao describes is rooted in the folk belief that spiritual initiation often occurs during liminal states (sleep, sickness, near-death), when the boundaries between self and other grow porous.

**The Red Veil (红盖头)** – The red bridal veil is a traditional accessory in Chinese weddings, symbolizing modesty and warding off evil spirits. In this chapter, it transforms into a tool of misdirection and dread. Li Huowang confirms the Second Spirit’s identity by matching the veil to the one from the night before, reinforcing the motif that “red” in the Dao-Twisted World is never just celebration—it is a signal of wrongness, a ceremonial trap.

**Chuma Initiation** – Bai Lingmiao’s admission that the Second Spirit “came in a dream” aligns with real-world accounts of becoming a chuma disciple. The process often involves a mythic dream or a near-death experience in which an immortal “chooses” the medium. The key difference here is that the novel literalizes the bond—the Second Spirit is not merely a spiritual guide but a tangible, physical double that can act independently, often without the Lead Spirit’s full awareness.

## Reading Guide
*Buckle up, fellow traveler—this one’s a slow, tense burn, and the payoff is pure dread.*

Li Huowang’s internal voice in this chapter is a perfect showcase of his current state: he’s not crazed, he’s **calculating**—backtracking through memories, picking up details, and forcing himself to stay calm while his skin crawls. This is the cold paranoia of a man who knows he shared a bed with *something*, and that something is still walking around wearing his friend’s face. His decision to suppress his anger and not confront the Second Spirit in front of Bai Lingmiao is a sign of growth—he’s learning to compartmentalize, to prioritize the group’s stability over his personal rage.

Bai Lingmiao’s voice, by contrast, is *quietly desperate*. She wants to trust Li Huowang completely, but she can’t give him the explanation he needs. Her inability to describe how the Second Spirit appeared isn’t evasiveness—it’s a genuine limit of language. In the Dao-Twisted World, some bonds are simply outside the frame of rational explanation.

Pay close attention to the final line: “I’m not a burden anymore.” Bai Lingmiao’s insistence on going with him is not just loyalty—it’s a quiet assertion of agency. She is choosing to stand beside him, even knowing something is wrong. That act of trust is worth more than any ritual.
