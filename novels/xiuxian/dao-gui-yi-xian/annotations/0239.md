## Chapter Overview

Welcome back, fellow Daoists, to another journey into the beautifully gut-wrenching madness of the Dao-Twisted World! This chapter is a masterclass in the slow horror of implication. We find our skinless, mask-faced protagonist, Li Huowang, frozen in a terrifying standoff with the “Noose-Son,” a creature that’s not a monster in the classic sense, but a *system* of cruelty made flesh. Forced into a cat-and-mouse still-life, Li Huowang gets a brutal lesson in folk horror that isn’t about fangs and claws, but about the quiet, community-sanctioned savagery of "eating the heirless inheritance." Get ready for a chapter that sticks with you not for its action, but for the chilling conversation at its core.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang and his group are trapped in a dark space with the "Noose-Son," a massive entity of which a hanged female corpse is merely a finger or appendage.
- By holding perfectly still, Li Huowang deduces the creature's behavior: it attacks movement, not stationary objects, allowing them to survive the encounter.
- The Daoist, Han Fu, reappears and uses a burning talisman to break the tension after the Noose-Son leaves. He introduces himself as a member of the "Luo Sect."
- Han Fu saves the group, but his presence is immediately disconcerting due to his grotesque hygiene—a mouthful of rotten, foul-smelling teeth.
- Li Huowang cleverly lies about his background (claiming temporary departure from the Ao-Jing Sect) and implies he has a powerful master to deter any potential treachery.
- Han Fu casually explains the nature of the Noose-Son: a folk solution for disposing of the elderly. Neighbors deliver a useless old person to it on an auspicious day, claiming it will "take care of them," so they can inherit their property.
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang burning with quiet, helpless rage at this systemic cruelty, finally named by Gouwa as "eating the heirless inheritance."

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Noose-Son (绳儿子 / Shéng Ér Zi) & "Eating the Heirless Inheritance" (吃绝户 / Chī Jué Hù)**

This chapter strikes at the heart of Dao-Twisted World’s horror by weaponizing a very real, dark aspect of traditional Chinese rural society. "Eating the heirless inheritance" was a historical practice where, upon the death of a childless or single person, the entire community would descend to divvy up their property like vultures. It was a naked display of greed masked as a collective "right." The novel takes this brutal social reality and ritualizes it. The "Noose-Son" (the entity, not the mother corpse) becomes the *aliibi* for this cruelty. The community doesn't simply abandon or kill its elderly; they dress it up in ritual, choosing an auspicious day, carrying the person out in a sedan chair like a funeral procession, and "gifting" them to a monster. This act reframes murder as filial piety. Han Fu’s names here are deeply symbolic. The "Rope Son" implies the entity is a substitute for the child who should be caring for them, but its "care" is a hanging. The "female corpse" is the one who has already been delivered. It's a grim twist on the concept of "sending off the end" (送终 / sòng zhōng), which usually refers to the duty of children to be at their parents’ deathbed. Here, "sending off" becomes an active, violent disposal. The monster isn't the worst thing here; the neighbors are.

### Sending off the end (送终)

The traditional Chinese filial duty of children to be present at their parents' deathbed. The chapter's title and the Noose-Son ritual corrupt this concept into an act of violent disposal.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is an emotional gut-punch, and it’s meant to be. The prose is masterfully slow in the first half, forcing you to feel every second of Li Huowang’s paralyzed terror. Notice the emphasis on the *stillness*—the horror comes from the lack of action, the dread of being within arm's reach of a corpse that hasn't moved yet. This is the "safe zone" horror rule in full effect: a location that *looks* like a trap, a creature that doesn't attack, but a situation that is morally decaying on its own.

The second half is all about Han Fu. Look past the "foul-mouthed sorcerer" act. His casual tone while describing what amounts to community-sanctioned murder is the real terror. He’s not a villain doing a monologue; he’s just stating the obvious about how the world works. Pay attention to how Li Huowang processes this. He doesn't charge in to fight the system. He can’t. He’s trapped in that rage at the end, a feeling many readers will find disturbingly relatable. The "copper coin mask" is also a key piece here. Han Fu notices it, and Li Huowang is wearing it amidst all the skinlessness—a tiny detail that hints at the tangled web of identities and artifacts our protagonist is juggling.
