## Chapter Overview

Alright, fellow Daoists, buckle up. We're finally cashing in those membership points. After all the pain, the skin grafts, and the lies, Li Huowang has dragged himself back to the Heavenly Office to pull the biggest lever he has: telling them the ENTIRE Zuowandao crew has shown up in the capital. This isn't a puny squad of tricksters; we're talking the full mahjong set. One hundred and thirty-six cards, including the terrifying Four Joys and Three Yuans. Our boy is about to start a war between two colossal powers, and he’s hoping to crawl out of the wreckage with a prize. But the real highlight? The man he has to convince isn’t some cultivator with a glowing aura—he’s a mortal Confucian scholar who reads *people* like a book.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang returns to the Supervisory Heavenly Office and delivers his bombshell: the entire Zuowandao sect, led by their leader “Dice,” is gathering in the capital.
- He uses the logic of the mahjong set to explain their structure: 108 “common cards,” 16 “wind” tiles (the Four Joys), and 12 “dragon” tiles (the Three Yuans), plus their leader who uses the dice to play them all.
- Nan Gong is initially skeptical of the claim’s gravity, but the threat of national-level chaos forces him to escalate the report to the Supervisory Censor.
- While waiting, Li Huowang hears a cryptic voice from behind a screen warning that “everyone is going to flee to Shu,” hinting that the Office’s old hands already sense the coming storm.
- Li Huowang investigates the source of the voice but finds only a grotesque root-carving of a laughing monk—a visual metaphor for forced cheer over chaotic foundations.
- He is summoned to meet the Censor, and Nan Gong reveals the shocking truth: the man in charge is not a sorcerer, but a mortal Confucian who relies only on his understanding of human nature. This terrifies Li Huowang more than any monster would.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Mahjong Hierarchy of the Zuowandao** — This is a brilliant piece of cultural coding. The author uses the structure of a literal mahjong set to describe the sect’s hierarchy. There are 108 “numbered” tiles (the common soldiers), 16 Wind tiles (East, South, West, North known as the “Four Joys”), and 12 Dragon tiles (Red, Green, White known as the “Three Yuans”). The leader is the “Dice” (骰子) because you need two dice to play the game. This isn’t just flavor text; it implies the sect has a rigid, almost bureaucratic structure of deception. For Western readers, think of it like a crime syndicate organized by a deck of cards, where “Dice” is the player who controls the whole table.

**The Mortal Confucian vs. The Supernatural** — The idea that a mortal can command a bureau full of exorcists is a fascinating subversion of the usual xianxia power fantasy. Confucian philosophy traditionally values “respecting ghosts and spirits but keeping them at a distance” (敬鬼神而远之). A scholar’s power is derived from moral rectitude, social order, and, most importantly, the ability to deduce truth through observation and logic. In a world full of liars (like the Zuowandao), being unreadable by supernatural means makes this Censor the most dangerous predator in the room. It’s a classic “the pen is mightier than the sword” logic, but here the pen is used to dissect minds.

### Four Joys (四喜)

The highest-ranked inner circle of the Zuowandao, consisting of the four Wind tiles; their appearance signals an escalation in the enemy’s threat level.

### Three Yuans (三元)

The second tier of high-ranking Zuowandao members, corresponding to the three Dragon tiles (Red, Green, White) in a mahjong set.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in setup. It’s quiet, mostly dialogue and internal monologue, but the tension is immense. Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s new mindset. After surviving the Sieve and the doppelgänger, he isn't just reacting anymore; he’s *strategizing*. The horror has shifted from "I might die" to "I might be the chess player." Also, don’t sleep on that root-carved monk. The visual of a perfectly happy, orderly top half stitched onto a chaotic, mangled bottom half is a perfect symbol for the Dao-Twisted World itself—a sick joke masquerading as a joke. Finally, get ready to meet the Censor. In a novel filled with body horror and cosmic dread, the most unsettling figure might just be a normal man with abnormally sharp eyes.
