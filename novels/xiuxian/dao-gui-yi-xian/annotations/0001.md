## Chapter Overview

This opening chapter immediately plunges readers into the grotesque, claustrophobic world of the Dao-Twisted realm. Li Huowang is shown as an outsider among a community of physically deformed disciples, all grinding alchemical ingredients in a damp cave temple. His impulsive act of protecting a girl from assault sets him against the fat disciple, but more critically, against the Master himself—a hideous old Daoist who casually murders and grinds up a woman for elixir refinement. The chapter ends with a startling reality switch: the cave vanishes, replaced by a hospital room where Li Huowang is strapped down, suggesting his experiences may be delusions—or that both worlds are true.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang grinds luminous stones in a cave temple alongside other disciples who all suffer from visible deformities (albinism, cleft lip, polio, etc.).
- A cleft-lipped fat disciple attempts to sexually assault an albino girl, and Li Huowang smashes him with a stone mortar to stop it.
- The fat disciple threatens Li Huowang with the Master's punishment; Li Huowang defies him, calling the Master "less than nothing."
- A woman with a twisted mouth brings Li Huowang to see the Master, who is revealed as a scabby-headed, underbitten old Daoist in filthy robes.
- The Master immediately grabs the twisted-mouthed woman, throws her into a stone vat, and pulverizes her with a giant pestle while chanting ritual verses.
- Her remains are dumped into an alchemical furnace as two rouge-cheeked child acolytes add other ingredients, producing a cloying, strange fragrance.
- The Master confronts Li Huowang about his insolent remark. Li Huowang closes his eyes, reciting internally that everything is fake.
- Suddenly, Li Huowang is in a clean hospital room, his lower body bound to the bed—suggesting the cave temple may have been a hallucination, or that both worlds coexist.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The chapter introduces several key elements of the novel's sinister take on Daoist alchemy. The chanted verse—"Dingchou, extend my years…"—is a modified version of the "Liujia Shenming" mantra from Daoist liturgical tradition, but here it accompanies a literal murder. The use of a human being as an alchemical ingredient is a corruption of the Confucian/Daoist idea that rare materials can enhance longevity; in this world, the "ingredient" is alive and screaming. The setting—a natural cave hollowed into a temple with rotting peach-wood plaques naming halls (Hall of the Numinous Officials, etc.)—draws on the folk belief that peach wood repels evil, yet the cave itself reeks of death. The sudden transition to a hospital at the chapter's end is the novel's signature device: the reader is forced to question whether the "real" world is the hospital or the cave. This duality—psychological horror vs. supernatural horror—will persist throughout the story.

## Reading Guide

Pay close attention to the sensory details: the grinding of stones, the screaming, the cloying fragrance. The horror in this chapter is not just visual but visceral—you can smell the blood, feel the damp cold. Li Huowang's inner monologue reveals a man trying to maintain his sanity by repeating that it's all fake, yet his actions (protecting the girl, defying the Master) show he engages with the cave world as if it's real. The hospital ending is the first major twist; it's not a dream or a simple escape, because we don't know which world is the deception. This uncertainty will become the novel's central tension. Also note the social hierarchy: outer disciples in tattered hemp, inner disciples in Daoist robes, and the Master who rules through murder.
