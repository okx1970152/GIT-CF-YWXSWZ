## Chapter Overview
What do you do when the world has taught you that you are weak, untrained, and completely outclassed? You find a cheat. Not the kind that breaks the rules of reality—no, this is the *Dao-Twisted World*, where cheats usually come with a side of flesh-horror. Chun Xiao Man gets thoroughly humiliated in a sparring match against a nameless youth with a strange rattan shield, and instead of moping, she does something far more dangerous: she gets an idea. A very clever, very folk-horror idea. And she drags Bai Lingmiao into it.

## Key Plot Points
- Chun Xiao Man challenges a young martial artist from the escort agency and loses decisively multiple times. Wang Chengxing (the agency’s manager) gently tries to send her home, thinking she’s a rich girl playing at being a heroine.
- Xiao Man refuses to give up. She pragmatically asks for advice on how to truly improve, revealing that her goal isn’t glory—it’s survival.
- Wang Chengxing diagnoses her weaknesses: lack of strength, unstable horse stance, and the need for consistent sparring and a proper teacher.
- An old woman suffering from “demonic possession” suddenly attacks Yang Xiaohai. Wang Chengxing subdues her and explains that despite hiring monks and nuns for rituals, his shifu’s wife has never recovered. This tragic detail plants a seed in Xiao Man’s mind.
- Xiao Man rushes back to the inn and finds Bai Lingmiao. She asks directly if Bai Lingmiao has truly become a spirit-dancer (tiao dashen), leading to a potential supernatural solution.
- Xiao Man drags a hesitant Bai Lingmiao to the escort agency at night and proposes that her friend can cure the possessed woman. The agency head, Zhao Qin, is weary and politely refuses, returning Xiao Man’s silver. He just wants his wife to have peace.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Demonic Possession (中邪, zhōng xié):** This is a major folk-horror concept in Chinese culture, distinct from the Western idea of demonic possession. “中邪” literally means “struck by evil” or “suffering from evil influence.” It often refers to encounters with angry ghosts, malevolent spirits, or other forms of *xie* (邪)—a pervasive form of unclean, twisted energy. In rural folk religion, possession is often treated less like a moral battle and more like a sickness or misfortune caused by a spirit that has latched onto a person. The traditional remedies involve exorcism rituals performed by Daoist priests, Buddhist monks, or folk mediums (like *tiao dashen* practitioners). The fact that every ritual has failed for the old woman is a strong signal that this isn’t a normal case—or that the corruption in this world runs deeper than any sect can fix.

**Spirit-Dancing (跳大神, tiao dashen):** In the previous chapters, we established the basics of *chuma* mediumship. Here, Xiao Man is essentially trying to turn Bai Lingmiao into a supernatural fixer—an exorcist who doesn’t use force but negotiation and spirit-knowledge. In real-world folk religion, spirit-dancers were often called in as a last resort when Buddhist and Daoist rites failed, because they dealt directly with the spirit world's “politics.” Bai Lingmiao’s reluctance is wise; each time she enters that state, the cost might be more than just weariness.

**Martial Arts Realism in the Dao-Twisted World:** This chapter does something refreshing: it shows that simply practicing moves in isolation isn’t enough. Xiao Man’s forms are “solid,” but she lacks a foundation (horse stance), physical strength, and the practical knowledge that comes from real sparring against different weapons (like the strange rattan shield). This isn’t a videogame where grinding experience points guarantees victory. Even in this horror-filled world, the basics of martial training still apply—raw talent alone won’t save you.

## Reading Guide
This chapter marks Chun Xiao Man’s transition from a frustrated learner to a clever strategist. She doesn’t get stronger in this chapter—she gets smarter. Her willingness to use Bai Lingmiao’s supernatural ability as a bargaining chip to gain access to a proper master is a survival instinct, not a heroic leap. Watch how her pragmatism contrasts with Bai Lingmiao’s visible hesitation. The chapter also sets up a classic folk-horror dilemma: a possession that *cannot* be cured by standard religious methods. In a normal novel, this would be a quest objective. In *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, it is probably a trigger for something much worse. Keep an eye on Zhao Qin’s wan expression; the man isn’t just tired—he’s given up. And in this world, giving up is sometimes the only smart move.
