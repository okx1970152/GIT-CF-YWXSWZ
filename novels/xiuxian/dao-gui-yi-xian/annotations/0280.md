## Chapter Overview
Li Huowang bids an uneasy farewell to Pi County and continues his journey with the Lü family opera troupe. On the surface, it's a quiet transitional chapter—travel permits are obtained, a new opera earns good money, and Lü Xiucai finally masters his first technique. But beneath this calm, Li Huowang is wrestling with a much deeper anxiety: his only shield against being identified as a Heart-Element is a copper coin mask, and he knows it's not enough. The chapter ends with a bitter taste as Lü Xiucai's newly acquired power goes straight to his head, revealing a personality that power will likely corrupt long before it redeems.

## Key Plot Points
- Magistrate Yang Hongzhi sends Li Huowang off with exaggerated courtesy but not a single copper of travel money, confirming his desire to be rid of the "plague god" as quickly as possible.
- Li Huowang secures official travel permits, giving the group a temporary identity as half-citizens of Great Liang—a small but critical logistical victory.
- The Lü family troupe's new opera style is earning real money, and Li Huowang, as their patron, takes half the profits. The troupe is now a reliable income stream.
- Li Huowang realizes his conversation with the Debt-Credit Peddler may have only been the first step; he has no certainty whether the Supervisory Heavenly Office will accept him or whether the peddler was a Zuowandao in disguise.
- Lü Xiucai successfully masters a Luo Sect technique through pure rote memorization, but his arrogance immediately surfaces. He refuses to help his father's troupe, considering himself above the "lowly classes."
- Li Huowang confirms that the technique carries no Nascent Soul trap, meaning he can safely practice it himself—but he also realizes the technique is useless without the bronze coin sword.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Travel Permits (路引, Lùyǐn)** In imperial China, travel permits were official documents required to move between cities, especially larger ones. Without one, you could be arrested as a vagrant or bandit. This bureaucratic detail adds a layer of gritty realism to the Dao-Twisted World—magic and monsters aside, you still need paperwork. Li Huowang getting a permit isn't just a convenience; it's a step toward formal existence in this world.

**The "Lowly Classes" (下九流, Xià Jiǔ Liú)** Lü Xiucai's contempt for his father's opera troupe is rooted in traditional Chinese social hierarchy. The "Nine Lowly Classes" included performers, prostitutes, servants, and other professions considered beneath scholars and officials. Opera actors were often treated as social outcasts, which explains Lü Xiucai's desperation to escape that label once he gains any kind of power. His disdain isn't just personal arrogance—it's a reflection of a deeply rigid social system.

**Luo Sect (罗教)** The Luo Sect was a real Chinese folk religious sect founded in the Ming Dynasty, blending elements of Chan Buddhism, Daoism, and folk belief. Its scriptures were often written in simple vernacular to reach illiterate followers—a fact that makes Lü Xiucai's rote memorization without literacy both plausible and darkly ironic. In the Dao-Twisted World, the Luo Sect's techniques are a dangerous shortcut for the desperate.

**Bronze Coin Sword (铜钱剑)** A ritual Daoist weapon made of 108 coins strung together on red thread. It's used for exorcism and spiritual combat, not martial warfare. In this chapter, Li Huowang notes that Lü Xiucai's "divine power" is entirely dependent on having this specific sword—without it, the technique is just empty motions. This is classic xianxia "hidden cost" logic: the flashy power comes with an equipment requirement that may not be easy to fulfill.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in the "quiet before the storm" pacing that makes *Dao Gui Yi Xian* so effective. Nothing explodes, no one dies, and yet the tension is palpable. Li Huowang's paranoia about his uncovered identity is the real engine here—every smile from Magistrate Yang, every successful test of Lü Xiucai's technique, is shadowed by the gnawing thought: *What if the Debt-Credit Peddler was Zuowandao?*

Pay close attention to the contrast between the troupe's growing prosperity and Lü Xiucai's growing arrogance. The old man is delighted with the money; the son already considers himself above the family. This is a classic "power before wisdom" arc in the making, and veteran xianxia readers will recognize the warning signs. Lü Xiucai isn't ready for real power, but he's about to get it anyway.

Also note Li Huowang's cold, methodical evaluation of the Luo Sect technique. He's not celebrating a new power—he's checking it for traps, like a bomb disposal expert. That's the signature Li Huowang we know: paranoid, pragmatic, and always thinking two steps ahead of the next disaster.
