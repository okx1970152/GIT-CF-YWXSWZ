## Chapter Overview
Hold onto your bucket of fingernails, fellow Daoists, because this chapter is a raw, screaming deep dive into Li Huowang’s shattered psyche. We get a front-row seat to his public meltdown—a horrific, violent, and painfully honest confrontation with his own hallucinations and the boundaries of reality. He screams, he begs, he buries his face in the dirt, all while his friends and a crowd of Qingqiu onlookers watch in a mix of horror and pity. This is the chapter where Li Huowang, out of sheer desperation, invents his own twisted form of *grounding therapy*, proving once again that this is *not* a power fantasy about cool, mentally stable cultivators. It’s a gritty portrayal of survival when your own mind is the deadliest battlefield.

## Key Plot Points
Li Huowang suffers a massive psychotic break, triggered by a hallucination of his mother and a therapist’s voice mocking him. He argues with thin air, screaming that he can’t afford to treat both “worlds” as real because the consequences are fatal and grotesque (chopsticks in eyes, iron nails in the mouth). The crisis is so severe that Bai Lingmiao has to take control, ordering the group away and comforting him alone. After an hour of convulsive rolling, Li Huowang emerges hollow and exhausted. He asks Bai Lingmiao to be *worse* to him so he can believe she’s real. To ground himself, he cuts off his own pinky finger, savoring the pain as proof of reality, and feeds it to his dog, Mantou. As the group prepares to leave Qingqiu, Sun Baolu reveals his illiteracy, solidifying his departure. On their way out, Mantou drops a familiar, horrifying object at Li Huowang’s feet: a piece of a *Black Tai Sui*, throwing the chapter’s emotional closure into total, chaotic disarray.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter’s horror is rooted in a deeply human psychology, but it also touches on classic Chinese tropes. Li Huowang’s act of self-harm—cutting off his own finger—isn’t a flashy martial arts move; it’s a desperate, earthly attempt at grounding himself using physical pain. This harks back to historical practices where monks or ascetics would use pain to transcend worldly illusions, though here it’s inverted into a survival tactic. Furthermore, the “Black Tai Sui” is a terrifying corruption of a real Chinese folk concept. *Tai Sui* (太岁) is often considered the “God of the Year” or a spirit of the soil, but in this novel, it’s been grotesquely inverted into a parasitic, wriggling flesh-entity. Its reappearance here isn’t a random callback; it’s a reminder that the true horror of Li Huowang’s world doesn’t care about his emotional breakdowns. It has its own agenda, and a piece of it has just literally followed him home.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is all about tonal whiplash and Li Huowang’s *character progression* in the worst possible way. He’s not getting stronger swords or cooler spells; he’s learning to use his own madness as a tool. Pay close attention to his logic about “treating both sides as real.” He rejects the therapist’s advice because he can’t afford to—for him, treating his modern-world hallucinations as real would *erase* the urgency of his friends’ survival in the Dao-Twisted World. It’s a bleak, cynical take on mental health that fits the novel’s grim, survival-horror logic. And just when you think the chapter has given him a moment of calm, the *Black Tai Sui* shows up. That’s the core of *Dao Gui Yi Xian*: every moment of peace is a trap, every victory is temporary, and the world will always, *always* pile on one more horror.
