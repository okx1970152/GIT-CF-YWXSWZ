## Chapter Overview

Strange days indeed when a pigsty sermon on the etymology of “home” cuts deeper than any ghost story. In this chapter, Li Huowang’s group is finally trundling toward the border, and Jin Shanzhao—that odd old man with the bamboo basket—spills the beans on his true identity. But it’s not a revelation that comes with thunder and lightning. It arrives over a muddy character etched in dirt, a bitter memory of a dead nun, and a row of black pottery jars that make Li Huowang reach for his sword. The chapter is a masterclass in quiet dread: the horror of recognizing that the world’s cruelty is normalized, the loneliness of knowing your one safe place is ash, and the unsettling suspicion that even a friendly map-reader might be playing a much longer game.

## Key Plot Points

- The group passes through a village to resupply; Li Huowang and Jin Shanzhao go in alone to avoid drawing attention.
- Li Huowang sees pigs being fed on filth, triggering a visceral memory of Abbess Jingxin’s corpse in a pigsty at the Anci Nunnery.
- Jin Shanzhao explains that feeding pigs this way is universal tradition, then illustrates the character *家* (home) in the dirt to prove his point.
- Li Huowang is visibly shaken by the lesson; he buries the character with soil, a quiet act of farewell to the only home he had in this mad world.
- Challenged, Jin Shanzhao reveals he is a disciple of the School of Vertical and Horizontal Alliance (Zongheng)—a strategic advisor tradition from the Warring States period.
- Li Huowang dismisses his grand claims with dry mockery; Jin Shanzhao admits he is currently “unemployed” as a diplomat.
- The chapter ends on an ominous note: Li Huowang spots black pottery jars under the eaves, each bearing a red inverted *福* (fortune) character—a folk symbol that here signals danger, not luck.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Character *家* and Its Dark Irony** – Jin Shanzhao’s lecture on the etymology of *家* is rooted in real Chinese character structure: 宀 (roof) + 豕 (pig). The conventional reading is that a home is a place where people and livestock coexist. But in the Dao-Twisted World, the pigsty under the house isn’t quaint—it’s where the dead Abbess Jingxin was left to rot. Li Huowang’s realization that “home” could be built on filth and normalized cruelty is a gut-punch. The folklore hook is that ancient Chinese characters do carry sedimented meanings: *家* literally “pig under roof,” but centuries of use have worn that crude image smooth. The novel restores its grit.

**School of Vertical and Horizontal Alliance (纵横家)** – This is a real school of thought from the Warring States period (475–221 BCE). Its practitioners, like Su Qin and Zhang Yi, were master strategists who traveled between kingdoms, forging alliances (vertical: weak states uniting against a hegemon) or breaking them (horizontal: serving one strong state to crush others). They were less soldiers than walking political calculators—think of them as “geopolitical ninjas with silver tongues.” Jin Shanzhao’s claim is not impossible in this setting; the Dao-Twisted World has many small kingdoms (Later Shu, Liang, Qingqiu, etc.), so a wandering Zongheng disciple could theoretically thrive. But his current “unemployed” status and his earlier terror of convict-soldiers make his boasts feel more like a tattered resume than a threat.

**Inverted *福* Character** – In Chinese folk tradition, pasting the character *福* (fortune/blessing) upside down on doors or posts is a common Lunar New Year practice; the wordplay is that “upside-down” (倒, dào) sounds like “arrive” (到, dào), so “fortune arrives.” However, in the context of a Xia Dynasty village (the Dao-Twisted World) and combined with black pottery jars (often used for storing corpses or ritual offerings), the inverted *福* takes on a sinister tone. It’s no longer a blessing—it’s a sign that something *else* has arrived.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a slow-burn breather that uses everyday objects—dirt, a pigsty, a clay jar—to dig into Li Huowang’s psyche. The core tension isn’t a monster or a battle; it’s the erosion of the protagonist’s emotional reserves. The *家* monologue is devastating because it reframes his memories of the nunnery: it wasn’t just a refuge, it was the only place where the word “home” felt real. Now that can’t come back. Pay attention to how Jin Shanzhao reveals his identity—it’s not a dramatic drop, it’s a trade for trust. And the final image of those black jars with inverted *福* is a classic Dao-Twisted World trick: a wholesome folk symbol turned into a threat signal. If you’ve been following the pattern of “happy auspicious things hiding horror,” you know something’s about to crawl out of those jars.

## Unsolved Puzzle: Who Put Those Jars There?

Li Huowang doesn’t act on the sighting—yet. But this village was just a random stop for supplies. Either the jars are a local custom that’s completely innocent (and our protagonist’s paranoia is spiking), or the village itself is a trap laid by pursuers who are already ahead of them. The red *福* is the same color as Zuowandao banners. Make of that what you will.
