## Chapter Overview
Oh boy, strap in, fellow Daoists. This chapter is a heavy one. After all the action and scheming, we finally get a look behind the curtain of the Great Liang Empire, and the truth is… not pretty. It’s a deep dive into the twisted, bloody logic of imperial power in the Dao-Twisted World. Right before his coronation, the new emperor, Ji Lin, confronts his walking-corpse father in a mausoleum and gets a history lesson that shatters everything he thought he knew about his family, his throne, and the very nature of reality. It’s a masterclass in tragic, systemic horror—no monsters, just the monstrous cost of a crown.

## Key Plot Points
- Frustrated by Princess Anping’s escape, Ji Lin orders a city-wide manhunt, growing increasingly paranoid.
- He learns the Ao-Jing Sect envoy, Er-Jiu, is missing, confirming his suspicion that he has been betrayed.
- Seeking answers, Ji Lin visits the mausoleum and opens his father’s dragon-pulled coffin one last time.
- A brutal argument ensues: Ji Lin demands to know why the emperor made his sons kill each other.
- The old emperor reveals the dynasty’s founding secret: the Ji family earned the Mandate of Heaven by being willing to commit mass kin-slaughter when no other house would. This is the source of their legitimacy.
- The emperor then delivers a grim revelation about ancient history: humans were once livestock for the “Wu,” and the Mandate is what holds their sheltered world together. The emperor is the sole being strong enough to carry it.
- Stunned and hollow, Ji Lin is left to wrestle with the horrifying implication that his own children will face the same bloody fate.
- The chapter ends on a note of pure vengeance: Ji Lin, upon hearing that the Ao-Jing Sect envoy has taken Princess Anping, orders his entire clan executed by slow-slicing.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Bloody Mandate of Heaven:** In traditional Chinese political philosophy, the “Mandate of Heaven” is a divine right to rule, justified by virtue. A dynasty loses the Mandate and is overthrown when it becomes corrupt. This chapter takes that concept and *perverts* it. Here, the mandate isn't granted by heaven for virtue; it is *seized* through an act of horrifying, sacrificial violence. The Ji family didn't win the throne by being the most righteous; they won it by being the most willing to butcher their own blood. This aligns perfectly with the novel’s theme: every source of power is rooted in a grotesque transaction.

**The True Dragon Son of Heaven:** The emperor is often called the “True Dragon.” This chapter literalizes the metaphor. The dragon’s roar from the coffin isn’t just for show; it visibly manifests as scales on Ji Lin’s body. It suggests the imperial power itself is a parasitic or symbiotic entity, a beast that lives *in* the bloodline. This explains why only the Ji family can bear the throne—they have literally been marked by the dragon, bound by the same horrific contract their founding ancestor made.

**Ancient History: Humans as Livestock:** The old emperor’s claim that ancient humans were livestock for the “Wu” taps into deep mythological roots. In classic texts like the *Shanhaijing* (Classic of Mountains and Seas), many tribes and gods were described with animal features, blurring the line between human, beast, and deity. Here, it’s used as a cosmic horror origin story, where civilization itself was a desperate escape from being food. It puts the entire “Great Nuo” and “Dao-Twisted World” in a new, terrifying context.

**The Ancestral Temple and Self-Slaughter:** The scene takes place in the Imperial Ancestral Temple, but not as a place of worship. It is a tomb. The emperor keeps his own father chained in a coffin, a living corpse. This is a brutal inversion of Confucian filial piety, a core tenet of Chinese society. Instead of honoring his ancestor, Ji Lin is confronting and despising him. The system is so corrupt that it has cannibalized the very moral code that once held it together.

## Reading Guide
Get ready for a major lore dump that recontextualizes *everything*. This chapter isn't about action; it's about the price of power. Watch how Ji Lin’s rage crumbles into hollow, existential dread. His father’s story about humans being livestock isn’t just a history lesson—it’s the thesis statement for the entire Dao-Twisted World. Also, take note of the emperor’s dismissal of “Siming” and “Fusheng Heaven” as lies for the masses. This is a direct challenge to the high-grade cosmic horror we’ve seen before. Which “truth” is real? The bloody, earthy one of the Ji family and their dragon-bound mandate, or the celestial one of the gods? Or are they both just different masks for the same terror? The chapter’s final twist—the betrayal by the Ao-Jing envoy—sets the stage for a very personal, vengeful conflict to come.
