## Chapter Overview

Holy hell. This is it. The chapter *every* reader has been waiting for—the one where all the tangled threads of the past arc snap at once. Danyangzi breaks free, the Xi Shen descend en masse, the Zuowandao realize they’ve been played, and Li Huowang—our exhausted, organ-drained, barely-human Li Huowang—pulls off the most terrifying gambit of his life. The chapter title *Done* (成了) is a masterclass in irony, because nothing about this ending feels clean or triumphant. It feels like watching a man burn his last shred of humanity for a victory that might not even be his. Buckle up, Daoists—this one hurts.

## Key Plot Points

- Danyangzi escapes his binding and immediately attacks the Zuowandao, taking advantage of the chaos Li Huowang deliberately created.
- Li Huowang openly admits to the Zuowandao that he *let* Danyangzi loose on purpose, laughing as he savors the taste of trickery.
- Three Xi Shen (Joy Spirits) emerge from the cavern walls, killing nearby Ao-Jing Sect mortals by forcing them into mad, fatal laughter.
- The Xi Shen engulf Li Huowang, draining most of his emotional capacity—including his despair and will to die—leaving him hollow.
- A clicking sound signals the Xi Shen’s next attack is imminent; Li Huowang accepts death since the Intercalating the Five Phases ritual is failing and his organs are gone.
- Danyangzi unexpectedly shields Li Huowang from the Xi Shen, but Li Huowang rejects the rescue as pointless.
- Spotting a lone surviving Zuowandao fleeing the massacre, Li Huowang’s death-wish is instantly replaced by a burning need to finish the job.
- Trapped in a dead end full of Ao-Jing Sect women and children, Li Huowang, running out of time and body, massacres the entire group to flush out the shapeshifted Zuowandao.
- The hallucination-monk breaks down, accusing Li Huowang of becoming something the *real* Li Huowang could never be.
- In his final moment of clarity, Li Huowang realizes the monk is right—*Li Huowang* wouldn’t do this. But Danyangzi would. And that means: he’s become Danyangzi. He’s *made it*.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Xi Shen (喜神)** – In this novel, the Joy Spirits are the Dao-Twisted World’s corruption of a positive folk deity. Traditional Chinese folk belief has many “Joy Gods” associated with happiness and celebration, but here they are entities that feed on human emotion and kill through ecstatic, forced laughter—blood from the seven apertures (七窍流血) is a classic death-sign in wuxia/xianxia, but weaponized through *joy* is uniquely horrifying.

**Intercalating the Five Phases (闰置五行)** – From the Thousand Greats Record (*Da Qian Lu*), a ritual that temporarily replaces the practitioner’s organs with writhing parasitic worms. It’s a body-horror twist on the Five Phases (Wuxing) theory of Chinese cosmology, where wood, fire, earth, metal, and water govern bodily systems. Here, the “intercalation” isn’t a calendar correction—it’s an overwrite of the self, and when the worms die, so does the flesh they held together.

**Becoming Danyangzi (成了)** – The term *成了* in Chinese web novel culture usually signals a successful breakthrough or completed cultivation stage—a moment of triumph. The *monstrous irony* here is that Li Huowang’s “success” is the final proof that his identity has been overwritten by the parasite. It’s a perversion of the genre’s own vocabulary of victory, and it lands like a knife.

## Reading Guide

This is one of those chapters where you have to sit with it afterward. The massacre of innocents is not played for shock value—it’s a *diagnosis*. Li Huowang’s final laugh isn’t joy; it’s the sound of a mind finally breaking into the shape of its occupier. The hallucination-monk’s line *“We’re through!”* is the novel’s way of telling us that the last witness to the old Li Huowang has walked away. And yet—the chapter makes you ask: did he *choose* to become Danyangzi, or did Danyangzi merely furnish him with a justification after the fact? The ambiguity is the horror. Pay attention to how the Xi Shen’s emotional drain leaves him without enough feeling to care about being saved—only enough to *want* to finish the revenge. That’s the knife-twist: even his hatred is no longer entirely his own.

## On the Horror of “Victory”

The title *Done* (成了) is usually a triumphant exclamation in cultivation stories: *I’ve broken through; I’ve become something greater.* Here, it means: *I’ve become the monster.* The chapter forces us to sit with the cognitive dissonance of a protagonist cheering his own spiritual overwriting. Li Huowang doesn’t grieve the massacre; he uses it as evidence. His logic is airtight and inhuman: “Li Huowang wouldn’t do this. Therefore, I am not Li Huowang. Therefore, I have won.” This is the novel weaponizing xianxia progression language against the reader’s own expectations. The character who was once desperate to hold onto his name has now happily surrendered it—and calls it success.

## The Zuowandao Survivor Hunt

The last Zuowandao’s escape attempt is a gritty, degrading chase scene. The tile-head cultivator, wounded and losing blood, blends into a crowd of civilians. Li Huowang, his arm literally falling apart as red bamboo slips scatter on the ground, doesn’t stop to strategize. He *cannot* afford to search because his body is dissolving, so he does the only thing the ruthless math allows: kill everyone in the room. The moral weight of this decision is not debated—it’s committed, and then rationalized afterward. The monk’s horror is our horror, but the novel gives us no time to grieve before the twist lands.
