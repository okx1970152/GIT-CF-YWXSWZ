## Chapter Overview

After a long day of travel, Li Huowang’s band makes camp, and he finally gets to put his investigative drive to work. Over a simple meal of noodles and wild greens, he probes Chun Xiaoman for broken fragments of local folklore about spirit-dancers—the *tiao dashen*. Unlike the ossified, monstrously corrupt institutions like Zhengde Temple, these wandering folk mediums seem to operate alone, in much the same sphere as Daoists. But just as Li Huowang is weighing whether they’d be strong enough to cleanse Danyangzi from him, a very different kind of night visitor arrives: an old man with a drum and a veiled woman in blood-red embroidered shoes, claiming to be a Lead Spirit and his “shy” wife. The chapter closes on a deceptively cheerful note, as the old man belts out a summoning cry that pierces the darkness. The tension is quiet tonight, but it’s all setup for a frightening collision.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang actively interrogates Chun Xiaoman for local knowledge about spirit-dancers (跳大神), learning they are itinerant exorcists who handle possession and curse work, similar to Daoist practitioners in lore. Her information is vague but confirms the existence of a “Five Spirits” cosmology (Red, Yellow, White, Willow, Sorrow).
- During the evening meal, an intimate moment occurs: Bai Lingmiao gives Li Huowang her duck egg yolk, a quiet act of care. Li Huowang responds by formally teaching her how to use Danyangzi’s Daoist bell as a last-resort protective measure, reciting the incantation step-by-step while she listens.
- A brief dissociative episode strikes Li Huowang as Bai Lingmiao walks away: her silhouette melts into Yang Na’s red high school uniform before he shakes himself back, reinforcing the porous boundary between the two worlds.
- Without warning, a figure approaches the campfire. Li Huowang hurls a burning brand and discovers a fifty-year-old man with grey hair wearing patched clothes, carrying a soiled drum covered in colorful ribbons. A veiled woman in vivid red embroidered shoes stands motionless beside him.
- The old man cheerfully identifies himself and his silent wife as a spirit-dancer duo: he is the *Great Spirit* (大神/Lead Spirit), and she is the *Second Spirit* (二神). Chun Xiaoman confirms this identity to a wary Li Huowang just before the man roars out a summoning cry into the night.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

Jumping straight into the folklore: **Tiao Dashen** (跳大神), literally “Leaping the Great Spirit,” is a real northern Chinese shamanic/mediumistic practice. You’ve got the *Lead Spirit* (大神), who handles the ritual and negotiations with spirits, and the *Second Spirit* (二神), who often acts as the possessed vessel or the drummer who guides the trance. In modern ethnographic contexts, the Second Spirit is sometimes a separate assistant who keeps the Lead Spirit from losing their mind during possession. In this chapter, the “wife” in embroidered shoes is unnervingly silent—she might be a possessed medium, a walking corpse, or something else entirely.

The **Five Spirits** (红黄白柳悲 or 红黄白柳灰 elsewhere)—Red, Yellow, White, Willow, and Sorrow/Grey—are a folk classification of animal spirits (foxes, weasels, snakes, etc.) that frequently appear in Chinese spirit-medium cults. “Sorrow” (悲) is an unusual addition; it might indicate a regional or corrupted variant, which fits perfectly with the Dao-Twisted World’s tendency to twist folk religion into something far more menacing.

On the philosophical side, Li Huowang’s comparison is sharp: spirit-dancers fill the same niche as Daoist exorcists, but where Danyangzi relied on insane alchemy and Zhengde Temple on institutional corruption, these wandering practitioners seem to operate on a simpler, more direct relationship with the supernatural. Whether that makes them *safer* or *more dangerous* is the question hanging over the chapter.

## Reading Guide

*Alright, friends, we’ve got ourselves a proper cryptid sighting tonight.* After weeks of temples, flesh Buddhas, and psychological torture, *Dao Gui Yi Xian* throws us a wild card that feels like a breath of fresh night air—if that air smells heavily of grave dust and burned offerings. This old man with his veiled, silent “wife” is a classic folk horror setup. Notice how the narrative clues function: the drum with colored ribbons, the silent woman, the bridal veil, the too-red shoes. Nothing is explicitly labeled *danger*, but everything *feels* like a trap door.

Pay attention to the small character beats. Li Huowang teaching Bai Lingmiao how to use the bell isn’t just a power-transfer scene; it’s him acknowledging that he may not always be there to protect her. His brief hallucination of Yang Na’s back superimposed on Bai Lingmiao is a sharp reminder that his reality fragmentation is ongoing, even during downtime. And then there’s the dandelion greens—one of those weirdly human details that grounds the horror. The old monk from Zhengde Temple taught him they were edible. In a world full of monsters, that tiny piece of practical knowledge feels oddly precious.

*Get ready: the drums are about to start, and they aren’t just for show.*
