## Chapter Overview

Home is supposed to be the end of the journey. For Bai Lingmiao, after countless miles of hardship and danger, reaching Oxheart Village should have meant reunion and relief. Instead, she finds a ghost town: every house intact, every pig starving in its pen, clothes still on the line—but every single person gone without a trace. The chapter is a masterclass in grief without a monster, where the heaviest horror is not a clawed beast but a silent, empty home. Li Huowang, who has learned to read the world’s dread through practical observation, methodically searches for answers while trying to hold together a woman whose entire world has just been erased.

## Key Plot Points

- Bai Lingmiao arrives at her home village of Oxheart only to find it completely deserted; everyone is gone, with no signs of struggle or flight.
- Lü Zhuangyuan confirms the eerie normalcy: doors unlocked, livestock starving, clothes still hanging—this was not an organized departure.
- Li Huowang conducts a grim investigation, finding no hostile spirits, no tracks, not even a clue left behind.
- Bai Lingmiao breaks down in grief; the group tries to console her, but the weight of the loss is too heavy for hollow words.
- That night, Bai Lingmiao performs a desperate spirit-summoning ritual with her drum, seeking answers from the immortals she serves.
- The next morning, the immortals have nothing—they cannot find her family either, a chilling confirmation that this disappearance is beyond even supernatural detection.
- Li Huowang reveals his true intentions: he was not indifferent. He has already decided to find the villagers himself, a quiet declaration of commitment that cuts through Bai Lingmiao’s despair.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Spirit-Summoning Verse (Bang Bing Jue)**
When Bai Lingmiao grabs her drum and chants into the night, she is performing a ritual rooted in the northeastern Chinese chuma (出马) medium tradition. The Bang Bing Jue (帮兵决) is a rapid, rhythmic summoning verse used by “lead spirits” to invite their spirit-generals and immortal patrons to descend and assist. The lyrics mix military imagery (stopping a horse’s head, pulling the reins), clan surnames (Zhang, Wang, Li), and legendary heroes like Li Cunxiao—all designed to call forth specific beings by name and lineage. In the Dao-Twisted World, this is not a performance; it is a desperate, genuine appeal to forces that may or may not answer. Their silence here is more terrifying than any scream.

**The Abandoned Village Trope in Chinese Folk Horror**
The “empty village where nothing is disturbed” is a classic setup in Chinese ghost stories and folklore. Unlike Western haunted houses, the horror often lies in the *absence* of violence—no blood, no destruction, just people erased as if they never existed. This taps into deep fears of communal collapse, banditry, or worse: forces that can take a life without leaving a mark. Oxheart Village’s state—pigs starving, clothes on the line—is the exact texture of that folk-horror dread.

### Bang Bing Jue (帮兵决)

A traditional northeastern Chinese spirit-summoning verse, chanted rapidly by a chuma medium to call spirit-generals or immortals for aid.

### Spirit-summoning drum (皮骨)

A ritual drum and mallet used by chuma mediums to accompany the Bang Bing Jue and invite spirits during possession ceremonies.

This chapter is a gut-punch for anyone who’s been following Bai Lingmiao’s journey. Her hope was the fuel that kept her going, and now it’s been replaced by an unbearable absence. He just investigates, plans, and then tells her he’s *already* going to fix it. That’s his love language: cold logic deployed for hot emotional problems. So grab a tissue, pay attention to the drums, and remember—when the immortals go silent, that’s when the real work begins.
