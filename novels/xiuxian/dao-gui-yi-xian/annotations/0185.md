## Chapter Overview

Ah, the sweet, tantalizing promise of a warm meal and a village wedding! For a brief, glorious moment, it felt like our battered trio might finally catch a break. The roasted buns are crisp, the lamb is plentiful, and Bai Lingmiao’s eyes are sparkling with that very human envy of a bride’s big day. But in the Dao-Twisted World, comfort is always a prelude to a gut punch. This chapter masterfully lulls you into a sense of cozy normalcy before dropping the cold, familiar dread right back into Li Huowang’s lap. Prepare for the tonal whiplash as our weary protagonist realizes that not even a joyous village ceremony is safe from the world’s corrupted gods.

## Key Plot Points

- The group arrives at a village in Later Shu, where a large wedding is underway. They use their escort credentials as an excuse to join the feast, gaining access with surprising ease.
- Li Huowang, ever cautious, uses Mantou to test the food for poison before allowing the group to eat. His paranoia is a direct scar from past horrors.
- The feast is a stark cultural contrast to Siqi, with a heavy focus on meat, dairy, and baked goods. Li Huowang notes the “hou shu” (Later Shu) setting.
- The wedding ritual unfolds in stages: the bride crosses a basin (for luck/redness), then a saddle (for peace), all punctuated by a master of ceremonies’ rhythmic gong.
- Bai Lingmiao’s visible longing for the ceremony triggers a moment of quiet tenderness between her and Li Huowang.
- The ceremony’s climax comes when the emcee announces a bow to the “囍神” (Double Joy God).
- Li Huowang is immediately triggered, his mind flashing back to earlier lore and recognizing the entity’s true, terrifying nature.
- The villagers and wedding party are oblivious, continuing the joyful rites as Li Huowang sits in silent, frozen dread.
- The chapter ends with his dark realization: these people have no idea what they are worshipping, and a concerned Gouwa asks if he’s having a relapse.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The “Dusk” Wedding (昏时成亲):** The chapter begins with Li Huowang noting that people in Later Shu marry at dusk. Historically, this is not just a quirky custom but a direct link to classical Chinese ritual. The character for “dusk” (昏, hūn) is the same as the character for “marriage” in ancient texts (婚). The *Book of Rites* (《礼记》) describes the ceremony as taking place “at the conjunction of yin and yang, when darkness rises,” perfectly capturing the liminal state between day and night. The author uses this historical fact to add an authentic folkloric layer to the culture.

**The “Xi Shen” (喜神) vs. The “囍 Shen” (囍神):** This is a critical lore distinction. In Chinese folk religion, the “Xi Shen” (喜神) is a benevolent deity of happiness and joy, often invoked in wedding ceremonies. However, our novel has previously established that the “Xi Shen” is a terrifying, corrupted cosmic entity. The “囍 Shen” (Double Joy God), represented by the double “囍” character commonly seen on wedding decorations, is implied to be an even more potent, fundamental version of that horror. The emcee’s line, “Bowing to the Double Joy God!!”, isn’t just a name change—it’s activating a deeper, more dangerous layer of the world’s corrupted celestial bureaucracy. Li Huowang’s fear is justified; the “囍” is a symbol of joy, but in this universe, it hides something monstrous.

**Ritual Bridges (跨盆/跨鞍):** The bride stepping over a basin of fire (跨火盆) and a saddle (跨鞍) are authentic Chinese folk wedding traditions. The basin represents cleansing away evil spirits and bringing a “red hot” life, while the saddle (鞍) is a pun on the word for “peace” (安), bringing safety to the new household. The author uses these real, comforting rituals to create a contrast with the terrifying entity being invoked. The mundane safety of the saddle makes the bow to the “囍 Shen” feel all the more like a trap.

### Stepping over the basin (垮盆)

A wedding ritual where the bride steps over a brazier of fire to cleanse evil and bring a “red” (prosperous) life.

### Stepping over the saddle (垮鞍)

A wedding ritual where the bride steps over a saddle, a pun on the word for “peace” (安), to bring safety to the household.

## Reading Guide

Buckle up, cultivators, because this chapter is a masterclass in tonal whiplash. The author baits you with the most classic of web novel comforts—a free banquet at a stranger’s house—only to twist it into a fresh anxiety. The key here is to focus on *how* Li Huowang perceives this threat. It’s not about a monster jumping out of the shadows; it’s about a systemic horror he can see that everyone else ignores. This is the quintessential “he knows the truth, and it’s isolating him” moment.

Pay attention to Bai Lingmiao’s reaction. Her envy of the bride isn’t just a romantic scene; it’s a reminder of the simple, human life that Li Huowang can never have. His squeeze of her hand is a silent, desperate promise he can’t fully keep because his world is full of “囍神.” The chapter’s real horror is the silence. The chatter, the gongs, the joyous laughter—all of it becomes noise covering up a prayer to a silent, terrible god. The final question, “Do they bow to that thing for weddings everywhere else?” is the punchline: for Li Huowang, the answer is always yes.
