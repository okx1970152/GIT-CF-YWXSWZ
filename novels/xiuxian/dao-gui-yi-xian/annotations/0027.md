## Chapter Overview

Get ready, fellow Daoists—Li Huowang finally gets a taste of the *real* wilderness, and it’s not what he expected. After being cooped up in Danyangzi’s nightmare cave-temple, he steps out into the open road, only to run smack into a classic piece of folk-horror: the **title-asking spirit**. This chapter is a masterclass in atmosphere—a bone-walking woman with no back to her head, a family of terrified traveling performers, and a protagonist who’s realizing the world is both scarier *and* less crowded with monsters than his twisted mentor led him to believe. Li Huowang’s voice shifts here: he’s no longer a hunted victim but a calculating survivor, learning the rules, testing his tools, and filing away every piece of knowledge like a man trying to write his own survival manual. It’s a breather chapter that breathes *wrong*, and that’s exactly why it works.

## Key Plot Points

- The Lü family’s camp is attacked by a small-footed woman spirit; Lü Zhuangyuan is terrified and recognizes the creature as a “title-asking” entity.
- Lü Zhuangyuan’s attempt to fight back (throwing a burning branch) fails completely—the fire passes through her.
- Bai Lingmiao makes a sharp observation: the woman has no back to her skull—both sides are a face, confirming her as a classic two-faced spirit.
- Li Huowang draws his sword and uses Danyangzi’s dented Daoist bell; the bell’s ringing destabilizes the spirit, forcing it to retreat.
- Li Huowang notes the bell cannot summon the Wandering Lord (likely due to damage), but it proves effective as a general repellent against yin entities.
- After the crisis, Lü Zhuangyuan becomes effusively grateful, offering food and flattery; Li Huowang seizes the moment to interrogate him about the world’s rules.
- Lü Zhuangyuan’s knowledge is revealed to be secondhand folklore—he has only encountered such entities four times in decades of travel.
- Li Huowang’s internal realization: the common people live *far* from these horrors, despite Danyangzi’s world being saturated with them.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter introduces one of the most enduring and unsettling motifs in Chinese folk horror: **讨口封 (Tǎo Kǒu Fēng)**, or “asking for a title.” In folk belief, a spirit or demon that has been cultivating for ages can request a confirmation of its humanity from a living person. If the person says “you look like a human,” the spirit gains power and form; if they say “you look like a god,” the spirit ascends and may reward or curse the speaker. If they refuse to answer, or answer wrongly, calamity follows. In *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, this concept is literalized into a terrifying encounter where the spirit doesn’t just *ask*—it *presses* until it gets what it wants.

Also noteworthy: the **two-faced woman** (两面女) in this chapter embodies a specific subtype of Chinese ghost lore where the entity’s anatomy is geometrically wrong—no back, no hidden side. This visual uncanniness is a staple of the novel’s horror aesthetics: a human shape that *almost* works, but fails in a single, bone-chilling detail.

The mention of **child’s urine (童子尿)** as a repellent is a classic piece of Chinese folk magic. It is considered “pure yang filth”—the one substance that bridges the gap between sacred and unclean, making it uniquely effective against yin entities. The fact that Lü Zhuangyuan has *never actually used it* but insists it works underscores the oral-tradition nature of this world’s knowledge system: everybody knows the rule, nobody has tested it.

### Two-faced woman (两面女)

A type of spirit in Chinese folk horror with identical faces on both sides of the head—no back, no hidden side. Her unnerving anatomy is a hallmark of the novel's body-horror aesthetic.

### Child's urine (童子尿)

In Chinese folk magic, a “pure yang filth” substance believed to repel yin entities. Its power comes from bridging the gap between the sacred and the unclean.

## Reading Guide

Okay, deep breath. After the claustrophobic, flesh-horror gauntlet of Danyangzi’s temple, this chapter is a palate cleanser—if your palate is made of ash and crushed fluorescent rock. The biggest takeaway? **Li Huowang is starting to *get* it.** He’s no longer flailing; he’s cataloguing. He tests the bell, learns its limitations, and immediately cross-references the old man’s folk wisdom against the creature’s behavior. This is a protagonist who is learning how to *play* the game, even if he still doesn’t know all the rules. Pay close attention to the way he processes the old man’s ignorance—it’s not contempt, it’s *filing*. He’s building a mental map of what people *think* is true versus what actually works. That’s the survival instinct of a xinsu. Also, note how Bai Lingmiao’s observation skill is highlighted: she spots the two-faced anatomy before Li Huowang does. She’s not just the blindfolded maiden anymore; she’s becoming a sharp-eyed companion. For newcomers, if you’ve been wondering whether this is all just “Chinese horror with extra steps,” this chapter is where the folkloric roots really bite down. Stay with it.
