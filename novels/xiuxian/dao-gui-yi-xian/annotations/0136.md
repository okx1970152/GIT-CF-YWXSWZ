## Chapter Overview
After the relentless pressure of Danyangzi's looming possession, Chapter 136 is a rare, uneasy exhale. Li Huowang leads his group into a bustling market town for rest and a hot meal. The atmosphere feels almost *normal*—mahjong tiles clatter, beggars sing for coins, and a hearty feast is laid out. But beneath this veneer of calm, a deeper dread crystallizes: the Lu troupe’s patriarch and his son argue in the stable with the quiet terror of men who have realized their leader might be a monster, yet are too afraid of the coming chaos to flee. It’s the horror of mundane social collapse, framed by a simple dinner.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang notes that Anci Nunnery is filthy but decides it can wait; the immediate threat of Danyangzi has receded, allowing him his first real pause.
- In town, the increased number of beggars is a quiet sign that the world outside is breaking down.
- A gambling hall full of mahjong players offers a jarring slice of ordinary life in the Dao-Twisted World.
- Li Huowang orders a lavish meal (no pork) despite Bai Lingmiao’s concern about their dwindling funds; he treats this as a small celebration.
- Jiang Yingzi’s ghost curse continues; Li Huowang has grown numbly accustomed to a dead woman shouting in his ear and a distant Danyangzi watching silently.
- The Lu family’s dinner-table deference reveals a deepening wariness around Li Huowang.
- In a private stable-side conversation, Lü Juren pleads with his father to leave, terrified that Li Huowang is a “monster” who will eat them. Lü Zhuangyuan refuses, arguing that the coming social collapse makes the dangerous young Daoist their best protection.
- Lü Zhuangyuan notices his daughter-in-law’s lack of appetite and sickness, suggesting pregnancy, and sends his son to fetch a doctor.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter is a masterclass in showing, not telling, the *shape* of apocalypse. The beggars are an early indicator—they are not yet starving in heaps, but there are *more* of them than before. In pre-modern China (and in xianxia worlds modeled on it), a sudden rise in beggars was one of the first signs that harvests had failed, bandits were active, or the state’s relief systems had broken. Lü Zhuangyuan reads this correctly: soon, people will be selling their children. His decision to stay with Li Huowang is not loyalty, but a cold, desperate survival calculation.

The mahjong scene is also worth noting. Mahjong (麻将) is a tile-based game that became wildly popular across China in the late Qing dynasty and remains a staple of social gambling. Its appearance here—the clatter of tiles, the cry of "self-drawn Pure One Suit"—acts as a deliberate anchor of normalcy, making the dread beneath the chapter feel even more dissonant.

Finally, the "threat" of a child-eating monster is a classic folk tale trope (from *The Legend of the White Snake* to oral "beware the stranger" warnings). Lü Juren’s fear that Li Huowang eats children is not random; it is a folk-coded accusation of demonic identity, reflecting the story’s core tension between what Li Huowang *looks like* (a monster) and what he *is* (a struggling survivor).

## Reading Guide
This is a quiet chapter, but it should not feel slow. Notice how Li Huowang has changed: he no longer panics at Jiang Yingzi’s phantom screams, he files away Danyangzi’s distant stare as background noise. He has become a creature of numb, functional tolerance. The most important scene is tucked away in the stable: Lü Juren’s whispered terror and Lü Zhuangyuan’s grim logic. Their argument is a microcosm of the novel’s central question—*do you trust the monster who protects you, or do you run from him into certain death?* That Li Huowang doesn’t even hear this conversation makes it all the more chilling; he has become a force of nature that others must navigate, not a person they can talk to.
