## Chapter Overview

This chapter takes a breather from the cosmic horror to deliver a sharp, character-driven encounter that tests Li Huowang’s new boundaries. Wandering the prosperous streets of Yinling City, he stumbles upon a face from the past: Lü Xiucai, the teenage troupe son who inherited the Purple-Tasseled Sword. What starts as a moment of uneasy nostalgia quickly curdles into a display of Li Huowang’s hardened pragmatism. He offers neither comfort nor mentorship, only a cold, transactional severance. The emotional blow is softened only by a quiet update on Bai Lingmiao’s well-being and a bizarre new development: Li Sui may now be able to perceive the hallucinations that haunt Li Huowang, a revelation that threatens to collapse yet another boundary in his fractured reality.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang wakes late after heavy drinking and decides to stay in Yinling City pending Tuoba Danqing’s next move.
- He walks the streets deliberately to expose Li Sui to a wide range of human speech, confirming that Li Sui’s mimicry is indiscriminate—absorbing curses and street vendors alike.
- He spots a familiar figure staring blankly at a food stall: Lü Xiucai, who has run away from the opera troupe to seek his fortune.
- Lü Xiucai reveals his travel money was stolen, leaving him stranded; Li Huowang buys him a bowl of wontons.
- Through Lü Xiucai, Li Huowang learns that Niuxin Village is thriving and that Bai Lingmiao is well, though often distracted.
- Lü Xiucai begs to follow Li Huowang, but is harshly rejected. Li Huowang gives him silver for the return trip and five blackened coins from the copper coin sword as a final, severance gift.
- Red Center jokes that Li Sui should call Bai Lingmiao “Mother,” triggering the alarming realization that Li Sui can hear the hallucinations.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**“Heirloom in a Strange Land”** (他乡遇故知): This chapter’s title invokes one of classical Chinese literature’s “Four Great Joys of Life.” The intended joy is ironic here—Li Huowang’s encounter is less a happy reunion than a bitter reminder of the ties he has left behind. The gap between what a “joy” ought to feel like and what it actually delivers is a signature Dao-Twisted-World move.

**Street Vendors and Proverb Rhythms**: The vendor calls Li Huowang hears—“磨剪子嘞锵菜刀” (sharpening scissors, honing cleavers)—are a staple of pre-modern Chinese urban soundscapes. The rhythmic, melodic shout is a form of oral branding; older readers would recognize it instantly. The novel inserts these markers of everyday normalcy to make the jarring contrast with Li Huowang’s inner alienation feel sharper.

**Copper Coin Sword**: a ritual Daoist tool made by stringing coins together. The five coins Li Huowang leaves for Lü Xiucai are not merely currency—they are detached from a ritual object, carrying residual spiritual weight as the old copper coin sword is dismantled. In traditional settings, such objects were believed to absorb yang energy from circulation and could serve as makeshift talismans.

## Reading Guide

Fellow travelers, this chapter is a quiet masterclass in Li Huowang’s emotional armor. Watch how he processes the village news: he barely reacts to his own legacy (the fields, the oxen), but the moment Bai Lingmiao is mentioned—just “eats and sleeps well, often distracted”—he scrubs his face, breathes deep, and mutters a mantra of relief. That’s the beating heart beneath the scar tissue. The rejection of Lü Xiucai is brutal, but it’s also the correct tactical call for a man being hunted by gods and institutions alike. And that final reveal—Li Sui can hear Red Center?—changes the rules. If the hallucinations can be heard by his own flesh-and-blood parasite, how much more real are they than Li Huowang is willing to admit? The ground keeps shifting under our feet, and that’s exactly why we can’t look away.
