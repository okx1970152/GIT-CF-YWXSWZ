## Chapter Overview
Get ready for a chapter that feels like a slow, bitter punch to the gut. After all the cosmic horror and divine scheming, ‘Old Ox’ brings us crashing back down to earth with a stark look at the mundane cruelties of the Dao-Twisted World. Lü Xiucai and Gouwa’s search for their master hits a wall of family drama and social humiliation, forcing our angry young scholar to confront a truth he never saw coming: his gruff, seemingly simple father actually had a long-term plan for him all along. But the real gut-punch comes when the shiny new sword they’ve been polishing meets a brick wall of bureaucratic reality. This is a chapter about powerlessness, the quiet grief of a lost homeland, and the indomitable, ox-like spirit of those who just keep putting one foot in front of the other.

## Key Plot Points
- Gouwa explains his plan to use the city’s beggars to find Li Huowang, revealing that the tactic came from Yang Xiaohai, not himself, leading to a spat with Lü Xiucai.
- The duo discusses their post-rescue contingency plans, ranging from a happy return to Niuxin Village to bringing Li Huowang’s body home for a hero’s burial.
- They encounter the Lü family opera troupe, stalled by the road after Lü Zhuangyuan receives a devastating piece of news: in the Great Liang Empire, actors are barred from taking the imperial exams.
- Lü Xiucai angrily confronts his father, who for the first time explains his reasoning for dividing his three sons among different paths (divine arts, opera, and exams), shocking his son with the revelation that he, too, was considered a viable ‘way out’ for the family.
- A group of local thugs arrives to demand protection money, humiliating Lü Xiucai when they invoke the authority of the Supervisory Heavenly Office, and forcing Lü Zhuangyuan to pay them off with a forced smile.
- Lü Zhuangyuan reveals that Siqi (their homeland) has been destroyed and its emperor executed, crushing any hope of returning home.
- The chapter ends with Lü Zhuangyuan, bearing the weight of a ruined world on his shoulders, stoically leading his family forward like an old ox.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司):** This isn’t just the local police force. Think of it as the empire’s supernatural intelligence agency, internal affairs bureau, and monster-hunting division all rolled into one. Its presence overpowers most divine powers, acting as a systemic dampener on ‘reckless’ cultivation. A thug claiming to have a buddy at the gate isn’t just a bully—he’s wielding the ultimate symbol of institutional authority that even a cultivator has to fear. This perfectly illustrates the novel’s core theme: raw power means nothing against the iron fist of a state that’s already seen it all.

**The Three Sons, Three Baskets Philosophy:** Lü Zhuangyuan’s plan is a perfect example of traditional risk management in a pre-modern setting. By forcing his three sons down radically different paths—divine arts, traditional opera, and classical scholarship—he creates a diversified ‘family portfolio.’ It’s a strategy born from being at the bottom of the social ladder. If one path fails or gets outlawed (as both opera and divine arts were in different empires), the other two might survive to support the clan. It’s a brutal, pragmatic kind of love.

**The Profound Grief of a Lost Nation:** Lü Xiucai’s shock at learning his homeland has been destroyed taps into a unique historical and emotional well. For a commoner in imperial China, your ‘nation’ was less a map and more a local identity tied to a social order. When that order is violently overthrown (the emperor beheaded), the very concept of ‘home’ becomes a phantom. It’s a trauma on a scale that transcends personal loss—it’s the erasure of an entire reality.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in using low-stakes conflict to build high-tension emotion. Pay close attention to the structure: the chapter begins with a petty argument between Gouwa and Xiucai, escalates to a family crisis, and then crashes into the cold, oppressive reality of the capital. The thugs aren’t a major threat; they are a *symbol*. They represent a world where even the lowliest bully has a connection to untouchable power, perfectly setting the stage for why every character feels so trapped.

The heart of the chapter is the brief, explosive confrontation with Lü Zhuangyuan. This is the first time he’s ever explained himself, and it completely reframes his character. He’s not a stubborn, illiterate old man; he’s a cunning strategist who has been playing the long game with the only currency he has: his sons’ futures. His final lament—that his homeland is gone—is the closing of a door. The old ox is walking toward an unknown future with nothing but the weight on his shoulders and the dust of his past on his feet. There’s a deep, quiet tragedy here that cuts far deeper than any monster attack.
