## Chapter Overview
Oh, so you thought the Ao-Jing Sect was just another bunch of Bashe-worshipping weirdos? Get ready to have that assumption gutted like a fish. This chapter isn't about a fight—it's about a conversation that peels back the sect's real agenda, and it's far more disturbing than simple worship. Li Huowang enters looking for a cure for Danyangzi and leaves realizing these "healers" might be offering a prescription from hell itself. Shou San, the charred and cheerful cult leader, lays out a philosophy that redefines every rule we thought we knew about power, pain, and what it means to be "sick" in the Dao-Twisted World. Buckle up, fellow sufferer—this one gets dark in a whole new way.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang confronts Shou San, realizing his earlier guess was wrong: the Ao-Jing Sect does not *worship* Bashe; they intend to *steal* its power, just as their own deity, the Gu-Spirit, supposedly did before them.
- Shou San explains their philosophy: "the Buddha was once a man" becomes a justification for deicide through emulation. Bashe is not a god to pray to but a precedessor to surpass.
- When Li Huowang demands help to get rid of Danyangzi, Shou San insists the only way is for Li Huowang to perform the Dung-Beetle Ascension again—summoning Bashe through extreme agony.
- Li Huowang admits he cannot summon the pain required; it's not a switch he can flip.
- Shou San reveals a deeply unsettling "cure": to create the necessary *mental* anguish, they propose Li Huowang take a girl, make her love him, impregnate her, and then—at the peak of her pregnancy—cut the fetus out of her belly.
- Li Huowang interrupts the description, his temple throbbing, realizing the Ao-Jing Sect's methods are just as monstrous as Danyangzi's twisted alchemy.
- Shou San cheerfully misinterprets Li Huowang's horror as *approval* of the plan's mildness, offering to escalate with drugs and Bashe's blessing.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Three Worms (三尸 / San Shi)**: Shou San casually dismisses the Daoist concept of the "Three Corpses" or "Three Worms"—malevolent spirit-entities that live inside the human body, craving decay and reporting a person's sins to heaven. Traditional Daoist cultivation aims to starve or expel them through asceticism and good deeds, as a prerequisite to immortality. Shou San's mockery of this doctrine is a signature of the novel: *every* sacred path is hollow or corrupted. The "Way of the Five Pecks of Rice" he mentions is a real historical Daoist movement from the Han dynasty, one of the earliest organized Daoist sects, known for its communal practices and healing rituals.

**Ao-Jing Sect (袄景教)**: This is a fictional fusion of two real but historically distinct religions. *Ao Jiao* (袄教) is the Chinese name for Zoroastrianism, a Persian dualistic faith centered on a cosmic struggle between good (Ahura Mazda) and evil (Angra Mainyu), often associated with fire worship. *Jing Jiao* (景教) is the Chinese name for Nestorian Christianity, an early branch of Christianity that spread along the Silk Road to Tang-dynasty China. By fusing them, the novel creates a completely alien and heretical sect that feels both historically grounded and profoundly wrong.

**The Gu-Spirit (牯神)**: Shou San's mention of the "Gu-Spirit" as a former mortal who stole Bashe's power introduces a horrifying new layer to the world's divine hierarchy. The name "Gu" (牯) usually refers to a male bovine, but in this context, it suggests a crude, earthly, and envious god—a far cry from the serene deities of orthodox religion.

## Reading Guide
Okay, here's the real gut punch of this chapter: the Ao-Jing Sect's "cure" isn't a medical procedure; it's a *ritual of desecration*. Shou San doesn't want to help Li Huowang—he wants to use Li Huowang to perform an act of such profound emotional and physical violence that the resulting pain becomes a offering to Bashe. The girl isn't a victim of the ritual; she is *the ritual*. The pregnancy isn't about creating life; it's about creating the perfect moment to destroy it.

Notice the narrative structure: Li Huowang enters this conversation with the intellectual upper hand, correcting his own mistake about the sect's beliefs. He's in control. But the moment Shou San describes the plan, the power dynamic flips completely. The horror doesn't come from a monster jumping out of the shadows; it comes from a calm, reasonable man explaining an atrocity as if it were a sensible medical recommendation.

Also, watch how the translation handles Li Huowang's reaction: his temple throbbing, his voice shaking. This is a *physical* response to a *conceptual* horror. It's not just that the act is evil—it's that the Ao-Jing Sect frames it with such cheerful, clinical precision. The most terrifying line in the chapter isn't the gory description of cutting the belly open—it's Shou San's simple assumption that Li Huowang stopped him because the pain *wasn't intense enough*.

This chapter is a masterclass in slow-burn dread. Enjoy your sleepless night.
