## Chapter Overview

Family drama explodes on stage, but that's just the appetizer. The main course? A full-blown military siege led by a female general whose face reads "精忠报国" like a curse. Li Huowang's cover is blown—not his identity, but his *presence*—and the slaughter order has already been given. This chapter pivots from the personal horror of a violated wife's public shame to the absolute, systematic terror of an army that kills first and asks questions never. Buckle up, fellow travelers—this one gets messy.

## Key Plot Points

- Lü Juren publicly humiliates his wife Luo Juan on stage for her behavior in the bandit den, triggering a raw, multi-generational family confrontation about shame, survival, and the crushing weight of being "lower than low."
- Li Huowang quietly intervenes, separating the two parties and trying to keep the troupe functional.
- The audience vanishes; panic spreads through the town as people gather at its edges.
- Li Huowang investigates and finds the town completely surrounded by a motley army of convict soldiers (贼配军), identifiable by the blue "囚" tattoo on their foreheads.
- He spots Hou Er among them, confirming the army is here for him.
- A local gentryman runs out to negotiate and is immediately decapitated by the general without a word—demonstrating that this is not a negotiation.
- The general lifts her visor to reveal a woman's face covered in the tattoo "精忠报国" (Serve the Country with Utmost Loyalty), its brutality making her features monstrous.
- She announces Qi spies have infiltrated and issues a chilling order: "One day without sheathing your blades. Slaughter them all."

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Tattooed Convict Soldiers (贼配军, zéi pèi jūn)**: In historical China, military conscription often drew from convicts, exiles, and social outcasts—men who were branded or tattooed to mark their status. These "criminal armies" were used for high-risk campaigns because they were expendable. The blue "囚" (prisoner) character on their foreheads is a literal brand of shame. In the novel, this historical practice is weaponized into a visually terrifying, lawless force that treats massacre as standard procedure.

**"精忠报国" Tattoo**: This phrase is famously associated with the Song dynasty general Yue Fei, whose mother supposedly tattooed these words on his back as a lifelong oath of loyalty. It carries immense patriotic weight in Chinese culture. Seeing it tattooed on the *face* of a woman leading a slaughter is a deliberate perversion of the symbol—it turns a noble motto into a warrant for atrocity.

**One Day Without Sheathing Blades (一日不封刀)**: A traditional military code in ancient warfare. When a commander declared "no sheathing of blades for X days," it meant a complete, unconstrained massacre—looting, killing, and destruction were permitted with no repercussions. It was a form of psychological warfare and punishment for resistance. Its use here signals that no one in the town is safe, regardless of guilt.

**Short-Swords (短兵)**: In chapter 6's glossary, "short-sword" was standardized as an ancient Chinese military term for a general's personal bodyguard—not a weapon but a human shield unit. The men carrying black iron spears and sticking close to the general are exactly this: his elite retinue, not random infantry.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in escalation from the personal to the systemic. The first half hits you with the raw, ugly reality of a marriage broken by trauma—Lü Juren's shame and Luo Juan's desperate defense are both heartbreaking and infuriating, because neither of them is wrong. The horror isn't a monster; it's poverty, class, and a system that leaves no room for dignity.

Then the second half drops a hammer. The female general is a new kind of threat—not a demon, not a corrupt monk, but a human with absolute authority and zero hesitation. Her face tattoo transforms a symbol of loyalty into a mark of terror. Her immediate execution of the negotiator tells you everything: this is not an army you can reason with.

Li Huowang's reaction is telling. He's not panicking—he's calculating. But the chapter ends before he can act. That's the cliffhanger: the enemy is here, they're fast, and they've already given the order.

**Lore Deep Dive: The "囚" Brand & Convict Armies in Chinese History**

Let's talk about that blue tattoo. In traditional Chinese criminal law, certain crimes—especially exile-worthy offenses—were punished by tattooing the face or forehead with the character for the crime. This was called *mo* (墨) or *ci* (刺) punishment. Once tattooed, you were marked for life, socially dead even if physically free.

Convict armies (*peijun*, 配军) were a Song dynasty innovation—send the criminals to the frontier, give them weapons, and point them at the enemy. They were cheap, plentiful, and utterly ruthless because they had nothing left to lose. In the Dao-Twisted World, this historical reality is twisted into a nightmare: an army of marked men and women who see slaughter as routine and answer to a commander who wears "loyalty" on her face like a brand of her own.
