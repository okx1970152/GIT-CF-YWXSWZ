## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this chapter hits you with a double dose of gut-twisting tension. On one side of the abyss, we follow Lyu Xiucai’s frantic return to the Lyu household, babbling about his master being snatched away by a coalition of six formation-wielding cultivators and a half-immortal. The old man tries to slam the door on him, but Xiucai is utterly convinced that if he plays hero now, he’ll earn the ultimate payoff: a secret technique from his master’s immortal hand. On the other side, we swing right back into the psychiatric ward where Li Huowang is trussed up in a straitjacket and metal muzzle, facing off against Wu Cheng—the man who just dropped the nice-guy mask. With Yang Na’s safety dangled like a blade over his loved ones, Li Huowang makes a chilling demand: he won’t talk to a pawn. He wants the man behind the curtain.

## Key Plot Points

- Lyu Xiucai bursts into his brother’s home, scarfing down food and announcing that Li Huowang has been abducted by a group of at least seven experts—six with a formation and one demigod.
- Lyu Zhuangyuan frantically forbids his son from going to the rescue, but Xiucai reveals his purely transactional motivation: he’s the only disciple, and if he sheds blood for his master now, the priceless technique will be his when Master ascends.
- The old man threatens to disown him—Xiucai walks out anyway, shouting that he won’t let any “low-born roosters and dogs” ride his coattails to heaven.
- Back in the modern world, Li Huowang wakes up restrained. Wu Cheng, no longer pretending to be a neutral therapist, tries to coerce him into cooperation.
- Wu Cheng reveals that Yi Donglai’s research is an experiment that will drive Li Huowang mad, and proposes his own shadowy organization as the only viable alternative.
- The surveillance-camera red light dims—Wu Cheng electroshocks Li Huowang to soften him up, but Li Huowang only laughs harder, even when the taser runs out.
- Wu Cheng shows a video of Yang Na walking toward a university gate, wearing earrings Li Huowang gave her. The threat is unmistakable: her pain for his defiance.
- After a long silence, Li Huowang goes eerily calm and demands a face-to-face with whoever is pulling the strings—no more pawn-level talk.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**低九流的姓 / Lowly surname from the Nine Classes:** In traditional Chinese society, the “Nine Classes” (九流) was a way of hierarchizing professions—Confucian scholars at the top, entertainers and servants at the bottom. Lyu Xiucai’s jab—“low-born Lyu”—is a deliberate insult to his own lineage. In his eyes, these are people tied to a vulgar, mortal status system. By joining a Daoist’s ascension path, he believes he is rebranding himself above all that. This is a classic trope in xianxia: the low-born disciple who wants to shed his social shackles by latching onto a master’s breakthrough—only here, the master is Li Huowang, and the “breakthrough” is swimming through madness, blood, and ontological terror.

**半仙 / Demigod:** Not quite “half a god”; in many xianxia contexts, a “demigod” (or someone called such) often stands at the cusp of true immortality or possesses a fragmentary divine quality. Xiucai’s description—“one of them was a demigod”—tells us that the kidnappers aren’t just street-level thugs. They have access to borderline-immortal combat power, enough to overwhelm Heart-Element Li Huowang when he’s caught off guard.

**师徒之间的利益交换 / Master-disciple transactional loyalty:** Classic xianxia often romanticizes the master-disciple bond as pure reverence. Here, Lyu Xiucai spells it out in brutally pragmatic terms: if I bleed for you now, you’ll owe me a technique later. This mirrors the cold, “ladder-climbing” ethos found in more cynical strands of the genre—where devotion is a form of spiritual investment.

### Nine Classes (九流)

Traditional Chinese social hierarchy ranking professions from scholar-officials to entertainers and servants. Lyu Xiucai uses it as an insult to dismiss his own low-status lineage.

### Straits of a disciple’s duty (师恩回报)

In traditional master-disciple relationships, the disciple is expected to repay the master’s teaching through loyalty and service. Xiucai interprets this as a transactional investment.

## Reading Guide

This chapter presents the perfect mirror puzzle: on one side, we have Lyu Xiucai’s gleeful, transactional loyalty in the Dao-Twisted World (“I’ll save him for the technique!”); on the other, Li Huowang’s stone-cold defiance in the modern world (“I won’t negotiate with a pawn”). Both men are driven by a calculating survival instinct, but the tones couldn't be more different. Xiucai’s enthusiasm feels almost comedic—the overeager disciple who thinks he’s already halfway to his reward. Li Huowang’s calm silence, by contrast, is the quiet before a storm. The surface-level “rescue” plot (Xiucai recruiting simpletons and bravos) may look like a minor side-thread, but it reminds us that Li Huowang is not alone in his world, even if his “allies” are schemers and opportunists. Pay attention to that final cold request: “I want to talk to the person behind you. Face to face.” Li Huowang isn’t just buying time. He’s repositioning himself for a real counterstrike—and the person behind Wu Cheng should be very, very nervous.
