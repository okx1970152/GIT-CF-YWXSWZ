## Chapter Overview

Welcome back, fellow pilgrims of the Twisted Dao. If you thought last chapter’s water-horror was the end of the ordeal, think again. Chapter 389, “Apricot Island,” is a masterclass in aftermath, showing us a protagonist who has crawled out of the monster’s belly—literally—only to find that the world still insists on trying to worship him as a god. This chapter is a grim, weary meditation on survival, identity, and the price of being the thing people fear. Get ready: the calm before the next storm smells like incense and rotting hopes.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang wakes up on the boat to find the crew kowtowing to him with “dragon king” offerings, including a terrified pair of children. He reacts with explosive rage, kicking over the incense burner and yanking the kids free.
- He realizes his body is covered in Black Tai Sui tendrils, which explains why the crew sees him as a possessed entity. He orders Li Sui to retract.
- The monk recounts how Li Huowang escaped the underwater horror: he burst out from the creature’s belly and crawled back onto the boat. Li Huowang reconstructs the hallucination-physics that saved his life.
- A severe auditory hallucination of Yang Na’s voice strikes him. He forces it down through frantic self-reassurance, labeling the modern-world events as “hallucinations” to preserve his sanity.
- He uses the *True Sutra of the Fire Vestments* to heal, while Red Center’s sarcastic commentary grinds at him, and the old gambler’s hope flares—if North Wind knows how to break the hallucination curse, he can still save himself.
- A short gambling scene reveals Li Huowang’s cold, pragmatic streak: he wins consistently, not with tricks but with focus, taking the money with flat satisfaction.
- Upon arriving at Apricot Island, Li Huowang faces a new problem: finding Zhuge Yuan on an island as big as half a province. His solution is a brutal work of self-surgery: he carves a bone from his own severed arm to use as a divination tool.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter contains one of the most visceral acts of self-mutilation in the story so far, and it’s worth unpacking the **alchemical and divinatory logic** behind it. In Daoist and folk magical traditions, **human bone** is considered a potent medium for divination—particularly for communication with the dead or for directional seeking. The idea is that bone, as the body’s most permanent structure, holds a direct link to the person’s fate. The act of using your own bone to “find” someone you trust on a cosmic level (Zhuge Yuan) is a desperate, literal form of *connecting essence to essence*.

Also noteworthy is the **offering scene**: the boat crew sets up “boy and girl” spirit children as part of their worship. This mirrors a dark folk practice known in parts of rural China where child effigies or live children were used as “dragons” to placate water deities. In the Dao-Twisted World, it is both a sign of Li Huowang’s terrifying appearance and the system’s willingness to sacrifice children to anything that might be a god.

### Self-bone extraction

Using one’s own bone as a tool for divination is a desperate, self-harming act that reflects the novel’s theme of paying a literal price for power.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is all about the *aftermath* of horror, and the translator does a fantastic job of letting the dread linger instead of replacing it with action. Notice how Li Huowang’s rage at being worshipped is not just a moral reaction—it’s a trauma response. He has been used as a human ingredient, a puppet, a target. To be deified is just another form of being dehumanized. His reaction is raw, ugly, and totally justified.

The **gambling scene** is a gem of suppressed characterization. Underneath his monstrous exterior, Li Huowang is still capable of sharp, calculating calm. Winning at cards is a small, human victory—a reminder that he exists beyond being a broken tool for cosmic forces.

And then there’s that final moment: carving his own bone out of his arm. This is not desperation. This is **terrible effectiveness**. He has learned that in this world, no price is too high if it means finding the only person who might help him. The chapter ends not with a revelation, but with a decision: brutal, cold, and deeply, deeply lonely.
