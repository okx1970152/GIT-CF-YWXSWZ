## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang is healing. His skin has grown back, *The Thousand Greats Record* is safely buried under his flesh, and the endless grasslands of Qingqiu feel almost... peaceful. But the quiet gives him room to think—and what he thinks about is the two headless corpses that follow him everywhere he goes. Peng Longteng and Jin Shan Zhang stand as silent witnesses to his past violence, while Li Huowang pokes at his own dead skin, feeds it to his dog, and speculates on whether his hallucinations are a Heart-Element ability or proof that he really is just losing his mind. As the group rests before the final leg to Nüren Mountain, he interrogates the quietest member of the party, Sun Baolu, about whether this land has any masters who might help him before his head fills up with ghosts.

## Key Plot Points

- The group crosses into Qingqiu successfully. No bandits, no spirits—a rare stretch of easy travel. The injury is improving, morale is cautiously up.
- Li Huowang walks with his crutch instead of lying on the cart; his new skin is growing in, and Mantou the dog has stopped barking at him.
- He scratches off a large piece of blackened dead skin and tosses it to Mantou, who gleefully eats it. This is established routine—Mantou has already eaten Li Huowang's other shed skin and even his severed leg.
- The two headless hallucinations, Peng Longteng and Jin Shan Zhang, still stand behind him. He can hear Jin Shan Zhang's internal organs dragging on the grass.
- Li Huowang turns over his old theories again: *Is this a Heart-Element power? Can Heart-Elements control the people they've killed?* But the monk from Zhengde Temple is still alive, so that theory doesn't hold. He lands on the most terrifying hypothesis: *Maybe I really do have a mental illness.*
- He comforts himself that at least these two can't talk. Immediately, a hallucinatory voice mimics his thought: *"Daosit, you find me annoying?"* — the monk's voice, proving the comfort is hollow.
- He recalls Danyangzi's fate—dozens or hundreds of hallucinations packed into one skull—and shudders. The fear of becoming that is a primary driver now.
- He calls Sun Baolu and questions him about Qingqiu. Sun Baolu says he knows the whole region because his family followed the grass like nomads for generations.
- Li Huowang asks whether Qingqiu has any "masters" like Danyangzi. Sun Baolu, wrapped head to toe as always, just stands there and smiles. The answer is not given in this chapter.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Qingqiu (青丘):** The name itself is a loaded one. In ancient Chinese mythology, Qingqiu is the legendary homeland of the nine-tailed fox spirits, a place of strange beauty and hidden dangers. The novel borrows the name but turns the terrain into an open grassland—a quiet, almost peaceful stage that feels disconnected from the horrors of the Dao-Twisted World. But in this story, peace is never permanent.

**Nomadic pastoralism in the Dao-Twisted World:** Sun Baolu's description of a family that "follows the grass" is a historically grounded depiction of pastoral nomadism in the steppe regions of northern China and Central Asia. In a world where the land is hostile and settled towns are few, such a lifestyle is a survival strategy, not a choice. That Sun Baolu knows the whole region is a rare and valuable asset, but his silence about what he *really* knows is a quiet red flag.

**Heart-Element speculation (心素):** Li Huowang's internal debate here touches on one of the novel's core mysteries. Is the ability to produce hallucinations a supernatural power of the Heart-Element constitution, or is it a symptom of a genuine psychiatric disorder? The novel deliberately refuses to give a clear answer, leaving the reader in the same agonizing uncertainty as the protagonist. Chapter 217 deepens this by framing his fear not of the hallucinations themselves, but of the *consequences*—of becoming a walking menagerie of ghostly prisoners like Danyangzi.

## Reading Guide

This is a quiet chapter, a breather between horrors, but don't be fooled into thinking nothing happens. This is Li Huowang doing what he does best in the lulls: *obsessively cataloging his own internal pathology*. The skin-peeling scene with Mantou is a masterclass in the novel's signature tone—casual, almost domestic body horror that normalizes the grotesque. He feeds his dead skin to his dog. The dog is happy. The reader is deeply unsettled.

The two headless corpses standing behind him in broad daylight are a visual that should make the hair on your neck stand up. They don't do anything. They *just stand there*. And that's the point. They are the weight of his past violence made visible, a reminder that he is never alone in the way he wants to be. His brief mental comfort that "at least they can't talk" is immediately shattered by the monk's voice—proving that his mind is no longer a private space.

Sun Baolu's introduction here is significant. He's been so quiet the reader—and Li Huowang—almost forgot him. But he knows the land, and he's heading home. In the Dao-Twisted World, "home" is a loaded word. A man who smiles and says "someone is waiting for me" is either a man with hope or a man walking into a trap. Keep an eye on him.
