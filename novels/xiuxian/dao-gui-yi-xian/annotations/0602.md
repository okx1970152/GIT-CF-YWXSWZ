## Chapter Overview
This chapter is pure, unadulterated *Dao Gui Yi Xian* energy: the kind of scene that makes you check your own reality for cracks. Trapped in a padded room, Li Huowang attempts a desperate bluff to get to the mastermind behind his torment—but Xu Shou isn’t buying it without proof. So it’s drug time. Under hypnosis and a heavy dose of psychiatric meds, Li Huowang’s mind is forced back into the Dao-Twisted World, where he wakes up mutilated and trapped in an Ao-Jing Sect shrine—face-to-face with the Imperial Preceptor himself, Huangfu Tiangang. The parallel between the two “therapies” is nauseatingly elegant: Wu Cheng’s chemical conditioning on one side, and the world’s brutal pressure to break him down on the other. But here’s where Li Huowang proves he’s not a passive victim. He vomits up the half-dissolved drugs, grabs a saw, and bites a chunk out of Wu Cheng’s face. A visceral, vicious, and deeply satisfying act of refusal.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang pretends to accept Wu Cheng’s terms so he can speak to “the boss” (Xu Shou).
- Xu Shou appears via tablet—an old man in sunglasses demanding proof of Li Huowang’s “supernatural” abilities.
- Wu Cheng forces Li Huowang to take psychiatric medication to “erase” Yi Donglai’s influence and recover his original mental state.
- Under the drug’s effect, Li Huowang undergoes an induced hypnosis and hallucinates being back in Daliang as “Er Jiu,” mutilated in a Cross-Shrine.
- Huangfu Tiangang appears, trying to recruit or neutralize him under the pretext of the Da Liang Dragon Vein.
- Li Huowang sees through the manipulation, uses self-harm in the hallucination (finger-saw, self-induced vomiting) to break the hypnotic state.
- In reality, he vomits the drug slurry onto Wu Cheng, pulls a small saw from his own abdomen, and attacks Wu Cheng viciously—biting his face.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Yang Life Pills (阳寿丹)**. These pills are the entire reason Xu Shou is desperate to use Li Huowang. In Chinese folk and xianxia cosmology, *yang life* is the finite lifespan measured from birth to death—a currency far more precious than gold. The fact that an old man in a suit is chasing immortality pills bridges the clinical modern world and the grimy alchemical desperation of the Dao-Twisted World. They make sense in both contexts: a dying rich man clutching at any straw, even one tainted by madness and delusion.

**“Going through fire blind” or 病急乱投医**. This idiom describes someone so desperate for a cure that they’ll consult any quack or try any method. Xu Shou’s behavior perfectly embodies it: a skeptical, sharp old man who still gives Li Huowang a chance because he’s out of options. It’s a mundane but chilling insight into how even the powerful can be reduced to grasping at myths.

**Ao-Jing Sect’s Cross-Shrine**. The mural of the Gu-Spirit on a twisted cross signals the sect’s syncretic madness—a fusion of Zoroastrian-derived imagery, self-flagellation, and blood rituals. The “cross” isn’t meant to evoke Christianity; it’s a corrupted, parasitic symbol in the novel’s own cosmology, tied to pain, sacrifice, and debt.

## Reading Guide
Li Huowang’s “capitulation” to Xu Shou is transparently false, and the text doesn’t pretend otherwise. What matters is the cost of that lie: the drugs, the hypnosis, the re-entering of the Dao-Twisted World in a broken body. Watch how Li Huowang weaponizes pain—both remembered and real—to snap the hypnotic hold. The saw in his own flesh, the severed finger forced down his throat, the vomiting—these are not random violence. They are deliberate, learned responses, the reflexes of someone who has been broken so many times that breaking *himself* has become a survival skill. The bite on Wu Cheng is grotesque and satisfying, but it also marks a turning point: Li Huowang isn’t just fighting with schemes; he’s fighting with his teeth. Pay attention to the parallel between psychiatric treatment in the modern world and alchemical “pressure” in Daliang—they are the same machine, just dressed differently.
