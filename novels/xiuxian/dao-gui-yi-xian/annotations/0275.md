## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang goes straight to the top. Impersonating an agent of the Supervisory Heavenly Office, he gains an audience with Magistrate Yang Hongzhi, hoping to gather intel on the invisible ghost that causes the horrifying “Retracted Yang” affliction. The magistrate is desperate and almost spills the beans, but stops dead, struck by a paralyzing fear. Before Li Huowang can pry more out of him, the yamen staff erupts into a frantic, absurdist folk ritual—beating bronze gongs, pinching their fingers black, and crawling on the floor to sprinkle chili powder on their own crotches. The sheer ridiculousness of it triggers a sharp pang of suspicion in Li Huowang. This pattern feels too familiar, too staged, almost like a bad joke. Is the town’s terror real, or has he stumbled into another elaborate trap?

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang decides to openly enter the county yamen and impersonate a member of the Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司), a gamble that pays off when the desperate magistrate immediately believes him.
- Magistrate Yang reveals the affliction began on Ghost Festival, but clams up the moment he tries to describe the trigger event at Dead Man’s Slope, terrified of supernatural retaliation.
- In a bizarre turn, the magistrate and his constables perform a grotesque protective ritual: beating bronze gongs, clamping their own middle fingers, crawling on all fours, and rubbing chili powder on their genitals to protect against the invisible ghost.
- Li Huowang is reluctantly drawn into the absurd ritual, but the sheer theatricality of it triggers a deep sense of suspicion. The amateur, almost farcical nature of the ‘solution’ feels painfully familiar.
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang’s internal monologue concluding that this level of ridiculousness feels deliberately staged—a hallmark that something is being hidden behind the mask of superstition.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司):** This is not a generic “heavenly court.” In a novel that systemically corrupts Chinese bureaucratic institutions, the *Supervisory Heavenly Office* functions as a secret police / supernatural affairs bureau under the Ministry of Rites. Li Huowang’s bluff works because it taps into a real fear of authority in this world—just as a town might fear a plague, they fear the Office’s investigation. Impersonating one is a high-risk, high-reward gambit.

**The Bizarre Folk Ritual:** The gong-beating, finger-clamping, and chili-powder sprinkling described here is deliberately **not** authentic Daoist liturgy. It reads like a desperate, self-devised folk invention—a classic example of “folk counter-magic” that arises when a community has no real exorcist and must rely on common-sense (and often painful) remedies. The chili powder to the nether regions is a raw, visceral attempt to “scare off” a ghost that targets the groin with something equally shocking. Its amateurishness is the point: it’s what terrified, untrained people would do when left with no other solution. Li Huowang’s disgust and suspicion come from recognizing that this “system” looks too much like a game designed to waste time—or to keep someone occupied while the real threat creeps closer.

**The “Retracted Yang” Motif:** The affliction—a folk name for a perceived shrinking of the genitals—is a real (though rare) culture-bound syndrome known in parts of Asia (also called *Koro*). In the Dao-Twisted World, it’s literalized into an attack by an invisible ghost. The horror here is not just the physical threat, but the emasculating shame of it. The ritual’s focus on the crotch is a panicked, humiliating defense against a hyper-specific fear.

## Reading Guide

Get ready for a chapter that feels like *The Sorrow and the Pity* meets *The Holy Mountain*—absurd, desperate, and crawling with suspicion. Li Huowang’s impersonation of a Monitory Bureau agent is a masterclass in street-smart pragmatism: he sees a power vacuum and fills it with a lie. But the real treat here is the tonal whiplash. After chapters of serious, flesh-rending horror, we’re suddenly dropped into a scene of grown men acting like terrified, chanting turtles, slapping chili on themselves while beating gongs. It’s deliberately funny, but the laughter dies in your throat because you *know* something is wrong.

Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s final thought: *“It feels a bit too fake.”* This is a man who has learned that in the Dao-Twisted World, the weirdest, most absurd occurrences are often the most dangerous traps. The question he’s asking is whether the town’s panic is real or whether someone—or something—is staging a performance just for him. The “familiar, revolting feeling” he mentions is a huge red flag. Could this be the work of the Zuowandao again? Or has the ghost itself learned to weaponize absurdity as camouflage?

This chapter is a masterful pivot from brute-force horror to psychological thriller. The threat isn’t a monster you can stab; it’s a system of fear so convincing that even the people running it believe their own farce. Follow Li Huowang’s gut: if it feels like a joke, find the punchline before it finds you.
