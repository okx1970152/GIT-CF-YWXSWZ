## Chapter Overview
Get ready for a gut-punch, fellow Daoists. After a brief respite of cake-sharing and banter, Li Huowang returns to the one place he dared to call safe—Anci Nunnery—only to find it hollowed out. The stench is gone. The nuns are dead. And Abbess Jingxin, that filthy, greedy, surprisingly kind old glutton, is lying half-eaten in her own pigpen. But worse than the massacre is the aftermath: nothing rots. No flies. No smell. The world itself has broken a fundamental rule, and our one-armed Heart-Element has just been dealt a loss that might finally push him over the edge.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang climbs Mount Heng carrying pastries for Abbess Jingxin, deliberately keeping the box away from his sweat—a small, mundane act of consideration that shows how much he’s come to trust her.
- He eats half the mung-bean cakes without guilt, reflecting on how Jingxin is one of the few people in this world who was kind to him without wanting to harvest his organs.
- He checks the *True Sutra of the Fire Vestments* translation and discovers he can no longer read a single character—a terrifying sign that Danyangzi’s possession is accelerating.
- He arrives at the nunnery to find it dark and silent; the thick, familiar stench is gone.
- He discovers Abbess Jingxin dead in the pigpen, being eaten by her own pigs. He slaughters the pigs and cradles her half-eaten face.
- He searches the nunnery frantically but finds only more dead nuns. No enemies.
- After digging a grave all night and into the next afternoon, he notices the corpse hasn’t decayed. Nor have the pigs he killed.
- He realizes with dawning horror that “decay” itself has ceased to function in this world—suggesting the massacre was an act of supernatural erasure, not a fight.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
### The Symbolism of Rot in Chinese Cosmology
In traditional Chinese thought, decay (腐, *fu*) is not just biological—it’s a natural, necessary part of the cosmic cycle. The qi of all things waxes and wanes, and decomposition returns matter to the earth to be reborn. When rot ceases, it signals a violation of the Dao itself. In *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, this cessation of decay is a chilling environmental tell: the killer(s) didn’t just murder people; they erased the world’s ability to process death. This ties back to the Fusheng Heaven / Siming theme of “world-rules being corrupted from above.”

### “Anci Nunnery” (安慈庵) as a Name
The characters *An* (安, peace) and *Ci* (慈, compassion/mercy) are deeply ironic here. The nunnery was never truly peaceful—it was filthy, corrupt, and its abbess was a gluttonous degenerate. But even so, it was the closest thing to “home” Li Huowang had. The name suggests the façade of Buddhist mercy, and its destruction reinforces the novel’s recurring idea: no institution in the Dao-Twisted World is truly sacred.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a quiet, devastating horror set-piece. There’s no grand battle. No villain monologue. Just a one-armed man climbing a mountain with pastries, and the shattered aftermath of a kindness he took for granted. The true horror here isn’t the graphic image of the pigs eating the nun—it’s the *stillness*. The absence of rot. The world has become a mausoleum where even the flies refuse to come. If you’ve been following Li Huowang’s journey, this is the chapter where he learns that safety was always an illusion. And the scariest part? He doesn’t even know who to blame. Keep your eyes on the bigger picture: something is systematically erasing the rules of reality, one corpse at a time.
