## Chapter Overview

Our boy Li Huowang is trying his best to live the remote-village-farmer life—no assignments, no drama, just peace and quiet. But the Dao-Twisted World has other plans. In walks Liu Zongyuan, a gregarious and seemingly friendly colleague from the Supervisory Heavenly Office, bearing news from the capital that hits harder than a sucker punch. The Zuowandao and the imperial court went at it tooth and nail for three days straight, leaving the Liang Emperor on death’s door and even one of the legendary Dice dead. On the surface, this is just office gossip over wine, but underneath, it’s a nervous dance of political survival. Li Huowang, ever the pragmatist, decides to cultivate this connection—because having a friend inside a powerful institution is never a bad bet in a world that changes its mind as often as the gods change their faces. Meanwhile, Bai Lingmiao is wrestling with a secret of her own: the quiet decay of her vision and a fate she’s been told she can’t escape.

## Key Plot Points

- Liu Zongyuan, a member of the Supervisory Heavenly Office’s Lunar Gate sect, visits Li Huowang’s village and immediately signals that he’s there to be useful. He formally grants the village to Li Huowang as rightful spoils from the White Lotus massacre.
- Liu delivers shocking capital gossip: the Liang Emperor is mortally wounded by the Zuowandao. The Office is unsettled. One of the Zuowandao’s highest-ranking members, a ‘Dice,’ has been killed, but the emperor himself is on his last legs.
- Li Huowang puts on a performance of disinterest in high politics, but his real instinct is tactical. He gauges Liu Zongyuan’s value and decides to wine and dine him, forging a personal bond for future leverage.
- Liu reveals that his source is credible—he’s from the Lunar Gate, known for intelligence. He gifts Li Huowang a set of moon-phase bamboo slips for contacting carrier pigeons, showing the depth of the connection being built.
- After Liu leaves, Li Huowang reflects on his place in the world. He doesn’t care about the emperor’s death. He doesn’t care about immortality. His only priority is the safety of his small patch of land and the people he cares about.
- Bai Lingmiao, watching from hiding, visibly relaxes that Li Huowang handled the visit. But her attention turns inward: she’s losing her eyesight. Her condition—being a White-Haired Baleful Star—is slowly taking her vision, a curse her parents could never break. She keeps it from Li Huowang for now.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **Lunar Gate sect (月亮门)**: In jianghu and bureaucratic lore, being “from the Moon” often implies a specialization in intelligence, shadow operations, or information networks. This isn’t a combat school—it’s a spy network. When Liu Zongyuan boasts that no one in the Jiangnan Circuit has better information, he’s stating his professional bona fides.
- **Zhuangyuan Hong (状元红)**: A famous traditional Chinese wine, often aged for years. Its name, “Number-One Scholar Red,” is a celebratory title tied to the imperial examination’s top graduate. Offering it to a guest is a gesture of high respect.
- **Ten Emotions and Eight Sufferings (十情八苦)**: This concept extends beyond standard Buddhist suffering. It’s a twisted, granular taxonomy of human pain used by the White Lotus tradition and possibly by supernatural observers as a “readout” of a person’s hidden state. That Li Huowang can see it means he’s reading people on a level beyond normal intuition—a reminder that this novel treats emotions and karma as visible phenomena.
- **Waist token ranks (腰牌身份降级)**: Within the Supervisory Heavenly Office, status isn’t just a title—it’s tied to mission completion. Letting the token go cold means losing rank, access, and possibly protection. Li Huowang’s willingness to let his rank slip shows how far he’s willing to go to avoid entanglement.

### Private slave (私奴)

A bonded laborer in historical Chinese society, tied to a household or land. Not equivalent to chattel slavery; servants had some legal protections and could gain freedom. The term reflects rigid class hierarchy without implying total dehumanization.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in low-stakes political maneuvering in the Dao-Twisted World. There’s no screaming, no tentacles, no blood-splattered rituals—just two men sharing wine and feeling each other out. And that’s precisely the kind of horror this novel does best: the quiet kind, where a friendly visit carries the weight of a knife pressed against your ribs. Liu Zongyuan is a fascinating new addition—a genuinely chatty, useful ally who might also be a leaky pipe or a double agent. The way Li Huowang plays him, offering just enough hospitality to secure the relationship without overcommitting, shows how far our protagonist has come from the panicked, desperate boy of earlier chapters. And then there’s Bai Lingmiao’s secret—a slow-burn tragedy that sits in the background, invisible but corrosive. This isn’t just setup for a new arc; it’s a reminder that even when the world isn’t collapsing, the people inside it are still dying, one quiet day at a time. Fellow Daoists, the calm before the next storm is here—and it smells like brandy and lies.

## The Slow Erosion of a Quiet Life

This chapter also gives us one of the most grounded emotional beats in recent memory: Bai Lingmiao’s quiet confession about her fading sight. She knows she’s a “White-Haired Baleful Star.” She knows she’ll go blind. She knows her lifespan is short. And she knows her parents tried everything and failed. There’s no drama here—just the quiet, soul-level acceptance of a young woman who has long since made peace with the idea that she was born “unlucky.” Her decision to keep this from Li Huowang isn’t malice; it’s a twisted form of love, protecting him from yet another burden. It’s moments like these that remind us why this novel isn’t just a horror show—it’s a story about people carrying unspeakable weights in a universe that doesn’t care.
