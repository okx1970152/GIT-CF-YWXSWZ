## Chapter Overview

Get ready, fellow Daoists, because Li Huowang just walked into the biggest, most horrifying trap in Xijing, and it’s not the one he was expecting. After surviving Danyangzi’s alchemical nightmare, he thought he’d found a safe harbor in the pious-looking Zhengde Temple. *Nope.* This chapter is a brutal rug-pull that reveals the Dao-Twisted World’s ultimate truth: there is no “good” side. The monks aren’t righteous warriors against evil; they’re just a different flavor of flesh-worshipping horror. Li Huowang *sees* their true, grotesque Buddha—a pulsating, parasitic mountain of organs—and instantly knows he’s stumbled into another den of monsters. The tension is a masterclass in panic: a lone man realizing he’s surrounded, outnumbered, and completely out of options, with only his wits and a terrible secret to cling to.

## Key Plot Points

- The chapter opens with Li Huowang’s vision of the “Buddha” shattering: what is a sacred, stone statue to the pious monks is, to him, a twenty-meter-tall mound of pink, writhing flesh covered in malformed reproductive organs and plasma. The monks are physically merging with it, feeding on its pink mist.
- Li Huowang’s worldview collapses. He finally understands that the conflict between Danyangzi and Zhengde Temple isn’t a holy war; it’s a gang war between two equally monstrous factions.
- The old beggar-monk, completely blind to the horror, tries to compliment the “Buddha’s” size, providing a terrifyingly mundane counterpoint to Li Huowang’s existential dread.
- Li Huowang attempts a quiet escape, pretending nothing is wrong, but his cover is blown by the towering monk Jian Wei, who uses a black, sucker-covered tentacle from his own tonsure scar to “sense” Li Huowang’s impure heart.
- A fight breaks out. Li Huowang slashes the tentacle, swallows a pill, and makes a desperate run for the temple gates, triggering a full-scale chase involving the rolling, fleshy “Buddha” itself.
- Just before the exit, Li Huowang is intercepted not by a monster, but by Abbot Xin Hui himself, who calmly returns his lost Daoist bell, calling it “inauspicious.”
- In a tense standoff, Li Huowang decides to lay his cards on the table, explaining what he saw and offering a truce. The chapter ends on a knife’s edge as Xin Hui, for the first time, shows genuine anger, calling Li Huowang’s testimony “preposterous.”

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **The Flesh Buddha (肉佛):** This is not a monster from nowhere. In the Dao-Twisted World, “becoming a Buddha” is a literal, horrific process. The monks are not worshipping an idol; they are merging their own flesh with a parasitic entity that has been “cultivated” over generations. This is a perversion of Buddhist cultivation, where enlightenment (*bodhi*) is replaced with corporeal fusion. The pink flesh and organs are a direct mockery of the “pure” and “detached” body a Buddha is supposed to have.
- **The Vertical Eye (竖眼):** The black tentacle emerging from Jian Wei’s tonsure scar is a type of “Vertical Eye” (竖眼), a metaphysical and often physical organ associated with forbidden sight and hidden knowledge. In many Chinese folk and Buddhist traditions, the “third eye” or “Buddha-eye” represents wisdom and insight. Here, it is literally a writhing, parasitic tentacle, symbolizing that the temple’s “insight” is a corrupt, predatory sense.
- **The Illusion Barrier:** It is crucial to understand that the old monk and presumably most of the temple’s lay followers do not see the Flesh Buddha. They see a grand, stone statue. The monks’ power is not just physical; it is a psychological and perceptual field that redefines reality for those within it. Li Huowang’s ability to see through it is a direct consequence of his nature as a Heart-Element (心素)—he is already a crack in reality, and these lesser illusions cannot hold him.
- **Eyes as Signature:** The constant emphasis on eyes in this novel—the vertical eye, the staring monk, the “thousand-eyed” bodies—is a signature motif. In the Dao-Twisted World, to “see” is to be complicit, to be a witness, and often to be a target. Li Huowang’s sight is his curse and his only weapon.

## Reading Guide

Alright, let’s talk about the *real* horror of this chapter. It’s not the tentacles, the pus, or even the giant rolling meat mountain. It’s the sheer *normalcy* of the situation for everyone else. The old monk is chirping about fried tofu while Li Huowang is staring at a nightmare. This is the core of cosmic horror: being the only sane person in a world that has collectively agreed to hallucinate something sweet while it eats them alive.

Notice how Abbot Xin Hui reacts. He doesn’t attack immediately. He *listens*. He looks confused. This is a brilliant character moment. Is he genuinely ignorant of his own temple’s depravity? Or is he trying to gaslight Li Huowang, to make him doubt the evidence of his own senses? The chapter ends on an open question, and that ambiguity is far more terrifying than a simple monster battle. The best horror asks, “Is the monster lying, or am I the one who’s crazy?” *Spoiler alert: in this book, the answer is usually both.*

Also, pay close attention to Li Huowang’s growth here. In the early chapters with Danyangzi, he was a victim. Here, he’s thinking strategically. His first instinct isn’t blind rage; it’s to assess the situation and negotiate a way out. He’s learning the rules of this world, even if those rules are written in blood and screaming.
