## Chapter Overview

Whoa. Buckle up, fellow Daoists, because Chapter 388 is a brutal, heart-wrenching ride right through the shredder of Li Huowang’s fractured psyche. After escaping the belly of a monster in the Dao-Twisted World, our boy doesn’t surface in safety—he surfaces in the White Tower prison, still trapped in the hallucination, still desperately swimming against a tide of reality he can’t trust. This chapter is all about that agonizing gap between what he perceives and what’s really happening. The raw, physical horror of a man crawling over live electric razor wire is just the setup for the real punch: the unbearable moment when Yang Na’s face emerges from the crowd, and Li Huowang must choose between slipping deeper into his delusion or letting her see the broken man he’s become. It’s a chapter that weaponizes love itself as a form of pain, and it hits like a freight train.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang escapes the creature’s stomach but immediately interprets his new surroundings (the prison yard) through a hallucinatory lens, believing he is still underwater and must “swim” back to a boat he perceives as the steel watchtower.
- He flees from pursuing guards and scales a chain-link fence topped with high-voltage razor wire, severely burning and cutting himself—his pain tolerance and desperate drive mark him as utterly inhuman to the onlookers.
- After climbing onto the watchtower platform, he stands down but is quickly surrounded by guards. Just as he prepares to gouge his own eye out as a last-resort tactic, Yang Na’s voice stops him.
- Yang Na, looking haggard and heartbroken, confirms that he is lucid. Li Huowang recognizes her but immediately pivots to a manic performance, pretending to be Sun Wukong to avoid the unbearable weight of facing her love and pain directly.
- The chapter concludes with a devastatingly quiet moment: Li Huowang, battered and pinned to the ground, offers Yang Na a tiny wildflower from his shredded hand and whispers an apology before being subdued.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Sun Wukong (孙悟空) – The Great Sage Equal to Heaven:** Li Huowang’s desperate cry of “I am Sun Wukong!” is a *lot* more layered than a random outburst. Sun Wukong is the central hero of *Journey to the West*, a classic of Chinese literature. He’s a stone-born monkey with immense magical power, who once rebelled against heaven itself before being tamed. For Chinese readers, Li Huowang claiming to be him is a tragic irony: he is not the unshakeable, immortal trickster but a human being suffocating under his own suffering. He chooses the loudest, most “crazy” identity possible precisely because it allows him to hide the one thing he can’t admit: that he is still capable of loving someone and hurting them.

**The Tiny Wildflower & Unspoken Apology:** This might seem like a simple sentimental gesture, but in the context of Chinese storytelling, giving something small and fragile from a place of extreme pain is a potent symbol. It is not a grand romantic gesture; it is an offering of a part of himself that remains uncorrupted—small, fleeting, but real. The apology is the emotional core, delivered not to the crowds or the guards, but in that single, exhausted whisper when he thinks the world can’t hear him.

### Face-Changing (变脸)

A traditional Sichuan opera technique where performers rapidly switch between painted masks; here, Li Huowang’s rapid shift between identities functions as a psychological form of face-changing.

### Zhong Kui (钟馗)

A Chinese folk deity and demon queller who, after being denied a government post due to his ugly appearance, died in shame and was appointed as the Demon Queller. His story mirrors Li Huowang’s tragic transformation.

## Reading Guide

Alright, let’s talk about the emotional knife-twist at the end. Li Huowang has been through horrors that would shatter most minds. But here, in this drab prison yard, he shows us his deepest wound is not physical—it’s the look in Yang Na’s eyes. His fake Sun Wukong act isn't just for the guards; it's a shield to keep *her* from seeing the person he's become. The irony is devastating: the only way he can protect her is to act like a lunatic so she finally gives up.

The real kicker? That tiny apology at the very end. “I’m sorry for everything before.” It’s not an apology for one thing; it’s an apology for the whole tangled, cruel mess of his existence—for the hallucinations, for the pain, for the fact that even when he recognizes her, he can’t stay with her without breaking both of them. This chapter doesn't advance a plot; it torque-wrenches a character’s soul until you can hear it creak. Get your tissues ready. This one hurts.

## Zhong Kui Mythology & Madness

Deep-dive time! Li Huowang's situation here resonates with an older, darker folk figure: **Zhong Kui (钟馗)**. In Chinese mythology, Zhong Kui was a brilliant scholar who, due to his ugly appearance, was unjustly denied a top ranking in the imperial exams. In shame and rage, he dashed his head against the palace steps and died. After death, he was appointed as the **“Demon Queller”**—a furious spirit who hunts and devours evil entities, but is forever locked between worlds. Does that sound familiar? Like Li Huowang, Zhong Kui is a figure whose suffering and injustice transforms him into something terrifying *and* righteous, but the transformation costs him his place among the living. Li Huowang’s own “demon hunting” has gotten him hailed as a hero, but it has also made him a monster in his own prison—a Zhong Kui for the modern age, but one who can’t escape his own hell to find peace.

## The Performance of Madness

Let’s talk about Li Huowang’s weaponized delusion. When Yang Na sees through him, he immediately *escalates* the crazy. He doesn’t break down; he *performs*. This is a survival technique we've seen before, but here it’s used as a kind of emotional armor. By becoming Sun Wukong, he doesn’t have to be Li Huowang—the man who owes apologies he can’t begin to make. The masks he wears aren’t just symptoms; they are a conscious retreat from a reality that demands he look into Yang Na’s eyes and see the wreckage he’s made of her life. This is a masterclass in “willful delusion” as a defense mechanism, and it shows just how far Li Huowang has come in mastering his own fractured mind as a tool, even if it means breaking her heart to keep her safe.
