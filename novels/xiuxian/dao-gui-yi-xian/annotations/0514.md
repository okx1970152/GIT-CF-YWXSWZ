## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this chapter is a deep intel drop. Our boy Li Huowang finally gets his hands on the Supervisory Heavenly Office’s after-action report on the Zuowandao purge, and the numbers are *sweet*. But that cold satisfaction is immediately undercut by the same old problem: he still has no idea where Dice is hiding Bai Lingmiao. The chapter is a masterclass in tactical rumination, as Li weighs his resources, his enemies, and the impossible geometry of his situation—all while his own hallucinations mock him to his face.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang reviews the Office’s Zuowandao kill records and learns the purge has been devastating: the Three Yuans and Four Joys are gutted, all four North Winds are dead, and Dice has lost multiple identities.
- He confirms the Office does not have a dedicated wanted poster for Dice, as the Sitian Jian is handling him personally.
- A moment of terror hits when he wonders if the Great Qi Zuowandao have joined forces to ambush the Sitian Jian, but Zhuge Yuan calms him: they are not Heart-Pans and cannot stay in Great Liang indefinitely.
- Nangong delivers a chilling warning: the Office absolutely forbids involvement in the imperial succession. If Li Huowang meddles, he is on his own—no backup, no body retrieval, no burial.
- Alone in his courtyard, Li reassesses his situation. His strength is insufficient. He decides to keep up his cultivation and continue following Dice’s plan outwardly while secretly gathering resources for a counter-strike.
- He hallucinates a mocking Zuowandao voice, punches it, and is immediately laughed at by the monk and Sui, who reveals the illusion is both “alive” and “dead” depending on where you stand.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter offers a rare peek into the **Supervisory Heavenly Office**’s internal operations and its relationship with imperial power. The Office’s stance—absolute non-interference in the succession war—reflects a recurring trope in Chinese supernatural bureaucracy: the agency tasked with policing the uncanny operates under a strict mandate that separates it from mundane politics. The comparison to *gu* (蛊) insect cultivation is deliberate: the Emperor is the strongest worm, and the Office only cares which one emerges from the jar.

The **Zuowandao ranking system** based on mahjong tiles (Three Yuans, Four Joys, Winds, Red Centers) also gets a bit more texture here. Seeing Dice’s faction listed as critically depleted—four North Winds dead, three East Winds, a White Board—gives the hierarchy real stakes. These aren’t just titles; each one is a combat identity.

Finally, Li Huowang’s dedication to “seeking truth” (修真, *xiu zhen*) is explicitly framed as both a path to power and a tactical necessity. In the Dao-Twisted World, this is never a peaceful meditation—it is a grinding, paranoid process of reinforcing one’s own reality against constant erosion. And the chapter’s final beat—where Sui’s tentacle phases through the hallucination the way the hallucination’s *own* punch just worked—is a subtle visual confirmation of the novel’s core rule: what is “real” is determined by who is believing and acting.

### Xiu Zhen

Literally “cultivating truth”; the standard xianxia term for Daoist cultivation, which in the Dao-Twisted World becomes a paranoid, defensive process of maintaining one’s own reality against constant erosion.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is all **atmosphere and logistics**—no big fights, no new monsters, just Li Huowang hunched over a desk doing math on his enemies and feeling out the political terrain. But that atmosphere is *tense*. Nangong’s warning about the Office’s hands-off policy during the succession crisis adds a new layer of danger: Li Huowang is now operating in a city where everyone who can officially protect him has declared he’s on his own. Watch for how this isolation pushes him deeper into his own head—and deeper into the embrace of the very hallucinations he hates. The punch at the end? That’s not just rage. That’s him fighting the only enemy he can actually hit.
