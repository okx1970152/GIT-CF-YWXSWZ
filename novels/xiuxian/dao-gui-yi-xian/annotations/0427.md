## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang gets his meeting with Zhuge Yuan, and it’s a breath of fresh, cynical air. He confirms the trap is set for the Zuowandao, receives a nifty identity-altering calligraphy trick to shake off Office surveillance, and then—finally—lets out the question that’s been eating him alive: “Is the other world real?” Zhuge Yuan doesn’t give him a neat answer. He gives him a mirror. In a chapter heavy with tea, ink, and quiet melancholy, two men bound by impossible dualities remind each other they’re not walking the road alone.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang meets Nan Gongluo outside the Liang residence; learns that most Supervisory Heavenly Office operatives are tied up at the palace due to an imperial decree, explaining the light presence in the capital.
- Nan advises Li not to move against Zhuge Yuan now—tangling with the storyteller while the Zuowandao lurks is a suicidal pincer risk.
- Li Huowang enters Zhuge Yuan’s residence; doors and windows slam shut as a deliberate, controlled gesture.
- Li reports that the plan to pit the Office against the Zuowandao is underway; Zhuge confirms the entire Zuowandao sect is gathered in the capital and won’t leave without causing a major spectacle.
- Zhuge Yuan uses his calligraphy-based sorcery to alter the characters of Li Huowang’s name on his palm, turning “Li Huowang” into “Ji Zai”—a perfect identity mask as long as he covers his face.
- Li Huowang, with visible hesitation, asks Zhuge Yuan to act as a friend rather than an ally and help him judge a dilemma: whether his “other world” might actually be real.
- Zhuge Yuan shares his own pain: a beloved woman from Great Liang became a mere concubine in Great Qi. He tried to force himself into believing Great Qi was the false world—but reality wouldn’t bend.
- Invoking the “fish and bear’s paw” paradox, Zhuge suggests that holding onto both truths is impossible for anyone, from commoners to Siming above.
- Li Huowang leaves, emotionally lighter from the confession, and the chapter ends on a warm, wry note—Zhuge declining future chess games over tea with affectionate mockery.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Calligraphy Sorcery & Name Alteration**: This is a wonderfully poetic use of the traditional Chinese belief that a written name carries the essence of a person. The Chinese writing system is logographic, meaning each character is a concept, not just a sound. By physically modifying the strokes of “Li Huowang” on his palm—adding a horizontal stroke to turn “Li” into “Ji,” capping “Huo” to form “Zai,” and erasing “Wang” entirely—Zhuge performs a literal act of identity rewriting. This taps into deep folk magic where one’s name holds binding power (e.g., avoiding naming spirits to prevent control). Here, it’s used as benign camouflage: a masterstroke of calligraphic concealment.

**The Fish and Bear’s Paw**: This is a classical Chinese reference from *Mencius*, where the philosopher uses a choice between two delicacies to illustrate that when faced with irreconcilable options—like life and righteousness—one must choose the nobler path. Zhuge Yuan deliberately subverts this original intent. Instead of using it to advocate for a moral choice, he applies it to a metaphysical impossibility: you cannot have both worlds be equally real. This reframes the ancient ethical dilemma into an existential horror—and a strangely comforting piece of companionship.

**The Confucian Art of the Friend**: In traditional Chinese social ethics, friends (朋友) were one of the “Five Cardinal Relationships.” A true friend was someone you could ask this of: not for an answer, but for a sympathetic ear. Li Huowang’s request that Zhuge judge his situation as a “friend, not an ally” is an explicitly Confucian gesture—a plea for the specific relationship of righteous mutual regard, not just a tactical partnership. The chapter title “Friend” is no accident; it marks one of the most grounded emotional connections in the entire Dao-Twisted World.

### Calligraphy concealment

A supernatural technique where altering the written strokes of a person’s name magically obscures their identity. Rooted in the folk belief that written characters hold binding power over the entities they name.

## Reading Guide

Get your tissues ready, but maybe not for tears. This is the chapter where Li Huowang finally stops scheming long enough to *feel*. The quiet tension in the calligraphy scene—brush strokes that rewrite a man’s name as casually as signing a letter—contrasts beautifully with the raw confession that follows. Zhuge Yuan doesn’t offer solutions. He offers *recognition*. When he talks about his Liang lover being downgraded to a concubine in another world, you realize: Zhuge isn’t just a plot-exposition device. He’s a man who has already lost the same war Li Huowang is still fighting.

Also, the bit about “calling you a lousy player would be an insult” is the kind of gentle, self-aware humor that makes this friendship feel real. Two broken men, sharing tea and ink, briefly forgetting they live in a world of gutted temples and laughter-hunting gods. Savor it.
