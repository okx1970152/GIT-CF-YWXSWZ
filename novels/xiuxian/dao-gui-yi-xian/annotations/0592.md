## Chapter Overview

The fragile peace Li Huowang has carved out in the Imperial Palace of Great Liang is already fraying at the edges. What begins as a seemingly relaxed stroll through the winter gardens with his companions quickly turns ominous when Li Huowang abruptly senses something wrong. His casual dismissal of the incident does not fool the perceptive Bai Lingmiao, and his subsequent private audience with the Chief Astrologer confirms that a new threat is lurking in the shadows. However, the Chief Astrologer refuses to share the details, citing a deep distrust of the Siming that stands behind Li Huowang. Forced to act on his own, Li Huowang resolves to hunt down this hidden problem before it can catch him off guard.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang interrupts a cheerful garden tour by staring intently at the sky, causing momentary panic among his companions. He quickly soothes them with a lie about the weather, but the tension lingers beneath the surface.
- The group accepts Li Huowang’s new lotus-root head with surprising ease, having seen far stranger things during their journey. Gouwa’s lighthearted banter about the Lü Family Troupe’s new opera house and their “spring operas” provides a brief return to levity.
- After everyone leaves, Bai Lingmiao pushes Li Huowang back to his room. Though blind, her sharp perception senses that Li Huowang was not simply admiring the sky. He asks her to summon the Chief Astrologer, revealing that he is already planning his next move.
- During his private meeting with the Chief Astrologer, Li Huowang directly asks if there is unfinished business from their previous campaign. The Chief Astrologer confirms the existence of a loose end but insists it is a minor matter for the Supervisory Heavenly Office to handle.
- When Li Huowang presses for details, the Chief Astrologer refuses, citing a fundamental distrust of the Siming that now has a stake in Li Huowang’s future. He warns that Li Huowang is “no longer just himself,” implying that the line between man and vessel has become dangerously blurred.
- Li Huowang accepts the standoff with cold pragmatism. Realizing that he cannot rely on the Chief Astrologer, he decides to find his own solution before the hidden trouble can escalate.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**“Spring Opera” (粉戏)** is a historical slang term for erotic or bawdy folk opera performances. In the context of this chapter, Gouwa’s enthusiasm for the Lü Family Troupe’s new theater being used for such shows is a darkly humorous contrast to the atmosphere of courtly elegance. It also subtly underscores how the Lü Family’s hard-won status does not automatically erase their roots in the vulgar, visceral world of traveling performers.

The **Chief Astrologer (司天监, Sitian Jian)** is a real historical title within the Ming and Qing imperial bureaucracy, referring to the head of the Astronomical Bureau. In the Dao-Twisted World, this title is repurposed to describe a figure who oversees the celestial and supernatural realms on behalf of the empire. His role is not merely to read the stars, but to monitor the boundary between the mortal world and the Siming—hence the office’s name, which puns on “overseeing the heavens” and “keeping watch over the celestial.”

The **Dragon Veins (龙脉)** are a foundational concept in Chinese geomancy and political theology. The flow of underground qi through the land’s “dragon veins” was believed to determine the fate and legitimacy of a dynasty. In this novel, the concept is made literal and visceral: the Mandate of Heaven is a stolen fragment of divine power that holds up reality, and those who support it must pay a terrible, ongoing price. The Chief Astrologer’s refusal to discuss the “minor matter” hints that it may threaten the very fabric of Great Liang’s existence.

### Mandate of Heaven (气运)

In this novel’s context, the Mandate is a stolen fragment of divine power that holds up reality; it can only be supported by a family willing to commit self-slaughter, making it a curse as much as a blessing.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a classic example of “the calm before the storm.” The surface-level interactions—Gouwa’s jokes, Li Sui’s childish clinginess, and the group’s delight in winter vegetables—are all designed to lull both the characters and the reader into a false sense of domestic comfort. Li Huowang’s sudden shift in demeanor is the first crack in that facade, and his private meeting with the Chief Astrologer widens it into a chasm of distrust.

Pay close attention to the Chief Astrologer’s reasoning. He does not doubt Li Huowang’s intentions *now*, but he explicitly fears the “one behind” Li Huowang—the Siming who is now entangled with his existence. This is a crucial expansion of the novel’s core horror: the problem is not simply that Li Huowang is fragile, but that his future self may be a weapon in the hands of a power that sees mortals as expendable tools.

Li Huowang’s final decision to “solve the problem himself” marks a return to his most pragmatic, predatory mindset. Having lost the unity of purpose he shared with the Chief Astrologer against The Dice, he must now operate as a lone agent in a political landscape that treats him as both a hero and a liability. His line, “If trouble has already found me, I have no intention of sitting around and waiting for it,” is the quiet, steel-edged promise of a man who has learned that trust is a currency that devalues the moment you stop paying it.
