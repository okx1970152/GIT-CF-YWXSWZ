## Chapter Overview
Oh boy. Li Huowang just got a fresh dose of existential horror, and this time it's *personal*. After Bai Lingmiao and everyone else from Qingfeng Temple flat-out deny ever seeing the jade pendant he *knows* he gave her, Li Huowang's mental gears start grinding. This isn't a simple trick or a bad memory. He tests his theory by calling in Liu Zongyuan, an informant from the Moon Gate, and asks about a major historical event he *witnessed firsthand*. The answer he gets proves his worst fear: the True-and-False Heavenly Law doesn't just change the present—it retroactively rewrites history. The past isn't fixed. It's a slab of wet clay being reshaped by every Heavenly Calamity. And that leads our poor, broken Daoist to a question so terrifying he can barely whisper it: what if the Great Qi that Zhuge Yuan gave his life to save… was just one of those erased histories?

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang asks Bai Lingmiao about the jade pendant he gave her from Qingfeng Temple. She has no memory of it.
- He calls together everyone from Qingfeng Temple; none of them remember the jade pendant either.
- Suspecting something far deeper than a trick, Li Huowang contacts Liu Zongyuan of the Moon Gate to verify a major past event.
- Liu Zongyuan recounts the "Eye of the Mountain God" incident in the capital. His version is completely different from what Li Huowang experienced.
- Li Huowang realizes that when the True-and-False Heavenly Law was taken from Doumu, it didn't just vanish—it retroactively changed all history associated with her.
- He extrapolates: if *every* Heavenly Calamity causes such retroactive changes, then the "fixed past" he remembers is an illusion.
- The chapter climaxes with Li Huowang staring at Zhuge Yuan's head on the Spine Sword and whispering the horrifying question: could the Great Qi he sacrificed everything for be nothing more than a timeline that got deleted?

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter is a masterclass in ontological horror—the fear of *reality itself* being unreliable. Let's break down the key concepts:

**The True-and-False Heavenly Law (真假天道):** This is not just magic that lies. It is the *authority over what is and is not true*. Think of it less like a spell and more like executive access to reality's file system. The possessor can declare something "false" and it ceases to have ever existed—not just forgotten, but retroactively deleted from cause and effect. It's the ultimate weapon and the ultimate nightmare, because if the law can be stolen and reassigned, so can your entire history.

**Retroactive History:** This is the core horror here. Traditional xianxia often has "history" as a fixed record, even if obscured. *Dao Gui Yi Xian* takes the Buddhist concept of impermanence and weaponizes it. The past is not a treasure to be uncovered; it's a hostage that can be executed at any moment. This isn't just "memory" being altered—it's the *causal chain* of events being rewritten, leaving only Li Huowang's (and the reader's) scarred memory as the last witness to a ghost timeline.

**The Sitian Jian Edict:** Li Huowang's mind jumps to the edict he saw: "By imperial decree, the Sitian Jian alone shall print and sell almanacs." Almanacs (历书) are sacred in Chinese folk culture. They dictate auspicious days for everything from marriage to burial. Control over the almanac is control over the *rhythm of society*. The edict is a chilling reminder that the state (via the Sitian Jian) is already in the business of publishing what is "true" about time itself.

## Reading Guide
Alright, fellow Daoists, take a deep breath. This chapter is a gut punch. It takes the philosophical question "what is real?" and turns it into a *functioning weapon* in the setting. Here's what to watch for:

*   **Pay attention to the logic, not the paranoia.** Li Huowang isn't jumping at shadows. He's conducting a forensic investigation of *reality*. He tests A (memory of pendant), then tests B (memory of a major event), and the results are consistent. His deduction is *rational*. That's what makes it so terrifying—the world is actually, verifiably broken.
*   **"The Dao-Twisted World is a palimpsest."** Imagine a parchment that has been written on, erased, and written over a thousand times. The faint traces of older texts are still there, but the current text claims to be the only version. Li Huowang is the only one who can see the faint, bleeding ghost of the old words.
*   **The Zhuge Yuan question is the core of the tragedy.** Zhuge Yuan's entire arc was about sacrifice, loyalty, and saving a doomed dynasty. His death was heartbreaking. But now Li Huowang is forced to consider a possibility worse than death: that his sacrifice was not just in vain, but that it was made for a *phantom*. A history that has already been erased by a cosmic edit. This isn't just sad; it's nihilism wearing a human face. Get ready for some serious emotional fallout.
*   **Meta-narrative alert:** This is the author pulling the rug out from under the *entire story so far*. It retroactively undermines everything the characters have bled for. That's the point. This novel is about the horror of a universe where "winning" doesn't mean a happy ending; it means surviving long enough to realize there was never a "real" ending to begin with.
