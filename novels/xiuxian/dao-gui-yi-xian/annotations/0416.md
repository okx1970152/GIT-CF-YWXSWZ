## Chapter Overview

For one tense chapter, *The Son* sits at the intersection of three power plays—Li Huowang’s reluctant double agency for the Supervisory Heavenly Office, Great Qi’s court purge scheming, and the Zuowandao’s inevitable third strike. The story holds its breath. Every conversation hums with conspiracy, every glance carries weight. The real horror, though, arrives in the final scene: a palace eunuch, visibly pregnant, proudly displaying his “son” as proof that Zhengde Temple can fix the only thing a castrated man truly fears. It’s a grotesque, body-horror punchline to a chapter built entirely on quiet dread.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang observes from the periphery as factions of Great Qi men argue over whether and how to enlist palace eunuchs in their coup plot.
- Zhuge Yuan remains pointedly silent, refusing to use his status to settle the debate; he predicts action by tomorrow and identifies the Empress Dowager—not the child emperor—as the real target.
- Li Huowang slips out of the Liang residence and meets a paper-cut agent of the Supervisory Heavenly Office. He admits he hasn’t located Zhuge Yuan’s old almanac yet and demands they wait for the next Zuowandao attack before striking.
- In a distant mansion, the Vice Commissioner calmly orders the release of two imprisoned Zuowandao, tasking them with spreading word for a coordinated strike at noon tomorrow. The Office’s target: the Six Luminaries Scripture, nothing else.
- A eunuch messenger arrives at Liang residence the next morning, declaring that his godfather—the Chief Recorder?—will not help the conspirators. The debate escalates into a confrontation.
- The eunuch reveals a grotesque pregnancy, claiming it as his “son” and proof that Zhengde Temple can restore what castration took. Qin Mengzhe denounces the monks as demons, but the eunuch doesn’t care.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Eunuch’s “Son”: A Fetal Horror Born of Folk Belief + Temple Corruption**  
The eunuch’s pregnancy is not a random shock. In premodern China, the foremost concern of court eunuchs was lineage—having a son to perform ancestor rites and inherit their accumulated status. Adoption was common, but a *biological* son was considered impossible. Zhengde Temple’s ability to make a eunuch visibly pregnant weaponizes that deep cultural anxiety: they offer the one thing no one else can, and the eunuch is willing to ally with “demonic monks” for it. The blurred handprint on the stretched belly skin is textbook body horror—a reminder that Zhengde can twist the very boundary between life and death.

**The Paper-Cut Spy: A Supernatural Communication Technique**  
Li Huowang’s conversation with a paper-cut window is a quiet showcase of the Dao-Twisted World’s unique surveillance magic. Paper-cut figures, traditionally a folk art for window decoration or funerary effigies, become real-time communication nodes for the Supervisory Heavenly Office. The fat woman crumples the used paper-cut and burns it, mimicking the disposal of a burned letter. This is espionage by folk-art magic—eerie, efficient, and deeply unsettling.

**The Three Yuans and Four Joys: Mahjong-based Inner Circles**  
The Office’s inner hierarchy is revealed to be structured around mahjong-themed ranks. The Three Yuans (三元) correspond to the three Dragon tiles—Red, Green, and White—and represent the second-highest circle. The Four Joys (四喜) are even higher, tied to the corrupted Deity of Joy. Their appearance signals that the Office is treating Zhuge Yuan not as a routine target but as a calamity-class threat.

### Three Yuans

The second-highest ranking circle within the Zuowandao, named after the three Dragon tiles in a mahjong set (Red, Green, White).

### Four Joys

The highest-ranking circle within the Zuowandao, associated with the corrupted Deity of Joy; their involvement signals a major escalation.

## Reading Guide

*The Son* is a chapter that earns its tension through implication. Nothing explodes, but you can *feel* the fuse burning. Watch how Li Huowang navigates his double role—he’s genuinely conflicted, not just playing a part. He *wants* to steal that almanac, but he also doesn’t want to get caught. Also note the Vice Commissioner's casual willingness to ally with Zuowandao. That’s not pragmatism; that’s a man who knows something the reader doesn’t. And if you’re wondering why Zhengde Temple’s pregnancy miracle feels so much more horrifying here than in earlier chapters—it’s because the victim *wants* it. That makes it infinitely worse.
