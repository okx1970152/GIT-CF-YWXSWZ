## Chapter Overview

Our boy is back to his old tricks: compartmentalizing horror like it's a day job. The dust has settled from the last catastrophe, and Li Huowang is trying to convince himself—and everyone else—that he's fine. But his body isn't cooperating. Between a floating Zuowandao hallucination that won't shut up, a monk half-stuck in a wall, and a Black Tai Sui tentacle casually poking out of his nose like it owns the place, "fine" is doing a lot of heavy lifting. Bai Lingmiao is worried sick, the Great Spirit is hiding secrets under a wedding veil, and Li Huowang's own belly is becoming a battleground. This chapter is a masterclass in atmospheric dread—no monsters attacking, just the quiet, gnawing horror of a protagonist realizing the enemy might already be inside him.

## Key Plot Points

1. **The Persistent Hallucination**: A Zuowandao (calling itself "Red Center") has taken up permanent residence in Li Huowang's field of vision, floating on Peng Longteng's shoulder and mocking him. Li Huowang resolves to treat everything it says as meaningless noise, but its mere presence is a psychological weight.

2. **The Great Spirit's Secret**: During a quiet meal, Bai Lingmiao tries to tell Li Huowang something about the immortals, but her Great Spirit (the veiled Second Spirit) stops her by physically covering her under the red veil. When the veil drops, Bai Lingmiao's expression is strained, and she refuses to say what was discussed. Li Huowang decides to ask Chun Xiaoman later.

3. **The Black Tai Sui Mutation**: Li Huowang casually pulls a slimy, writhing tentacle from his own nose—a clear sign that the Black Tai Sui inside him is changing. Bai Lingmiao begs him to get rid of it, but Li Huowang refuses without hesitation. The hallucination-suppressing benefits of the Black Tai Sui outweigh the body horror, in his cold calculation.

4. **A Failed Attempt at Communication**: After the meal, Li Huowang tries to call out to the Black Tai Sui directly, hoping to negotiate or at least understand its intentions. It doesn't respond. In a final gambit, he removes the cotton batting from his ears—letting the full sensory noise of the world flood in—an act that signals either a desperate new strategy or a resignation to madness.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Red Center (红中, Hongzhong)**: This is the name of the "Red Middle" tile from the classic Chinese tile game *Mahjong*. In the novel, it's a perfect name for a Zuowandao—a liar who wears borrowed identities like costumes. The Zuowandao's choice of this alias is a deliberate mockery: it's a name from a game, a symbol of luck and chance, which fits their philosophy of treating life as a performance to be rigged. For Western readers unfamiliar with Mahjong, think of it as the Zuowandao equivalent of a joker card wearing a tuxedo and laughing at the table.

**Black Tai Sui (黑太岁)**: We've encountered this before, but its new behavior is worth highlighting. The Black Tai Sui is no longer just a passive parasite inside Li Huowang; it's developing agency. The tentacle emerging from his nose suggests a growing symbiosis—or parasitism. In Chinese folk religion, *Tai Sui* is traditionally a star deity associated with the passage of time and annual fortune. Inverting this sacred figure into a writhing, organic entity that lives inside someone is a classic *Dao Gui Yi Xian* move: taking something holy and making it viscerally, horribly alive.

**The Great Spirit's Silence**: The Second Spirit's intervention by covering Bai Lingmiao under the bridal veil is a fascinating supernatural detail. In Chinese spirit-medium traditions, the veil is often a tool of transition—a barrier between the mortal and the spiritual. That the Great Spirit uses it to physically shield Bai Lingmiao from Li Huowang's perception hints at a power dynamic even Bai Lingmiao may not fully control. The spirits have their own agenda, and they're not shy about keeping secrets from the "master."

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a slow burn of dread disguised as a recovery scene. Pay close attention to Li Huowang's internal calculus: he's not in denial about the Black Tai Sui's danger; he's making a conscious, cold-blooded choice to keep it because the alternative (unfiltered hallucinations) is worse. That's the kind of moral calculus that makes *Dao Gui Yi Xian* so compelling—there is no clean option, only degrees of horror.

Also, keep an eye on the Zuowandao hallucination. It's not just background noise. Every word it says is a psychological pressure test, probing Li Huowang's defenses. And the final image—Li Huowang pulling the cotton from his ears, letting the world scream back in—is a powerful metaphor. He's not trying to silence his madness anymore. He's trying to listen to it. And that's either a breakthrough or a breakdown.
