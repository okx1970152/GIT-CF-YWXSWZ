## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang is so close to his destination he can almost taste it—and, predictably, the Dao-Twisted World decides that’s the perfect moment to throw another nightmare into his path. What starts as a quiet moment of envy watching a carefree child quickly devolves into a classic folk-horror setup: a ship under siege by something ancient and hungry beneath the waves, and a terrified crew ready to toss a little girl overboard to appease the “Dragon King.” The chapter is a tight, claustrophobic vignette that pits pragmatic survival horror against moral instinct, and it features a surprising new ally who isn’t afraid to call out a mob’s cowardice. Get ready for cold salt water, even colder logic, and a dive that’s equal parts bravery and stupidity.

## Key Plot Points

- The ship begins lurching violently, but the sea surface is flat—the impacts are clearly from something *underneath* striking the hull, not natural waves.
- The captain, in a blind panic, reveals the folk belief: a “Dragon King” (龙王爷爷) is attacking, and it only feeds on people with the surnames Chen or Fan. He demands the child with the surname Chen be thrown overboard as a sacrifice.
- A burly, bald passenger with a beard—the best card player from earlier—intervenes, shoving the captain aside and declaring he will fight the entity rather than let a child die.
- Li Huowang, initially planning to stay out of it, is moved by the man’s speech and decides to join the fight, revealing his skinless, monstrous appearance as he prepares to dive.
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang plunging into the sea, his skinless body screaming in agony from the salt water as he prepares to confront the “Dragon King” head-on.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Dragon King (龙王, Lóngwáng)** – In Chinese folk religion and mythology, the Dragon Kings are water deities who rule over the seas, rivers, lakes, and rain. They are typically depicted as powerful, often benevolent figures who can bring rain for crops or calm storms. However, in the Dao-Twisted World, as with everything sacred, this image is corrupted. The “Dragon King” here isn’t a majestic rain-bringer—it’s a hungry, anonymous thing that thrashes against hulls and demands blood sacrifices based on surname. This is a classic example of how the novel weaponizes familiar folk-deity imagery: take something that should be worshipped and respected, and turn it into a predator that rewards superstition with violence.

**Surname Taboo and Sacrificial Logic** – The belief that a water spirit only feeds on specific surnames (Chen and Fan) is a folkloric detail rooted in real Chinese superstitions about “named” or “claimed” victims. In traditional folk religion, knowing an entity’s name or feeding pattern could give humans power over it—or, conversely, make you a target if your name matched its appetite. The captain’s desperation to find the “false name” is a survival instinct that’s both logical and monstrous, a perfect microcosm of the Dao-Twisted World’s cruel, rule-based horror.

**Bronze Coin Sword (铜钱剑)** – A ritual Daoist weapon made of old Chinese coins strung together on red cord. Traditionally used for exorcism and feng shui, it’s more symbolic than practical in most stories. In this novel, it’s a real, combat-ready tool that can scatter into individual coins and reform, capable of cutting supernatural entities. Li Huowang using it to slice through the living black hair shows that even in this corroded world, some ritual tools still hold power.

### Face-Changing (变脸, bianlian)

A traditional Sichuan opera technique where performers rapidly switch between painted masks. In the Dao-Twisted World, it becomes a psychological trigger for Li Huowang, who associates any change of face with Zuowandao deception.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in building tension from a simple, primal fear: the thing under the water. The "Dragon King" isn't described in detail—it's just a blur, black hair, and a series of bone-rattling impacts. That vagueness makes it more terrifying than any fully-rendered monster. The horror here is also deeply social. The captain isn't a villain; he's a terrified man doing what his worldview tells him will save everyone. The mob isn't evil; it's desperate. And into this mess walks a nameless, bearded card player who decides that being a man means standing between a child and a monster. Li Huowang doesn't lead this charge—he follows someone else's moral clarity for once. Pay attention to that moment: it’s rare in this novel to see someone else take the heroic stand first. The question hanging over the chapter’s cliffhanger is brutal: the saltwater agony is just the opening price. What comes next is the real toll.

## Deeper Dive: The Psychology of the “Sacrificial Stranger”

This chapter taps into a deeply unsettling social psychology that the Dao-Twisted World explores repeatedly: the willingness of a group to sacrifice a single, often innocent, member to save themselves. The “outsider” (the little girl with the unlucky surname) becomes the focal point of collective fear and violence. This isn’t just monster horror; it’s a mirror held up to real-world mechanisms of scapegoating and mob justice. Li Huowang’s hesitation—he *almost* stays out of it—is a grim reflection of how easily even a hardened survivor can be tempted to let someone else die for his own safety. The bearded man’s interruption is a rare spark of uncorrupted human decency in a world that burns decency away. It’s worth noting that Li Huowang doesn’t embrace this morality; he *follows* it, at least for now.
