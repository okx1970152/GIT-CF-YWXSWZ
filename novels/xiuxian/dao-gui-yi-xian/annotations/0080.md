## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang, after surviving the horrors of Zhengde Temple, adopts a new survival strategy: reconnaissance. This chapter is a quiet, methodical breather—a deliberate shift from non-stop body horror to procedural intelligence-gathering. Our protagonist plays the role of a calculating detective, using gold beans as keys to unlock local gossip and dissect the safety of Anci Nunnery before committing to a visit. The chapter's tension comes not from monsters, but from Li Huowang's paranoid preparation and the subtle, unsettling details that surface about the nuns' living conditions. It's a masterclass in the quiet before the storm.

## Key Plot Points

- The group arrives at an inn near Mount Henghua. Gouwa flaunts their newfound wealth, but Li Huowang insists on eating in the main hall to eavesdrop on the townsfolk.
- Li Huowang listens to local chatter but learns nothing useful about the nunnery; the townsfolk barely acknowledge its existence.
- He bribes the waiter with a gold bean for information. The waiter reveals that Anci Nunnery has poor incense and that women usually become nuns only as a last resort after being impoverished or widowed.
- The waiter disgustingly describes the nuns' filthiness: never bathing, their blue robes turning black from grime, and being able to roll dirt off their skin.
- Li Huowang notes Sun Baolu's abnormal behavior—never undressing, hiding to relieve himself—but decides not to pry, respecting his secret as he would have others respect his own.
- He spends days gathering intel before finally confirming that the nuns seem non-threatening based on public opinion (they are described as dirty, lazy, and fat).
- He orders the others to flee if he hasn't returned by the next day, then ascends the mountain alone. Bai Lingmiao watches him go, speechless.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**"Gu-zi" (姑子) as a colloquial term for nuns**: In Chinese folk language, "gu-zi" is a somewhat dismissive or diminutive term for female Buddhist monastics. It carries a distinctly different weight than the more formal "ni-gu" (尼姑). The waiter's use of "gu-zi" frames the nuns not as pious devotees but as social outcasts—women who have fallen through the cracks of the village system. This aligns with the historical reality that for many women in imperial China, entering a nunnery was not a spiritual calling but a survival mechanism when faced with destitution or persecution.

**The social trap of "eating the estate" (吃绝户)**: This brutal practice refers to a widow without a male heir being stripped of her property by her late husband's relatives or even the entire village. Since she "belongs" to the family only through her husband, and without a son to inherit, the community sees her as a loose asset to be divided. This is the fate the waiter describes: her husband dead, her family far away, and unable to bear a son. Becoming a nun was preferable to being stripped bare and left to die.

**Greenish-blue robes turning black from grime**: The detail about the nuns' robes turning from "qing" (青, a blue-green) to black is a horrifically vivid bit of folk horror. In traditional Chinese society, dirty robes weren't just a hygiene problem; they were a moral signifier—a sign of decay, neglect, and a broken space. The waiter's disgusted anecdote is the world's way of telling Li Huowang: "This place is not sacred. It's merely where the broken go to rot."

### Gu-zi (姑子)

A colloquial, slightly dismissive term for a Buddhist nun, often used by common folk. It carries less formality and more social weight than 'ni-gu' (尼姑), framing nuns as societal rejects rather than spiritual practitioners.

## Reading Guide

**Why is Li Huowang so careful this time?** Because the trauma from Zhengde Temple is still fresh. He explicitly names it—he doesn't want a repeat of that. His reconnaissance is a sign of growth: he's learning to treat this world like a system with rules, not a nightmare to be endured. Every gold bean he spends on info is an investment in survival.

**The "ordinary horror" of human filth**: The chapter leans into a different kind of dread. The nuns aren't described as demonic or possessed. They're described as *dirty*. That's scarier in some ways. It implies decay without supernatural cause—just poverty, neglect, and the institutional rot of a broken system. Li Huowang's paranoia is almost disappointed.

**Bai Lingmiao's silence**: She watches him leave, eyes wet, but she says nothing. This isn't passivity—it's a character beat. She's learning that in this world, her words don't always help. Li Huowang is isolating himself again, and she can feel the distance growing. Her silence is a wound.
