## Chapter Overview

We catch our breath—sort of—with a quiet chapter that’s anything but filler. Li Huowang has a new solution to his double-world nightmare: just get strong enough that the cage can’t hold him. It sounds simple, but this chapter is really about what “growth” means in a world where the cost is carved into your own living skin. We watch Li Huowang forge his second *human-skin Dharma artifact*, and the casual horror of the process reminds you this is no ordinary cultivation—it’s self-mutilation as progress. But the heart of the chapter (pun slightly intended) is the slow, awkward conversation between a father and a daughter who isn’t human. Li Sui’s endless questions about fear, acceptance, and belonging hit harder than any monster.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang decides the only way out of the psychiatric ward is to master his cultivation art—not by bargaining with doctors but by brute-forcing his freedom from the Dao-Twisted side.  
- He forges a second palm-sized human-skin artifact using mercury and his own flesh, confirming the process is becoming easier with repetition.  
- The artifact is more than a parlor trick: it’s a backup plan for decapitation-level death and can be mass-produced, signaling a shift in Li Huowang’s tactical confidence.  
- Li Sui, watching the process, begs to learn. Li Huowang refuses, then reconsiders, handing her the *Compendium of Talismans*—pragmatic but risky.  
- Their walk down the mountain becomes a raw Q&A: Li Sui asks why everyone fears her, whether showing her true form would change things, and why she can’t go back inside his belly.  
- Li Huowang confronts his own revulsion toward leeches (cow-leeches are apparently a thing in these rice paddies), a rare moment of mundane vulnerability that contrasts the body horror of his cultivation.  
- The chapter ends in the sunlit fields, with Yang Xiaohai proudly showing off his two acres of land—a glimpse of fragile peace and ownership that Li Huowang, for now, can’t quite touch.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The golden-light incantation (“天地玄宗”)** is a direct pull from Daoist protective lore. The full text Li Huowang chants is the *Jin Guang Shen Zhou* (Golden Light Spirit Incantation), a classic apotropaic mantra meant to shield the practitioner from harm and evil. It’s a staple in Daoist ritual, often used before dangerous procedures. That Li Huowang recites it while shrinking his own skin with mercury tells you everything about how the novel twists sacred text into survival tech. The mantra’s job is to protect—but from what? Himself? The ritual? The reader gets to decide.

**Mercury (shuǐ yín, 水银)** was historically a key ingredient in Daoist external alchemy, famed for its transformative (and highly toxic) properties. Alchemists believed it could extend life and purify substances; in reality, it kills you. The novel leans hard on that double edge: it’s the physical medium for “preserving” the human skin while simultaneously being a poison that would rot a normal person from the inside. Li Huowang surviving the process is itself a declaration that he’s no longer fully human.

**The cow-leech (niú làn dāng, 牛烂裆)**: Yang Xiaohai casually calls it *cow rot-crotch*, a vivid folk name. Li Huowang’s visceral disgust stands out in a story where he rarely flinches at supernatural monstrosities. It’s a smart character beat: the horror of the familiar (a blood-sucking worm) can sometimes bypass defenses that cosmic terrors can’t touch. It also grounds the setting in the gritty, agricultural reality of Niuxin Village—practical, muddy, alive in the most mundane way.

**“Human-skin artifact”**: This echoes the *pí rén* (皮人) in folk sorcery and the dreaded *huàn pí* (换皮) legends, where sorcerers used human skin as a medium for disguise or resurrection. Li Huowang shrinking his own skin into a palm-sized doll is a brutal inversion: instead of stealing someone else’s face, he’s using his own as a phylactery. The taboo isn’t spiritual—it’s logistical. It works.

### Human-skin artifact

A palm-sized doll made from Li Huowang’s own skin and mercury; acts as a phylactery that can prevent death even from decapitation.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in tonal dissonance, and *Dao Gui Yi Xian* does it better than almost any other web novel on the market. You have a scene of self-flaying and mercury washing followed immediately by an innocent child asking why “even the steamed buns are afraid of me.” The emotional whiplash is the whole point. Li Huowang is trying to build a life here—teaching, farming, raising kids—while simultaneously preparing for the day he might need to survive a beheading. It’s tragic and darkly funny and weirdly heartwarming all at once.

Pay special attention to Li Sui’s dialogue. She’s not a monster having a crisis; she’s a child having a crisis who happens to be a monster. The distinction matters. Li Huowang’s blunt “You’re not human” is meant as a safety warning, but to her, it’s a rejection. The novel understands that identity horror works best when wrapped in everyday concerns: “If I’m nice to them, will they stop being scared?” Oof.

Yang Xiaohai’s triumphant claim over two acres of mud and leeches is the chapter’s emotional anchor. In a world of gods, worms, and skin-crafting, this kid just wants to never go hungry again. That’s the *real* cultivation right there.
