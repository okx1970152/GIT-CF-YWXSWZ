## Chapter Overview

Remember that bloodthirsty, slick-talking scholar wannabe from earlier chapters? Yeah, guess what—**karma’s a bitch**, and it just sat down right next to him. This chapter flips the script hard: we see Lü Xiucai at rock bottom, scraping for a meal, while Bai Lingmiao—the same girl who used to whisper and hide behind her sleeves—drops the hammer on him with a coldness that'll make your teeth rattle. It’s a brutal, squeamish feast of social humiliation, simmering rage, and the first real glimpse of how much Bai Lingmiao has *changed* after touching the Purple-Tasseled Sword.

## Key Plot Points

- **Lü Xiucai’s Desperate State**: He’s broke, hungry, and has been abandoned by all his former drinking buddies. His wife Tao’er offers him vegetable pancakes, but his pride refuses to take “leftovers” from a woman.
- **The Crushing Reunion**: On the road to Niuxin Village, Lü Xiucai stumbles upon Bai Lingmiao and Chun Xiaoman’s cart. His attempt to hitch a ride is met with a kick and sharp rejection from Bai Lingmiao.
- **Bai Lingmiao’s Transformation on Full Display**: She is no longer gentle or merciful. Her words are clipped, dismissive, and carry absolute authority. She mocks him for his past cruelty toward his father (Lü Zhuangyuan) and threatens to evict the entire Lü family troupe if he follows them.
- **The Silent Second Spirit**: A subtle but powerful presence. The Second Spirit emerges behind Bai Lingmiao as a visual threat, and Lü Xiucai backs down without another word.
- **Hunger and Humiliation**: Following the cart all the way to an inn, Lü Xiucai sits at an adjacent table, reduced to watching them eat while his stomach growls. Bai Lingmiao’s final dismissal shatters his last shred of dignity.
- **A Violent Breaking Point**: Later, a man in an alley tries to buy Tao’er as a “leased concubine” for three taels of silver. Lü Xiucai fights him, gets severely beaten, and ends up losing two molars and all remaining pride.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **Leasing a Concubine (典妾)**: A grim historical practice in pre-modern China, where a poor man could pawn his wife to a wealthier family to bear a male heir in exchange for money. The transaction was temporary, but the sheer dehumanization of treating a woman as a rental womb is the whole point: this is the dark side of a world without social safety nets. Lü Xiucai’s violent refusal is a rare moment of moral backbone.
- **The Purple-Tasseled Sword’s Corruption (紫穗剑)**: This is the second character we’ve seen warped by the same blade. First Lü Xiucai turned arrogant and cruel; now Bai Lingmiao becomes sharp-tongued, domineering, and almost predatory. The sword doesn’t just boost aggression—it rewires the wielder’s personality, making them a “sharpened” version of themselves. The true cost of power here isn’t pain; it’s losing who you were.
- **Bai Lingmiao’s Evolution from Secretive to Commanding**: Her past trauma (kidnapping, abuse, escape) stripped her of passivity, but the sword gave her a new weapon: unapologetic authority. In Chinese literary archetypes, this is a “resolute heroine” (女强) born from suffering rather than training. Her refusal to coddle Lü Xiucai is a direct inversion of the idealized “saving grace” trope.

### Leased Concubine (典妾)

A grim historical practice where a man temporarily pawned his wife to a wealthier family for the purpose of bearing a male heir. The transaction was temporary but deeply dehumanizing.

### Purple-Tasseled Sword (紫穗剑)

A cursed weapon of the “military path” that gradually corrupts its wielder’s personality, sharpening aggression and arrogance while eroding gentleness and empathy.

## Reading Guide

If you were still holding onto the idea that Bai Lingmiao is a soft, fragile flower—**let this chapter stomp that into the mud**. She’s not the one begging for help anymore; she’s the one deciding who gets to stand in her presence. And let’s be honest: watching Lü Xiucai get verbally eviscerated by the same girl he once dismissed is more satisfying than any monster fight. But here’s the rub: is this newfound ruthlessness really *her*, or is the sword doing the talking? Keep that question burning in the back of your mind.

## From the 'Dao Twisted' Lens

- **Power as Cruelty, Not Liberation**: The novel consistently shows that gaining power doesn't make you benevolent—it makes you *harder*. Bai Lingmiao’s cruelty is a survival adaptation. The Dao-Twisted World doesn’t reward kindness; it burns it out of you. Compare her now to the girl who used to kneel in terror: the world has reshaped her into a weapon, and weapons don’t hesitate.
- **The Second Spirit as a Silent Enforcer**: She appears without a sound, her presence alone enough to silence Lü Xiucai. In this world, you don’t need to speak your threat—sometimes the right entity standing behind you is worth a thousand words.
- **Class Dynamics and Abandonment**: Lü Xiucai’s fall from a potential “young master” to a starving outcast is a classic jianghu tragedy. Friends abandon you the moment your silver runs out. The Dao-Twisted World mirrors the gritty, unromantic reality of historical survival: loyalty costs money.
