## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, fellow travelers on the twisted Dao, because this chapter is a brutal gut-punch of identity crisis. Li Huowang, fresh from the cosmic chaos of Doumu’s rampage, has a new trump card: he’s turned Zhuge Yuan into a hallucination. It’s a fragile, desperate hope, a drop of water in a desert of despair. But that hope immediately curdles into something far more sinister. As Li Huowang and his ghostly companion flee the ruins of the Liang Imperial Palace, a deeply buried piece of himself claws its way to the surface. Memory fragments lead to stone-cold evidence, and suddenly the question isn’t just “Am I crazy?” but “Was I *ever* who I thought I was?” This chapter doesn’t just twist the knife; it pulls the entire rug out from under the reader’s feet, questioning the very foundation of the protagonist we’ve followed for over four hundred chapters.

## Key Plot Points
* Li Huowang successfully conjures a hallucination of Zhuge Yuan, viewing it as a critical first step toward true resurrection. He explains his plan to use his Heart-Element power to turn the hallucination back into a real person.
* While exiting the chaotic battle between the Zuowandao and the Supervisory Heavenly Office, Bei Feng tries to entice Li Huowang with the promise of a cultivation manual from his past as Hong Zhong. Li Huowang dismisses it as a trap.
* After escaping the capital, Li Huowang reveals the source of his newfound knowledge to a skeptical Zhuge Yuan: he claims he is remembering the truth. He is not Li Huowang, a mental patient from the modern world. He is Hong Zhong, a high-ranking member of the Zuowandao.
* To prove his point, Li Huowang changes his face to Hong Zhong’s and visits a roadside stall, where a secret agent he planted six years ago confirms his identity by producing a hidden mahjong tile inscribed with “Red Center — Li Huowang.”
* Just as Li Huowang is about to accept this devastating reality, Zhuge Yuan argues against it, presenting a radical alternative. He posits that Li Huowang’s past wasn’t *remembered* but was *swapped* with another version of history, just as the true history of Great Qi was swapped with the false history of Great Liang.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Heart-Element (心素) vs. Heart-Pivot (心蟠): Same ‘Heart,’ Different Rules.** This chapter clarifies a crucial limitation in Li Huowang’s power. Both he and Zhuge Yuan share the “Heart” (心) designation in their supernatural titles, implying a deep metaphysical connection. The Heart-Element’s power is the Primordial One Qi (先天一炁), the ability to make subjective beliefs and hallucinations bleed into objective reality. However, Zhuge Yuan points out a critical piece of lore: the Primordial One Qi, for all its world-shaping power, cannot create a Heart-Pivot. This suggests that a Heart-Pivot occupies a different ‘weight class’ or ‘root code’ in reality. Pivots are not just products of belief; they are fundamental anchors of a specific world’s structure. This is a classic xianxia power-system nuance—abilities have specific *categories* they cannot cross, regardless of raw power.

**The Swap of Worlds (天下换).** Zhuge Yuan introduces a mind-bending concept: historical reality itself can be swapped. According to his theory, the world where Great Qi is the rightful, unified empire is the *true* reality. The world we’ve seen, where Great Liang holds power and the land is fractured, is a *false* reality that has been superimposed or swapped in. This isn’t a simple illusion; it’s a large-scale ontological overwrite. This explains how Li Huowang might *feel* and have *memories* of being Hong Zhong without *being* Hong Zhong. The memories are artifacts of a swapped past, not a true history. This elevates the novel’s conflict from personal madness to a cosmic battle of historical narratives.

**Secret Agents in Mahjong Tiles.** The method Li Huowang uses to confirm his identity is wonderfully grounded in the novel’s lore. The Zuowandao uses mahjong tiles as their rank insignia. A hidden, engraved tile is a perfect, un-fakeable credential in this world, a physical key to verify a metaphysical identity. It’s a clever bit of spycraft that feels perfectly at home in the bizarre logic of the Dao-Twisted World.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is where the “jig is up” for Li Huowang’s sanity. Get ready for a philosophical rollercoaster. The text directly echoes the abbess’s prophecy from Anci Nunnery, and the final revelation forces the reader to re-evaluate everything. Don’t just read the conclusion Li Huowang draws; pay close attention to *how* Zhuge Yuan deconstructs the evidence. His counter-theory is the only lifeline left, and whether you believe it or not will determine how you view the entire story going forward. The chapter title, ‘The Past That Was,’ is a perfectly loaded hook. It doesn’t ask if the past is true or false, only that it *was*, leaving its ownership deeply ambiguous. This is a masterclass in how to use a core identity crisis as a narrative accelerator, not a dead end.
