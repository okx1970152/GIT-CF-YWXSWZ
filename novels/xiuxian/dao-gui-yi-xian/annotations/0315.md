## Chapter Overview
Let’s be real for a second: Li Huowang has a *terrible* poker face. When Tuoba Danqing hands him a mission slip, the poor guy practically shouts “YES, FINALLY” with his eyes. But here’s the kicker—Tuoba is no fool. A mid-level bureaucrat in a supernatural enforcement agency who didn’t survive by being dense, he sees the eagerness and immediately suspects an ulterior motive. What follows is a razor-thin tightrope walk where Li Huowang must weaponize his genuine trauma into a believable lie, pass a loyalty test, and secure a fast track to the Office's classified archives. All while a certain domino-wielding trickster snarks from the sidelines. Get ready for a masterclass in bureaucratic intrigue, wrapped in a drunken dinner.

## Key Plot Points
- Tuoba Danqing presents Li Huowang with a case file from a county town, detailing bizarre events. Li Huowang immediately suspects the Zuowandao, getting an earful from Hongzhong.
- Li Huowang’s overly enthusiastic acceptance of the mission raises Tuoba Danqing’s suspicions. The atmosphere turns cold as Tuoba openly questions his motives.
- Thinking fast, Li Huowang recalls the *Manual of Deception* and delivers a performance: he claims he joined the Office to seek revenge on the Zuowandao, who he says wiped out his entire temple in the Ao-Jing Sect. The hatred in his voice is real, even if the story is a fabrication.
- Tuoba Danqing is moved by the performance, apologizes, and drinks three cups as penance. He promises Li Huowang priority on all Zuowandao-related cases.
- Seizing the moment, Li Huowang asks to see the Office’s secret files on the Zuowandao. Tuoba explains they are Celestial-Grade secrets, only accessible to someone of Geng-Qi rank.
- Tuoba outlines the promotion system: it’s based on mission completion, not raw power. A promotion to Ren-Wu could take six months.
- Unable to wait that long, Li Huowang asks for a faster path. Tuoba offers him a particularly difficult case, promising to put in a good word with the higher-ups if he succeeds.
- Tuoba casually mentions that “Blind Chen” is waiting in Yinling City to return a certain mirror, a loose thread from a previous encounter.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
Let’s unpack the bureaucratic hall of mirrors that is the **Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司)** . On the surface, it’s a government-sponsored supernormal law enforcement body—imagine the FBI but with ghost-busting powers and a pay grade measured in lifespans. But Tuoba Danqing’s casual line about “not giving a damn what the emperor’s name is” reveals its true nature. The Office exists to *stabilize* the supernatural ecosystem, not to serve the throne. They are mercenaries with a badge, pragmatists who will let a village burn if it prevents a province-wide catastrophe. Joining them isn’t a patriotic duty; it’s a career move with great insurance (and a pension in yang-life).

Then there’s the **rank system**. Li Huowang is currently a *Gui-Zu (癸卒)*, literally a “Tenth-Grade Runner.” The ranks use the **Ten Heavenly Stems (十天干)** —Jia, Yi, Bing, Ding, Wu, Ji, Geng, Xin, Ren, Gui—from highest to lowest. It’s a classical system used for everything from calendar dates to bureaucratic hierarchy. In the Office, climbing from Gui to Geng (skipping six grades) is like going from a mailroom intern to a mid-level manager. The key detail? Promotion isn’t about cultivation strength; it’s about *merit*. You can be a demigod, but if you’ve never bled for the Office, you’re still a paper-pusher in their eyes. This is a system designed to foster loyalty, not power—making Li Huowang’s impatience all the more understandable.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterful display of Li Huowang’s evolution from a reactive survivor to a proactive schemer. The core tension lies not in a monster attack or a ritual gone wrong, but in a *polite dinner conversation*. Every question from Tuoba is a landmine; every answer from Li Huowang must be calibrated to within a hair’s breadth of believable. The highlight is his improvised confession about the Ao-Jing Sect massacre—a lie built on a foundation of real, unfiltered rage. It’s a perfect example of the novel’s core theme: boundary erosion. Is he lying, or has he simply re-framed his own trauma to serve a new narrative? The Office, meanwhile, is revealed as a deeply political beast—slow, bureaucratic, and requiring a long grind. For a reader accustomed to protagonists who level up in weeks, this six-month prospect is a refreshing dose of gritty realism. Watch how Li Huowang decides to hack the system, and ask yourself: at what point does the mask become the face?
