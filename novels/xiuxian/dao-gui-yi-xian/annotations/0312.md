## Chapter Overview

This is the chapter where the last of Li Huowang’s hope is surgically removed, and the wound is left open to the rain. After the massacre at Niuxin Village—where he killed Bai Lingmiao’s entire clan—our walking catastrophe of a protagonist finally stops running. He trudges through mud, endures living rainwater on fresh scar tissue like a walking acupuncture session from hell, and holes up in a temple whose optimistic couplet about fate is already half-eaten by moss. The chapter then folds inward: Li Huowang has a four-on-one intervention with his hallucinations (Red Zhong, the Monk, Peng Longteng, and Jin Shan Zhao), nearly suicides with the Purple-Tasseled Sword, and then slides into a sensory-switch where his “real world” body is now a homeless man eating out of trash cans. By the end, a kid throws a rock at him, calls him a madman, and Li Huowang laughs—loudly, freely, and absolutely broken. This is the sound of a man accepting the label the world gave him, because fighting it hurt too much.

## Key Plot Points

1. **Silent Walk from Niuxin Village**: Li Huowang’s paper clothes and new skin dissolve in the rain, exposing his charred flesh and new buds—a physical mirror of his stripped-bare emotional state.

2. **The Temple Couplet**: Two lines about human effort and the mind creating circumstance, which read less like wisdom and more like a bitter joke against the man who just butchered an entire family.

3. **The Hallucination Confrontation**: Red Zhong gloats, the Monk offers hollow Buddhist platitudes, Peng Longteng scoffs, and Jin Shan Zhao silently watches. Li Huowang *finally* snaps at them—demanding to know what they are and why they follow *him*—but his sword passes through them harmlessly.

4. **Failed Suicide by Sword**: He collapses mid-outburst, the weapon skidding away. He is too exhausted even to die.

5. **Reality-Switch to Homelessness**: The temple dissolves into a bridge underpass. Li Huowang is now a vagrant, drinking river water and scavenging half-eaten food from a public trash can.

6. **Final Laugh**: A stranger takes a photo for social media. A child throws a stone. Li Huowang laughs, declares himself a “madman,” and the chapter closes on his accepting grin.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter leans heavily on the *condition of the heart-element (xinsu)* as an existential trap. Li Huowang’s question, *“Why does it have to be me?”* is not a typical protagonist’s grievance but a man realizing his own ontology is a cosmic error. The novel’s core horror—that a Heart-Element *makes belief into reality*—means that his madness is not a side effect; it is the function. His hallucinations (Red Zhong, the Monk, Peng Longteng, Jin Shan Zhao) are persistent, unkillable constructs that mirror his own fractured self: trickery, guilt, violence, and mute observation.

The temple couplet, *“Human effort decides all; the mind creates the circumstance,”* is a classical piece of Chinese folk-wisdom that typically encourages self-reliance. In this context, it becomes a sick joke: Li Huowang’s “effort” destroyed a family, and his “mind” creates a reality that tortures him. The rotting, half-swallowed characters on the wall echo the story’s central theme—that any comforting philosophy is just a pretty mask on a skull.

The transition to homelessness in the “modern world” hallucination is a grim innovation: the novel has long maintained a duality between a supernatural “Dao-Twisted World” and a psychiatric “Modern Reality.” Here, the two fuse into a *third state*—a gutter-level existence where Li Huowang’s identity is reduced to “madman” by a camera-wielding public. The stone thrown by the boy is the final external confirmation of what he has internalized: he is not a tragic hero; he is a spectacle to be avoided and mocked.

### Chinese temple couplet

A pair of parallel poetic lines flanking a temple door, meant to offer wisdom or blessings. The couplet here (“Human effort decides all; the mind creates the circumstance”) is a classical proverb on self-reliance, now reading as cruel irony.

## Reading Guide

Get ready to feel *uncomfortable*, fellow Daoists, because this chapter doesn’t give you a cool power-up or a dramatic vow of vengeance. It gives you a man eating cold barbecue out of a trash can. Li Huowang is past bargaining, past rage, and sliding straight into the “depression” phase of grief, with the volume turned up to eleven and the dignity turned down to zero. If you’ve been reading for the catharsis of him fighting back—this is the chapter where he first stops fighting entirely and just… lets the rain hit him.

Pay close attention to the way his hallucinations *don’t* react when he swings the sword. They aren’t scared of him. They can’t be hurt by him. They’re stuck to him like a disease that has already won. And when the Monk tells him to “recite more Amituofo,” it’s not comforting—it’s a sign that even his own mind’s version of “mercy” is a shallow, useless script.

The final laugh is the one thing to watch for. It’s not a villain’s laugh. It’s the sound of a man who has finally stopped trying to convince anyone—including himself—that he’s sane. He accepts the label. And in that acceptance, there is a terrifying kind of freedom. The chapter ends there, with nothing resolved, and that is exactly the point.
