## Chapter Overview
Get ready for one of the most psychologically unnerving chapters yet. Our boy Li Huowang, fresh off his recent spree of aggressive pragmatism, decides to get answers the old-fashioned way: by strapping a local waiter to a chair and putting his… special toolkit on display. The target of this interrogation? The quiet, communal terror behind the county’s epidemic of *suoyang* and *suoyin*. But what he gets is not the secret of a rival demon cult or a rogue alchemist—it’s the revelation of something far more insidious: a ghost that can only spread by word of mouth. This chapter is a masterclass in shifting horror from the physical to the informational, and it’s going to leave you feeling a little cold.

## Key Plot Points
Li Huowang calls the inn’s waiter to his room for a private conversation. When the waiter tries to flee, Gao Zhijian blocks the door. Li Huowang calmly lays out his toolkit, giving a clinical description of how one tool is used to wind intestines, which finally breaks the waiter’s spirit. The man reveals that the cause of the mysterious bodily shrinkage is “ghosts” (*gui*)—wandering souls seeking to steal male reproductive organs so they can be reborn as men. He adds that people cannot see these ghosts; they simply get closer and closer until their victims die. Li Huowang is puzzled, but what alarms him most is the secondary rule: the waiter and others refuse to speak of it because they believe that merely *telling* someone invites the ghost’s attention. The chapter ends with the waiter checking himself and freezing in horror, suggesting that the telling has already begun.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
Let’s talk about the *informational ghost*. In classic Chinese folk horror, not all monsters are physical beasts. There is a long tradition of plagues and curses that spread through speech, legends, or even the sharing of a name. The idea here is deeply rooted: mentioning the *suoyang* ghost is seen as an invitation, a kind of auditory summoning. This taps into a primal fear—the fear of knowledge itself being a vector for danger. It’s a cousin to the “old ghost story” trope where speaking of a demon after dark gives it power. Notice how the waiter was terrified not of the ghost’s fangs or claws, but of the *act of confession*. This turns every potential source of information into a trap, a clever inversion of the "helpful informant" scene you see in standard xianxia.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is all about atmosphere and a new, viral kind of terror. Pay close attention to the shift in Li Huowang’s strategy: he doesn’t face a physical enemy here; he faces a rumor. The real horror is not in a monster attack, but in the waiter’s desperate silence and the implication that knowledge itself is dangerous. Watch how Li Huowang’s cold, methodical mind works here—he tests the story’s logic, but his survival instinct tells him to treat it as real anyway. And that final freeze? The waiter checking what he’s lost? It’s a perfect, quiet punchline to a piece of dread. We are officially moving into a new, more subtle phase of supernatural threat in the Dao-Twisted World.
