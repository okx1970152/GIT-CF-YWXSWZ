## Chapter Overview

Welcome back, fellow travelers of the twisted path! This chapter serves up a different dish of horror. Forget the screaming flesh-monsters and the splattering gore for a moment. Here, the real terror is a quiet, yellowed envelope. Li Huowang, fresh from his confrontation with the Ao-Jing Sect, finds a letter from Bai Lingmiao—a lifeline of hope dangled right in front of him. But in this world, hope is just the bait in a Zuowandao trap. We watch Huowang’s mind spiral from manic joy to cold, biting suspicion as he tries to puzzle out if the letter is a gift from his missing wife or a performance staged for his torment. It’s a masterclass in psychological dread, proving that sometimes a single, kind word can cut deeper than any blade. And just when you think you've caught your breath, the plot pivots to a new scene: Lü Zhuangyuan’s troupe finally arriving at the capital, setting the stage for a whole new kind of trouble.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang rejects Zu Yi’s offer of help, confirming that the Ao-Jing Sect has no real solution for the Dice, and telling him to leave with a flash of anger that reveals his lingering trauma from the *Thousand Greats Record*.
- A little beggar delivers a letter from Bai Lingmiao, which Li Huowang receives with trembling excitement. The letter is warm, apologetic, and mentions “two years” — a specific timeframe that immediately triggers a shocking connection in Li Huowang’s mind.
- Li Huowang pieces together that the “two years” reference matches something he said to Yang Na in the modern world, leading him to a frantic hypothesis: Bai Lingmiao might not have been kidnapped by the Dice at all, and the whole thing was a lie.
- The brief euphoria is shattered when Li Huowang realizes the beggar is a Zuowandao agent (a Nine-Wan). He attempts to interrogate him, but the enemy kills himself before any information can be extracted, leaving Li Huowang with nothing but doubts.
- The chapter cuts to a new scene: Lü Zhuangyuan’s opera troupe, carrying Li Huowang’s travel permit, passes through the capital’s gates, marking their arrival in the politically complex heart of Great Liang.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is a fantastic example of the **"psychological horror of the mundane."** The horror here isn't a monster you can stab; it’s a piece of paper. In the Dao-Twisted World, information is a weapon, and trust is a liability. Li Huowang’s frantic analysis of the letter—was it real? Was it faked? Who wrote it?—is a painful reminder that for a Heart-Element, reality is negotiable. The Zuowandao don’t need to torture you with knives; they torture you with *possibilities*. The letter’s mention of “dried persimmons” (柿饼) is a masterstroke of this. It’s a sweet, domestic detail meant to bypass Huowang’s paranoia and appeal to his heart. It's a folk-horror version of a phishing email, and the fact that it almost worked is the most chilling part.

The scene also gives us a peek into imperial bureaucracy with Lü Zhuangyuan’s arrival at the capital. The **travel permit (路引)** is a crucial historical artifact. In ancient China, you couldn't just wander around; you needed official documentation to pass through checkpoints, especially into a walled city. Guarding this permit with his life shows Lü Zhuangyuan’s practical wisdom—in a world of gods and monsters, it’s often the mundane, man-made rules that keep you alive. The **barbican (瓮城)** is a real architectural feature of Chinese city walls, a fortified enclosure designed to trap invaders. Passing through it symbolizes entering a place where the rules—both political and supernatural—might be completely different.

## Reading Guide

Get ready for a shift in tone, folks. This chapter is a *breather chapter*, but don’t let that fool you. These quiet moments are where the best horror simmers. Pay close attention to the emotional whiplash. Watch Li Huowang go from a weary, cynical leader to a kid opening a birthday present, and then watch that joy curdle into cold, hard suspicion. The real action here is in his head. Also, keep an eye on that new setting. We’re in the capital now, the big show. The Supervisory Heavenly Office, the emperor, the walking corpse emperor—the stakes just got a lot more political. This is the calm before a very, very bad storm.

## The Horror of a Kind Word

The deepest cut in this chapter isn't a sword or a tentacle. It's a simple line: *"The dried persimmons here are very sweet. I'll send you some."* In a novel built on screams and blood, this quiet, domestic kindness is a masterful piece of psychological warfare. Bai Lingmiao’s voice—whether real or fabricated—offers Li Huowang something he can't afford to have: hope. And in the Dao-Twisted World, hope is the most dangerous currency of all. This chapter proves that the Zuowandao understand their victim's psychology perfectly. They know Huowang's deepest wound isn't physical; it's the loss of the people he loves. By making him *choose* to doubt a message of love, they are torturing him without lifting a finger. That's the true horror of the Dice.
