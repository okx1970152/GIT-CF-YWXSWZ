## Chapter Overview

This chapter offers a quiet, character-driven respite from the relentless action, but don’t mistake “quiet” for “safe.” Li Huowang’s body is finally rebelling in a new and terrifying way: the Black Tai Sui inside him tries to *emerge on its own*, literally crawling out of his throat. After forcing the parasitic entity back, he slips into his hallucinatory bridge-world, where he unexpectedly finds a moment of human warmth. The real punchline, however, comes at the very end—when Li Huowang’s protective instinct kicks into high gear, only to discover that the threat he perceived might not have been aimed at the innocent at all.

## Key Plot Points

- At the post station, Li Huowang wakes from a rare, deep sleep to a sudden, violent nausea. The Black Tai Sui inside him, in the form of Li Sui, forces its way out of his throat—tentacles, mucus, and all. This is the first time the entity has extruded itself *without* being summoned or aided by Li Huowang.
- After vomiting out the tentacle-mass, Li Huowang orders Li Sui to hide under the bed, then mentally rebukes Hong Zhong for her constant, unhelpful commentary. The transition to the hallucinatory world follows immediately.
- Back in the modern bridge-world, Li Huowang finds himself alone—no mother, no Yang Na—and feels a twinge of relief at the solitude. He talks to himself, observing that Li Sui is learning faster and can now understand Hong Zhong.
- Huanhuan, the little girl with the red backpack, reappears. For the first time in many cycles, she recognizes that Li Huowang is “present.” He apologizes for his violent outburst at the kindergarten, accepts her *shaomai*, and warns her to stay away from him when he’s having an episode.
- After she leaves, Li Huowang spots a gold-chain-wearing fat man lurking at the street corner, watching the bridge. In a rage, he bludgeons the man, drags him into the darkness, and impales his palm with broken glass to interrogate him.
- The fat man’s tearful answer is the chapter’s final twist: “I wasn’t staring at the little girl! I was staring at you!”

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

Let’s talk about the *Hope* reference. In this chapter, Li Huowang mentally alludes to a film called *Suwon* (素媛). For Western readers, this is a direct reference to the 2013 South Korean film *Hope* (also known as *The Crucible* or *Silenced* in different markets? No—*Hope* is correct here), which tells the harrowing true story of a young girl who is sexually assaulted. Li Huowang’s immediate leap to that specific film when he sees a man watching Huanhuan reveals a crucial facet of his character: even in his own hallucination, where he knows nothing is “real,” his morality is absolute and unwavering. He will not tolerate the kind of cruelty he knows exists in the real world, even in the fantasy one. This is a rare window into the core ethical principles that still drive him—beneath all the blood, the scavenging, and the paranoia, there’s a man who still *cares* about protecting the vulnerable.

Also noteworthy is the behavior of the Black Tai Sui. In Chinese folk religion, the “Tai Sui” (太岁) is often associated with a year’s “commander in chief” god, a powerful but neutral force. In this novel, it’s been twisted into a parasitic entity that tries to overtake its host entirely. The fact that it’s now pushing out of his throat *voluntarily* is a terrifying escalation. The creature is no longer waiting to be commanded; it’s acting on its own initiative, driven by a childlike, possessive love. “Dad, you won’t die!” is not a threat—it’s the entity’s attempt to *take over the body* in order to keep it alive. This is a uniquely horrific form of loyalty: a parasite trying to *infect* the host to protect it.

## Reading Guide

Get ready for a masterful misdirection, folks. The chapter leads you by the nose into thinking Li Huowang is being a good guy—protecting a little girl from a predator. The narrative rhythm, the lighting, the sudden cold fury… it’s all designed to make you cheer for the violent comeuppance. Then the “predator” turns out to be a terrified, broke fool who was just staring at *him*. It’s a brutal little gut-punch that flips the entire emotional arc of the scene on its head. It also leaves one hell of a question dangling: if the fat man was staring at Li Huowang, *why?* Is he an observer? A bounty hunter? Or just a random civilian who saw a raving, filthy lunatic and couldn’t look away? The ambiguity is the real horror here—not the violence itself, but the realization that Li Huowang might have just tortured an innocent man based entirely on a misconception.

Also, take a moment to appreciate the subtle character work in the bridge-world. Li Huowang is learning to treat the hallucination *differently*. He’s no longer fighting it, no longer interrogating reality. He’s just… *living* in it. He eats the food, he talks to the ghost-girl, he allows himself one moment of softness. This is a survival mechanism, sure—but it’s also a step toward something much darker: the point where the hallucinatory world becomes his *preferred* reality.
