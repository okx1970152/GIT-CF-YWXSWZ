## Chapter Overview

Okay, buckle up, fellow horror fans, because this chapter is a masterclass in “false safety.” Li Huowang is deep in a Supervisory Heavenly Office task force, surrounded by eccentric experts and a Chief Recorder so theatrical he could be a Ming-dynasty eunuch villain. The quarry? A Heart-Turbid—a terrifying entity that warps memories and reality. But the real knife-twist here is emotional. The Second Spirit, his monstrous “wife,” shows up uninvited, clinging to him with an affection that is equal parts terrifying and pitiable. Just when you think you have a handle on the mission’s rules, the Heart-Turbid strikes not with claws, but with a quiet, insidious attack on Li Huowang’s very perception. The chapter ends on a gut-punch of pure cognitive horror: a man holding a weapon he has no memory of acquiring.

## Key Plot Points

- The task force (Chief Recorder, Hong Da, Shen Tugang, Tuoba Danqing, Li Huowang) enters the village to hunt a Heart-Turbid.
- The Second Spirit appears, mistakenly attacked before Li Huowang intervenes; her grotesque appearance and obsessive attachment to Li Huowang shock the team.
- Li Huowang orders her to leave, prioritizing her safety and Bai Lingmiao’s involvement.
- The group moves deeper. Shen Tugang takes command, demonstrating a powerful, commanding Bang Bing Jue that drains color from the world and signals a major supernatural threat.
- Hong Da loans Li Huowang a rusty knife, which Hong Da claims came from a Knife-Pawnbroker and carries supernatural properties.
- Li Huowang uses his bone-divination talisman; the crack pattern aligns with a torn spiderweb on a stone well.
- Peering into the well, Li Huowang sees a figure in the mud; he looks away just in time.
- Turning to warn the group, Li Huowang’s memory blanks—he no longer remembers why he’s at the well or where the knife in his hand came from.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The Heart-Turbid (心浊 / *xīn zhuó*) is a new type of entity introduced in this chapter. While the Heart-Element (心素) is a rare human constitution, the Heart-Turbid appears to be a parasitic or reality-bending monstrosity that targets perception and memory, possibly by “contaminating” the heart-mind (*xīn*). The term “turbid” (浊) is the direct opposite of “clear” (清) in Daoist cosmology, often used to describe muddy, impure *qi*. A being that makes you forget you’re holding a weapon is far more insidious than one that simply attacks.
Also introduced is the Knife-Pawnbroker (赊刀人 / *shē dāo rén*). This is a figure from Chinese folk legend and *jianghu* lore. They would “loan” knives or scissors to people, returning years later to collect not money, but the borrower’s luck or fortune. Hong Da’s rusty knife, capable of improving divination, is a classic example: a supernatural tool that may come with a hidden debt.

## Reading Guide

If you’re new to *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, this chapter is a perfect example of its unique blend: grotesque body horror (Second Spirit’s fused face), grim folklore (Knife-Pawnbroker), procedural horror (a bureaucratic exorcism squad), and the trippiest of cognitive attacks. The “I forgot what I was going to say” moment isn’t just a jump scare; it’s a deep, unsettling *glitch* in Li Huowang’s reality.
Keep an eye on the hierarchy: the Chief Recorder is a eunuch official with immense bureaucracy; Tuoba Danqing is his sycophantic deputy; Shen Tugang is the muscle; Li Huowang is an outsider tolerated for his unique constitution. Every power dynamic here is fragile. And that missing memory? It hints that the Heart-Turbid’s power might already be in the room, hiding in plain sight.

## Knife-Pawnbroker Lore: The Loan That Collects Luck

The *Shē Dāo Rén* (赊刀人) is a fascinating bit of Chinese folklore that deserves its own spotlight. They were itinerant merchants who wandered through villages, offering knives, scissors, or other iron tools on credit. They would leave the item with the buyer, only returning to collect payment *years later*—but the price was often a prediction. “I’ll collect when the price of grain rises to ten silver coins a bushel.” When that impossible event inevitably came true, they would return, and the debt (or their prophetic aura) would be satisfied. In *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, as in the darkest corners of the lore, that debt isn’t just money—it’s your *luck*. Hong Da’s rusty blade, sharp or not, is a token of a contract that can see through fate itself.
