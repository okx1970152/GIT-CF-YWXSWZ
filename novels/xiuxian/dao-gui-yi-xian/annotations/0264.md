## Chapter Overview
Whoa. This chapter hits different. Forget the monsters and the rituals for a second—this one’s a quiet, creeping character study that lands like a punch to the gut. Li Huowang, with his endless experience as a trauma survivor, watches on as a normal, timid kid named Lü Xiucai gets a crash course in what the “military path” does to a person. The sword didn’t just give him a temper; it *broke* him and remade him in a familiar, terrifying image: a Little Peng Longteng. Our protagonist isn't just an observer either. He’s forced to confront a horrifying realization about the nature of power in this world—that every path, every cultivation, every bite of strength comes with a price that rewrites your very soul. Get ready, fellow travelers. This one’s about the cost of becoming strong, and it’s not paid in silver.

## Key Plot Points
- Lü Xiucai, still hanging upside down for his attempted theft, explodes at his father with a shocking level of hatred and violence, a complete departure from his usual timid nature.
- Li Huowang immediately recognizes the change in the boy’s eyes—they are filled with the same predatory brutality he saw in Peng Longteng.
- He realizes the Purple-Tasseled Sword, a “military path” weapon, is the direct cause. Its side effects corrupt the user’s personality, a danger his Heart-Element constitution made him immune to.
- This prompts a major epiphany for Li Huowang: all cultivators in this world absorb something that fundamentally alters their nature. He connects it to the rot of Anci Nunnery, the lust of Zhengde Temple, and now the bloodlust of the military path.
- Lü Ban Zhu, terrified that his son is “possessed,” begs Li Huowang for help. Li Huowang can only offer a grim prognosis: the boy may or may not recover, but prepare for the worst.
- Even Bai Lingmiao’s spirit-summoning ritual fails to diagnose any problem, confirming the change is not a spiritual ailment but a fundamental corruption of the self.
- While Lü Xiucai is eventually untied and forced to travel with the group, his hatred is now solely focused on his father. He still fears Li Huowang, showing he’s not completely lost to madness, just bitterly, dangerously changed.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**“兵家” (The Military Path): Real-World Context and Novel Corruption.** In real-world Chinese history and military thought, “兵家” (Bing Jia) refers to the School of Military Strategy, one of the Hundred Schools of Thought. Think Sun Tzu’s *Art of War*. It’s about strategy, tactics, and logistics. It's an *intellectual* tradition. Li Huowang’s realization is that in the Dao-Twisted World, these ancient philosophical labels are literally baked into the cultivation system, and they carry a *physical and spiritual* toll. The “military” path here isn’t about clever tactics; it’s about absorbing an overwhelming, corrupting **“killing intent” (煞气)**. This chapter brilliantly shows that even a normal, harmless boy can become a vessel for this primal, violent force. It's a dark commentary on the old saying, “To a man with a hammer, everything looks like a nail.” To a man wielding the military path, the world becomes a battlefield, and his own soul becomes a casualty.

**The Heart-Element (心素) as a Natural Shield.** This chapter explicitly shows another hidden benefit of Li Huowang’s “condition.” His innate nature as a Heart-Element is so distorted, so infused with “bewilderment,” that the monofocused corrupting force of the military sword simply slides off him. It’s a curse that acts as a paradoxical blessing. The lesson, again, is that this world’s rule system is based on overwhelming, singular attributes. A normal person is like an empty cup; a power fills them up and becomes their entire identity. Li Huowang’s cup is already overflowing with existential dread, leaving no room for petty things like “bloodlust.”

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in “show, don’t tell.” The horror here isn't a monster; it’s watching a familiar, harmless person *break* right in front of you. Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s cold pragmatism. He isn’t shocked or horrified in the way a normal person would be. He’s **analytical** about a boy’s soul being corrupted. That detachment is his own trauma talking. Also, note the final detail: Lü Xiucai still fears Li Huowang. This is key. The Power Corrupts course isn't instant or absolute. The boy is now a better hater, which is a tragedy. And one final thought—if this is what happens to someone who just *borrows* a military sword, what kind of monster must Peng Longteng have been *after* a lifetime of wielding it?
