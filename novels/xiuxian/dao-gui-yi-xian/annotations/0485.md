## Chapter Overview

Welcome back, fellow daoists, to another trip down the rabbit hole—except this time the rabbit hole has marble floors and eunuch concierges. Li Huowang, our favorite trauma sponge, finds himself reluctantly roped into a guided tour of the Great Liang imperial palace by none other than the Emperor himself. And if you thought the Zuowandao were weird, wait until you see what the imperial family keeps in the basement. What starts as an awkward “thank you for not letting me get scammed” audience turns into a descent into a literal royal cellar of flesh, gold, and ancestor worship gone feral. Get ready: it’s time to see what happens when the imperial cult takes “family bonding” just a little too literally.

## Key Plot Points

- The young Emperor of Great Liang warmly invites Li Huowang to stay and be an official, but the Imperial Preceptor immediately shuts him down, exposing the Emperor’s lack of real power.
- Li Huowang is forced to follow the entourage as they prepare sacrificial offerings for the late Emperor—and one of those offerings is the Spirit-Blight he risked his life to capture.
- The procession moves to a hidden underground chamber beneath the Imperial Temple, accessed through a nine-dragon-carved stone door.
- Inside, a massive golden coffin is suspended by chains, pulled in all directions by nine golden dragons—a spectacle Li Huowang initially mistakes for “nine dragons pulling a coffin.”
- As the young Emperor performs the rites, the coffin lid opens, revealing the horrifying truth: the generations of deceased emperors are stacked into one living, writhing pillar of flesh, bound by dragon-chains, their bodies half-transformed into something scaly and serpentine.
- The young Emperor, seeing his father still moving inside, tries to rush forward but is held back by the old eunuchs, who explain that this is the “Dragon Vein”—the very foundation of Great Liang.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

Let’s talk about the “nine dragons pulling a coffin.” This image is a twist on an iconic motif from ancient Chinese mythology and funerary culture—“九龙拉棺,” where nine dragons are depicted drawing a casket through the heavens or across the void. It’s an image that carries overwhelming gravitas: dragons are symbols of imperial might, cosmic power, and divine mandate. To have them *pull* your coffin means you, the deceased, are being escorted to the afterlife by beings of world-shaking stature.

But here’s the kicker: the setup in this temple isn’t *pulling*. The chains are taut, yes, but they’re resisting the coffin’s movement, not guiding it. The imagery suggests not a celestial procession but an imprisonment—a violent binding. The dragons aren’t honoring the dead; they’re *holding them down*. This flips the entire symbolic register from sacred apotheosis to grotesque containment. And it gets worse: the emperors aren’t just dead bodies; they’re a *living pillar*, a stacked dynasty of half-scaled things that *writhe* and *howl*. This is what Xiao Mao means by “corrupting the sacred”: the Imperial Dragon Vein—normally a geomantic blessing that ensures a dynasty’s prosperity and longevity—has been turned into literal flesh masonry. The imperial family *is* the foundation, and the foundation is *screaming*.

Also note the Emperor’s reaction: he sees his father *moving* and assumes he’s alive. This plays on the deep Chinese horror of the “dead who don’t stay dead in ritual settings,” a folk anxiety that powers everything from zombie myths to exorcism opera. The temple isn’t a tomb; it’s a *prison for the undying*. And the Emperor himself is just another piece being shuffled toward the stack.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in using *scale and revelation* for horror. Notice how the narrative carefully builds anticipation: first, the casual Emperor. Then the rules-bound Preceptor. Then the solemn temple. Then the hidden door. Then the cold air. Then the gold coffin. And *then* the lid opens. Each step raises the stakes without a single monster attack—the dread is architectural, institutional, and *hereditary*. This is the kind of cosmic horror that doesn’t need a face; it just needs a slowly opening lid and a father who still breathes.

Also, pay attention to Li Huowang’s reaction. There’s no panic. No screaming. He processes the horror as a *logical puzzle* and files it away as another piece of the Dao-Twisted World’s operating system. That’s character growth, folks—the man who once screamed at leeches now watches a dynasty of dragon-corpses and thinks, “Well, that’s inconvenient.” If that doesn’t say “I’ve been broken and rebuilt one too many times,” nothing does.

And yes, the Zuowandao hallucination gets a line in. Even in a scene of imperial revelation, the liar can’t stop quipping.
