## Chapter Overview

New chapter, new horror. After three chapters inside a hallucination-ambush that weaponized a folk deity's image, Li Huowang finally claws his way back to reality—but not without a cost. This chapter is a masterclass in *dao-gui-yi-xian*’s signature trick: the false wall. You think you've fought a giant White Wuchang? You *did*, just not in the physical sense. The real trap was a rope around his neck the whole time, a quiet, strangling death disguised as a monster brawl. Once he cuts through the illusion, he finds the Bai family imprisoned and gets his first real look at the community that produced Bai Lingmiao. But something's off. The clan leader, Bai Sai, talks like a politician giving a eulogy—lots of words, zero substance. And Hongzhong is there, whispering that the man is lying. Buckle up; the real fight might just be beginning.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang perceives a massive White Wuchang, but after a gruesome self-mutilation and a fall down its throat, a wooden mask clamps onto his face. He can't breathe. Only by cutting the mask off his own skin does he realize he's been strangled by a rope in the real world.
- The illusion is gone. His arm is intact, but his neck is genuinely wounded, proving the attack was real even if the monster's form was not.
- He frees the Bai family from their wooden cangues, earning their gratitude. Bai Lingmiao's mother breaks down upon learning her daughter is alive.
- Bai Sai, the clan head and a gruff, authoritative figure, introduces himself. He claims ignorance about why his family was taken, chalking it up to his son finding a "stone."
- Hongzhong appears in Li Huowang's peripheral perception, warning him that Bai Sai is lying.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**White Wuchang (白无常)** — In Chinese folk religion, the Black and White Wuchang are a pair of underworld messengers tasked with escorting souls to the afterlife. The *White* one is often associated with punishing the living through possession or binding, and his iconic feature is the long red tongue. By using this figure—a known, mythologically defined entity—the novel weaponizes cultural familiarity: you *think* you know what you're fighting, but the real threat is that the fight itself was a decoy.

**Cangue (木枷)** — A portable wooden pillory, historically used in imperial China to restrain prisoners. It's heavy, degrading, and immobilizing. In the Dao-Twisted World, being fitted with one isn't just punitive—it's ritualistic, marking the wearer as helpless prey for whatever supernatural game is being played.

**"Shortening my lifespan" (折寿)** — A common Chinese expression rooted in folk belief that one's allotted years are finite. When Li Huowang says the old man kneeling would shorten his life, it's a humble refusal, but it also echoes a very real supernatural currency in this novel's universe: lifespan as a tradeable, depletable resource.

## Reading Guide

Alright, fellow travelers down the twisted path of the Dao, this chapter is a *meat grinder* of psychological warfare. Our boy Li Huowang just got hit with a two-layer ambush: first a physical attack disguised as a folklore boss fight, then a suffocation trap that nearly finished him off while he was busy swinging at shadows. If you're new to the genre, here's the rule: in *dao-gui-yi-xian*, never trust a monster's appearance. The real horror is always hiding in the detail you ignored. Also, pay close attention to Bai Sai. He's smooth, he's authoritative, and he's almost certainly feeding Li Huowang a rehearsed script. And *yes*, the reappearance of Hongzhong is a massive red flag—he's supposed to be gone, so why is he back? This is a chapter that rewards the paranoid reader. Keep your eyes peeled, your throat clear, and your trust on a short leash.
