## Chapter Overview
Welcome back, fellow survivors! After the frantic escape from Qingqiu, Chapter 262 gives us a breather—a chapter of inventory management, gear upgrades, and quiet character beats. But don’t mistake this for a “slice of life” episode. This is the calm before the storm, where Li Huowang does what he does best: take stock of his assets, weaponize his surroundings, and make cold, pragmatic decisions. We get a hilarious (and slightly pathetic) spat between Gouwa and Lü Xiucai, a heartwarming man-dog bonding moment, and a masterclass in how to turn a useless slab of rock into a piece of improvised armor. It’s a palate cleanser, but one that subtly sharpens the group’s combat readiness. Get ready for a chapter that feels deceptively safe—which should make you, dear reader, *very* nervous.

## Key Plot Points
- **The Bickering Duo**: Gouwa and Lü Xiucai have a petty argument. Gouwa lords his connection to Li Huowang over Lü Xiucai, who gets so angry he storms off, muttering about his dad’s future opera house. It’s a classic kid-behaving-badly scene that establishes the social dynamics within the group.
- **Li Huowang’s Arsenal Audit**: Alone in the granary, Li Huowang studies Han Fu’s bronze coin sword. He realizes that with this weapon’s scattering ability, he might finally have a viable ranged option that doesn’t require self-mutilation via the *Thousand Greats Record*.
- **The Gear Up Sequence**: Li Huowang decides to repurpose the useless Heavenly Scripture. He straps the stone slab to Gao Zhijian’s chest as a heartguard, then orders him to also wear Peng Longteng’s lightweight armor. The result is a terrifying, heavily armored juggernaut who can hold his own in a spar.
- **The Failed Apprenticeship**: Lü Xiucai bursts in and begs Li Huowang to take him as a disciple. Li Huowang flatly refuses, honestly stating he has nothing to teach. The boy is left kneeling on the floor.
- **A Transfer of Trust**: One-armed Chun Xiaoman enters. Li Huowang, after a moment’s reflection, tosses her the longsword he’s carried since Qingfeng Temple. It’s a quiet but significant act of delegation and trust.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Bronze Coin Sword (铜钱剑)**: This isn’t just a cool weapon; it’s a real artifact with deep roots in Daoist ritual. Traditionally, a bronze coin sword is made by stringing together ancient Chinese coins (usually 108, a sacred Buddhist number co-opted by Daoism) with red thread. The red thread signifies yang energy, warding off evil. The coins themselves are imbued with the “sovereign qi” (王气) of the empire, making them powerful apotropaic tools. Han Fu’s ability to make the coins scatter and reform is a supernatural upgrade on this folk tradition, turning a ritual object into a tactical weapon. It’s a perfect example of how *Dao Gui Yi Xian* takes real-world religious items and twists them into horrifying (or in this case, very useful) tools for its macabre world.

**The Heavenly Scripture as Armor**: The idea of using a sacred text as a shield is a darkly humorous twist. In Chinese folklore, there’s a long history of talismans and scriptures being used for physical protection. A copy of the *Dao De Jing* or a sutra was sometimes placed over the heart to ward off evil spirits or even physical harm. Li Huowang is applying this folk logic in the most brutally pragmatic way possible. The scripture’s content is useless to him, its spiritual power non-existent, but its physical form—a sturdy stone slab—is still a resource. It’s a brilliant, grimly materialist approach to the supernatural world.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in “the quiet before the storm.” The tone is deceptively light, with slapstick comedy from the kids and a heartwarming scene between Gao Zhijian and his dog, Mantou. But don’t be fooled. Li Huowang is doing what he always does—preparing. His decision to arm Gao Zhijian is a tactical upgrade that will almost certainly pay off in the next crisis. Pay close attention to the inventory scene: he’s unhappy with his reliance on the self-harming *Thousand Greats Record* and is actively seeking alternatives. That’s a crucial character detail. Also, note his refusal to teach Lü Xiucai. It’s not cruelty; it’s a bitter, honest recognition that his “power” is a curse he wouldn’t wish on anyone. The chapter ends with a quiet transfer of his old sword to Chun Xiaoman, a sign that he’s grooming her as a fighter, even with one arm. It’s a chapter of small moves, but each one is a chess piece being placed for a coming, larger conflict.
