## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang finally arrives at Youdu, the capital of the Great Qi—except it’s also the Upper Capital of the Great Liang, depending on who you ask. The closer he stays to Zhuge Yuan, the more the world twists around him: shops swap out, cages of female prostitutes become cages of male prostitutes, and even the map in his own hands claims allegiance to a dynasty that supposedly vanished a thousand years ago. This chapter is a dizzying deep-dive into the Heart-Mandala’s passive reality-warp, forcing both Li Huowang and the reader to sit with the nausea of not knowing which world is “real.” And because nothing ever stays simple, there’s eunuch perfume, boy-toys, and an old man having a stroke over the city’s moral decay. All in a day’s work for our favorite mad daoist.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang confronts Zhuge Yuan directly about the Heart-Mandala, demanding a straight answer for once. Zhuge Yuan deflects with a cryptic “can’t say” and points his fan toward the sky—because of course he does.
- The group reaches Youdu, where Li Huowang immediately notices the city looks identical to the Great Liang’s capital, down to the street layout, but the details are all subtly wrong.
- A jarring scene at a brothel quarter: cages that once held women now hold heavily made-up young men. Old Qin loses his temper, blaming the eunuchs for turning the capital into a den of pederasty.
- Two eunuchs stroll past arm-in-arm, reeking of perfume and urine, followed by a scholar-official who uses a boy as a mounting step for his cart. The subplot dripping with political rot.
- Once Zhuge Yuan moves far enough away, Li Huowang tests the effect: the boy-prostitutes revert to women; a passing woodcutter confirms it’s still the Great Liang. Proof that the Heart-Mandala’s reach is strictly localized.
- Li Huowang concludes the ability is harmless to him, files it away as a peculiarity tied to the “Three-Bodied Old One,” and hurries to catch up to Zhuge Yuan.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The **Heart-Mandala** (心蟠) in this chapter reveals itself as a passive, bounded domain—a localized bubble of reality warping that doesn’t require active effort from Zhuge Yuan. This is a fascinating twist on the usual xianxia “domain” trope, where cultivators deliberately exert pressure on their surroundings. Here it’s just *there*, like a chronic condition. The phrase “Three-Bodied Old One” hints at a higher cosmic entity—possibly the source of this ability, and a nod to ancient Chinese myths of primeval gods with multiple forms, like the Three Sovereigns or Pangu’s transformations.

The depiction of eunuchs and boy-prostitutes has deep roots in Chinese historical fiction and satire. The eunuch class, often vilified as corrupt and decadent, was a staple target of moral outrage in dynastic-era literature. The phenomenon of “male brothels” (the *xianggong* trade) was particularly noted during the Ming and Qing periods, especially in Beijing. The smell of perfume masking urine is a classic detail—a symbol of rot covered by fragrance, very much in line with the Dao-Twisted World’s love of visceral, sensory horror.

## Reading Guide

This is a “breather chapter” that does more than just move the plot—it sharpens a core existential question: what is reality when one man’s presence overwrites it? Watch how Li Huowang’s desperation shifts from wanting answers to *accepting limited safety*. His pragmatic decision to stop arguing and just trust that the Heart-Mandala isn’t lethal is a quiet but significant character beat. He’s learning to compartmentalize the impossible. Also, keep an eye on the political background radiation—the eunuch corruption isn’t just set-dressing; it’s a sign that the Great Qi / Great Liang schism has real-world consequences, and the Supervisory Heavenly Office may have more than one kind of stain to clean up.
