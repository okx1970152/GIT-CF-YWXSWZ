## Chapter Overview
The dinner party continues, but the mood shifts from celebration to cold, hard education. We don't get a spectacular fight scene or a ritual sacrifice this time—instead, we get something far more insidious: a lesson on how the Dao-Twisted World's criminal underworld *actually* works. Zhao Qin, our grizzled escort captain, drops some serious lore on Li Huowang about the Greenwood (绿林)—the vast and terrifying network of bandits, outlaws, and hidden powers that control the roads. And as the wine flows, Li Huowang learns that the "wild west" of this world is not ruled by muscle, but by a web of coded speech, political connections, and the ominous possibility that some bandits serve something far more sinister than just their own greed.

## Key Plot Points
- Gouwa pitches a practical plan to Li Huowang: using Zhao Qin's connections to become traveling escorts, earning money while heading to Liang Kingdom. It's a rare moment of strategic thinking from the usually sycophantic disciple.
- Zhao Qin enthusiastically agrees to vouch for them, but immediately warns that escort work is only 50% martial skill—the other 50% is "currying favor" and navigating relationships.
- Li Huowang rejects Zhao Qin's offer of unlimited silver, drawing a clear line: he saved the wife, but didn't fully cure her; asking for the moon would be crossing a boundary.
- Zhao Qin promises to teach Li Huowang "coded speech" (切口)—the underworld passwords used to negotiate safe passage with bandits.
- Li Huowang connects the dots: the river bandits' "companion, share your vine" line was indeed a challenge from the Greenwood's etiquette system.
- Zhao Qin drops a bombshell: behind the biggest bandit kings are often official connections, and many can be "amnestied" overnight—turning from black-hat outlaws into legitimate power players.
- The conversation turns dark when Li Huowang asks if these bandits use "evil arts or immortal techniques." The silence at the table, and Zhao Qin's guarded answer, confirms his worst suspicion: the *Thousand Greats Record*-wielding river bandits were not a fluke. The nightmare goes deeper.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Greenwood (绿林)**  
This term has deep roots in Chinese history and literature. Originally referring to the Green Woods Fort, a legendary bandit stronghold from the Western Han dynasty, "Greenwood" became a standard term for the outlaw underworld—the Robin Hood-esque realm of bandits, highwaymen, and mountain kings. In *Dao-Guai Yi-Xian*, Zhao Qin paints this world with gritty realism: not just a chaotic horde of thugs, but a structured, hierarchical society with its own rules, etiquette, and even political ties to the imperial court. The "black banner" (抗黑旗) is a clear signal that a group is operating in the open, defying the law.

**Coded Speech (切口)**  
"切口" (qiēkǒu) is the traditional jargon used by Chinese underworld societies—from theatrical troupes to criminal gangs—to communicate secretly in public. Think of it as a historical, real-world "thieves' cant." The example Li Huowang recalls—"companion, share your vine" (并肩子, 甩个蔓)—is a genuine piece of *jianghu* lingo from martial arts fiction. "Companion" means brother; "share your vine" means state your name. Failing to respond correctly labels you an outsider and an acceptable target. It's a verbal checkpoint, not just a bit of old-fashioned slang.

**Three Parts for Safe Passage (三分保平安)**  
Zhao Qin's mantra is a classic piece of *jianghu* wisdom, reflecting the real philosophy of traditional Chinese escort agencies. It emphasizes demeanor, compromise, and social lubrication over brute force. The idea is that an escort's primary weapon is not his blade, but his ability to smooth things over, read a room, and never let pride or anger close a negotiation. This flies in the face of the typical xianxia "I'll just kill them all" approach, underscoring the novel's commitment to grounded, strategic survival.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in worldbuilding through conversation. There's no action, no monsters, no cultivation breakthroughs—just a grizzled old veteran spilling the tea on how the *real* power games work. For Li Huowang, and for us as readers, this is a vital calibration moment. The world isn't just a gauntlet of demons and mad alchemists; it's also filled with human systems of control that are just as dangerous. The takeaway here is chilling: even if you can fight off a monster, you can't fight off an entire web of political patronage and coded etiquette. And the question that haunts the end of the chapter—"do serious bandits also use supernatural arts?"—is answered not with words, but with *silence*. Li Huowang is learning that the deeper he goes, the less room there is for simple solutions. These are the rules of the road, and they are not optional.
