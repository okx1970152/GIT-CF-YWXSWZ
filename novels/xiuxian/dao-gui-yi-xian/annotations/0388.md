## Chapter Overview

Zhang here, and I need a moment. This chapter is a brutal, disorienting gut-punch that perfectly embodies why we read this twisted masterpiece. The action doesn’t give you a second to breathe. Li Huowang just survived a terrifying underwater attack from a colony of “water monkeys” (水猴子)—only to lose his companion, be swallowed whole by something *much* larger, and slam headfirst into a new layer of White Tower reality. The transition is seamless, violent, and absolutely sickening—in the best way. Get ready. This is horror at its most visceral and intellectually maddening.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang fights off a swarm of water monkeys in the sea. The creatures are not supernatural in a grand sense—they’s just dozens of hair-covered, monkey-faced things with twisted limbs, a brutal, grounded horror.
- The bald, bearded monk-companion (who charged into the water full of faith) is found dead at the bottom. Half his face is ripped off. His eye is gone. The casual loss stings.
- As Li Huowang surfaces, a massive maw, larger than a house, filled with dark tentacles, swallows him whole.
- He wakes bound to a bed in White Tower Psychiatric Hospital—the “real” world or a hallucination within the monster’s gut.
- A simple-minded, middle-aged patient (who calls him “brother”) is his caretaker, offering him flowers and feeding him. Li Huowang immediately deduces that this is a trap *inside* the creature’s stomach.
- In a furious, cold-pragmatic escape, Li Huowang breaks his own hand to slip the restraints, knocks out a guard, takes electric baton hits to the chest, and leaps from the fourth floor.
- He lands on the grass, bleeding, grinning defiantly at the prison building: “Dream on!”

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Water Monkeys (水猴子, shuǐ hóuzi)**: In Chinese folklore, the *水猴子* is often synonymous with a *水鬼* (water ghost) or *河童* (kappa-like creature) that drowns people and drags them underwater. They are typically blamed for swimming accidents and are said to look like a hair-covered, monkey-faced creature with long claws. In this novel, the folk version is preserved almost exactly—but multiplied into a swarm, stripped of any supernatural grandeur. Just wet, stinking, *animalistic* malice. That’s the Dao-Twisted World for you: no gods, just hunger.

**Face-Changing (变脸, biànliǎn)**: Although not directly performed here, Li Huowang’s trauma with deception and transformation echoes this famous Sichuan opera technique. Performers flick through painted masks mid-song. In this novel, *any* change of face reads as betrayal.

**White Tower Hospital / Prison (白塔监狱)**: The name “White Tower” (白塔) is deliberately ambiguous—it could be a real place, or it could be a *memory-projection* of the White Jade Capital lodged inside the Dao-Twisted World’s hierarchy. The novel plays with the meta-structure: White Tower is the real-world psychiatric facility *and* the monster’s digestive illusion.

**Two-Layer Heart-Turbid Trap (一生一死心浊)**: A devastating concept introduced here. A dead Heart-Turbid hides a living one, and the living one hides inside the dead. It’s a karmic knot that encloses the victim in a fabricated version of reality, cutting them off from the real world. Li Huowang’s escape from the hospital—breaking his own bones, fighting with cold pragmatism—is him clawing through the trap’s outermost layer.

A paradoxical containment where a dead Heart-Turbid hides a living one, trapping victims in a fabricated karmic layer.

Traditional Sichuan opera technique of rapid mask-switching; in the Dao-Twisted World, it triggers Li Huowang’s paranoia about deception and transformation.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in **cognitive rupture**. The jump from underwater horror to sterile hospital ward is one of the series’ most jarring transitions—it forces you to question everything. Is this the real world? Is it the monster’s digestive hallucination? A projection of Li Huowang’s fractured mind? The answer is *all of the above*.

Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s reaction time. He doesn’t panic. He doesn’t despair. He *plans*. The moment he realizes this is a trap, he systematically breaks himself free—hand first, then leverage, then the leap. That’s not madness; that’s survival honed to a razor edge. The final grin at the tower wall is both triumph and mania. He’s not sure he’s out, but he’s sure he’s not dead.

Also note the minor character: the simple, honest patient who was brought in from the streets. He’s a tragic mirror—showing us what “innocence” looks like in a world that rewards only ruthless cunning. Li Huowang barely acknowledges him past using him as a tool. That’s the cost of survival.

**Spoiler-free warning**: The term “Two-Layer Heart-Turbid Trap” first surfaces here. It’s not just a cool name. It will become a recurring structural threat.
