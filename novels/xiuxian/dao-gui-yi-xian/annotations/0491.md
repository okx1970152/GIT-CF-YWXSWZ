## Chapter Overview
Alright, grab your popcorn—or maybe a stiff drink—because chapter 490 takes us on a wild, heartbreaking trip into Gao Zhijian’s fractured mind. Forget cosmic horrors and scheming cults for a moment; this chapter is a quiet, claustrophobic character study set in the mud and dirt of Niuxin Village. Chun Xiaoman and Zhao Wu try to decode Gao Zhijian’s past by watching him draw in the earth, and what they get is a jigsaw puzzle of a life that makes absolutely no logical sense. It’s messy, it’s raw, and by the end, you’ll feel the same confusion and pity that Chun Xiaoman does. Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this one’s a gut-punch of a different flavor.

## Key Plot Points
- Chun Xiaoman catches Gao Zhijian drawing elaborate pictures in the packed earth with his bare fingers. She tries to get him to talk, but he insists on showing, not telling.
- Gao Zhijian draws a dragon, an emperor, a mountain of gold ingots, and then a row of smaller figures. He points to the last one and slaps his own chest: “Me!”
- He draws a larger figure in a blood-red Daoist robe with three swords on its back. Chun Xiaoman immediately recognizes it as Li Huowang.
- He draws a ‘伍’ (five) character over the child version of himself, then slashes through the figure with a line. The implication: Li Huowang killed the five-year-old Gao Zhijian.
- He then draws version after version of himself dying: pierced by spears, drowned, burned, hanged. The list of deaths keeps growing.
- Finally, he draws a massive black circle that swallows everything. He becomes visibly terrified of his own drawing, stamping the dirt down to bury it all.
- Gao Zhijian then reveals the gold again, draws a line from it to Chun Xiaoman, and says “Take!”—offering to risk his identity for the group’s survival.
- Zhao Wu arrives and proposes a practical interpretation: Gao Zhijian was a noble, he has many dangerous ways to die associated with that past, and he’s willing to brave them to get money for the group.
- Gao Zhijian bluntly rejects this and insists he was the Emperor himself.
- Zhao Wu points out the logical holes: Great Liang has never had a five-year-old emperor, and Li Huowang couldn’t have killed a child Gao Zhijian since they’re roughly the same age.
- Overwhelmed, Gao Zhijian beats his own head in frustration, shouting “Chaos!” before being calmed down by Chun Xiaoman.
- He walks away, and Zhao Wu and Chun Xiaoman agree they must tell Li Huowang about this when he returns.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
The number ‘伍’ (wǔ, five) is used here as a classical financial anti-fraud character, identical in purpose to how ‘壹’, ‘贰’, ‘叁’, ‘肆’ replace ‘一’, ‘二’, ‘三’, ‘四’ in formal banking contexts. Chun Xiaoman recognizing this as the simplest of the set tells us she’s been learning from a traditional curriculum, not just street slang.

The image of a five-year-old emperor is, as Zhao Wu points out, absurd in real-world Chinese history, where child emperors existed but were rarely under six and never an only son of a reigning monarch without a regency crisis. This deliberate contradiction is the key to Gao Zhijian’s tragedy: his memory may not be of *this* dynasty, *this* timeline, or even *this* identity.

The “black circles” covering all previous drawings deserve special attention. In traditional Chinese feng shui and exorcistic practice, sealing a negative image by burying it under earth is a protective act—Gao Zhijian is literally trying to bury his past, but the fear he shows suggests the past is not done with him.

## Reading Guide
When you’re reading this chapter, don’t try to solve the puzzle. Gao Zhijian isn’t giving a coherent testimony—he’s showing a scarred psyche, not a clean timeline. The horror here isn’t a monster or a plot twist; it’s the quiet, desperate frustration of a man who can’t even tell his own story without it collapsing into contradictions. Much like Li Huowang’s own reality crisis, Gao Zhijian’s “I might be the emperor but also I might be false” is the quietest screaming you’ll ever read. Keep your eyes on Chun Xiaoman’s reactive, human confusion—she’s the reader’s avatar, trying to be patient and failing, and that failure is the most honest thing in the chapter.

## Deeper Dive: The Stutter and the Silence
Gao Zhijian’s stutter is not just a speech impediment—it’s a narrative device that forces him to communicate through art rather than words. Every time he opens his mouth, reality threatens to leak out wrong. The drawing allows him to show layered, contradictory truths (emperor, victim, corpse, survivor) simultaneously, which spoken language cannot. When the words finally come (“Chaos!”), they’re not a revelation—they’re a surrender. The dirt he stamps down is the same dirt he would bury a memory in.
