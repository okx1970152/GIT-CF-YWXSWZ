## Chapter Overview

Fellow Daoists, our boy Li Huowang is having a rough week. After a cryptic bone-divination session yields a hexagram with no instruction manual—just the charming suggestion of “burn the paper, then eat the ash”—he’s left staring at a skull like it owes him money. But a chance encounter with a magistrate’s daughter yields a crucial breakthrough: a sachet embroidered with a pattern that mirrors his oracle. The craftswoman? A suspiciously fragile-looking “embroidery maiden” with a startling secret. Get ready for wooden torture implements, a gender reveal that nobody asked for, and a Li Huowang who is *very* good with a saw.

## Key Plot Points

* Li Huowang attempts a bone-divination ritual from his new talisman manual. The hexagram is marked “Heaven above, Heaven below—Heaven’s Purity,” but the manual gives no interpretation—only the instruction to burn the talisman and swallow the ash.
* After consuming the ash, nothing happens. Frustrated, Li Huowang nearly gives up until the magistrate’s daughter appears with a green sachet.
* The sachet’s embroidery pattern partially matches Li Huowang’s divination result. He immediately demands to see the artisan: a “Embroidery Maiden” who appears frail and soft-spoken.
* Under Li Huowang’s pressure, the maiden breaks down—and her voice shifts from female to male mid-sentence. A quick pat-down confirms the truth: the “maiden” is a man in disguise.
* In the prison, Li Huowang applies his signature brand of brutal interrogation. After half a shichen of creative persuasion, he’s convinced that the cross-dressing rapist is telling the truth: he committed the assaults, but the infant thefts were someone else’s work.
* Li Huowang realizes the two crimes only *seemed* connected because they occurred at the same time. The real culprit is still out there, and his lead is dead.
* The chapter ends on a darkly comic note: a terrified guard, kicked by his superior, forces a smile and praises Li Huowang’s “handiwork” as he sees him out.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Bone Divination (骨卜)** is an ancient Chinese practice predating the bronze age. Oracle bones—usually ox scapulae or turtle plastrons—were heated until they cracked, and the resulting fissures were interpreted as divine messages. The Shang Dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE) was particularly famous for this practice, using it for everything from harvest predictions to military campaigns. In the Dao-Twisted World, Li Huowang’s version uses a human skull and a *talisman manual*, which is both a neat callback to classical roots and a grotesque reinvention.

**The Hexagram Pattern**: The sequence “Heaven’s Purity, Retreat, Escape” corresponds to three of the 64 hexagrams in the *I Ching* (Book of Changes). The manual’s refusal to provide a reading—only the instruction to burn and swallow the ash—is a deliberate subversion of traditional divination. In the Dao-Twisted World, knowledge is often incomplete, dangerous, or both. You don’t *interpret* the oracle; you *consume* it.

**The True Sutra of the Fire Vestments (火袄真经)**: This is the sacred text of the Ao-Jing Sect, introduced earlier in the story. When recited after torture or injury, it produces a healing effect—a fire-slug that seals wounds with agonizing precision. It’s a brutal, transactional form of medicine that perfectly fits the novel’s aesthetic: healing that hurts, salvation that scars.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in procedural horror. The bone-divination scene is slow, weird, and deeply satisfying in its mundanity—Li Huowang is essentially a detective trying to read a *chicken bone*, and he’s as frustrated as we are. The moment with the sachet is a classic *puzzle box* reveal: the hexagram isn’t read; it’s *lived*. Also, shoutout to the poor magistrate’s daughter, who just wanted some fatherly affection and instead got a 10 on the Trauma Richter scale.

The torture scene is brutal but efficient—no lingering on gore, just the clinical satisfaction of Li Huowang cross-referencing his subject’s pain responses. The reveal that the rapist is telling the truth is a genuine twist: the crimes are unrelated, and the case is colder than ever. That’s the *Dao-Twisted World* for you—sometimes the monster isn’t hiding. It just hasn’t shown itself yet.

And that final guard’s line—“Excellency, your handiwork is truly remarkable!”—is the kind of black comedy that makes this novel sing. Even in a cesspool of torture and impostors, there’s a guy trying to be polite.
