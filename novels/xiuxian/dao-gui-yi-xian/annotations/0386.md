## Chapter Overview

Get ready, because something has shifted. The seas are changing, Li Huowang is heading toward a reunion that fills him with a terrifying cocktail of emotions, and the wider world of the Great Liang is starting to show its scars. This chapter is a masterclass in eerie calm before the storm. We watch Li Huowang navigate mundane seaside life—tasting raw-marinated seafood, brushing off a shady card player, watching a fight break out over cheated bets—but the author keeps tightening the screws. A warship the size of a mountain glides past, its brass lion heads glinting like it just came back from killing something big. Li Huowang puts the pieces together in his head: the Chief Recorder, the Heart-Turbid hunt, the “big thing” the court is clearly mobilizing for. He feels it, the weight of something enormous grinding into motion. And then, to top it all off, the Black Tai Sui snatches a kid’s fried dough with its tentacle, and Li Huowang’s only reaction is to watch it lick a sticky handprint off his robe. This is a man who has stopped even flinching at the body horror inside him. The chapter ends with a smile from a little girl who thinks the tentacle is the coolest thing she’s ever seen. It’s unsettling, it’s domestic, and it’s the calmest part of the storm.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang arrives in Huating City, tries raw-marinated seafood, and gets a good night’s sleep before boarding a fuchuan headed for Apricot Island.
- The boat captain grills Li Huowang about his surname; Li Huowang tries “Er” (ear) as a joke but is forced to give the name “Bai” to board.
- Li Huowang reflects on his upcoming reunion with Zhuge Yuan, feeling a complex mix of suspicion, excitement, joy, and curiosity. He touches his belly (where the Black Tai Sui resides) for reassurance, acknowledging he is no longer the same person who left Qingfeng Temple.
- A man with a dog-plaster patch tries to recruit Li Huowang for a game of leaf cards (a type of hand-held mahjong), but Li Huowang coldly refuses.
- A massive Great Liang warship passes the fuchuan, its brass lion-head cannons clearly visible, its hull scarred from recent battle. Everyone except Li Huowang immediately prostrates in fear.
- The warship is rumored to be returning from overseas, searching for something—a detail that resonates with Li Huowang’s own Heart-Turbid mission with the Chief Recorder.
- During the voyage, Li Huowang observes the card players and learns their habits: the dog-plaster man cheats, but the bald, bearded man wins through pure skill. A fight breaks out when the cheating is discovered.
- A little girl offers Li Huowang her half-eaten fried dough. When the Black Tai Sui’s tentacle snatches it, she is delighted, not frightened.
- The girl’s mother is terrified and bows repeatedly before rushing away. Mantou the dog licks the sticky handprint off Li Huowang’s robe.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is a fantastic intersection of two cultures that feel miles apart but are really just two sides of the same Dao-Twisted coin: the *worldly* side and the *body-horror side*. Let’s break it down.

First, the **fuchuan (福船)** . These are historically specific Chinese sailing vessels, known for their sturdy, flat-bottomed hulls designed for coastal and deep-sea navigation. They were the workhorses of the maritime silk road. In the novel, the casual mention grounds the setting in a recognizable historical texture—before the horror kicks in.

Then, there’s the **warship with brass lion heads**. These aren’t just decorations; they represent the imposing centralized military power of the Great Liang dynasty. The fact that everyone *immediately* prostrates is a reminder of the raw, unquestioned authority of the state in this world. In traditional Chinese culture, failure to kneel before an official or military procession could be punishable by death. Li Huowang not kneeling is a subtle act of defiance—or perhaps just the numbness of someone who has stared down gods and demons and no longer fears mere soldiers.

The game of **leaf cards (叶子牌)** is a historical precursor to modern mahjong. It was a popular gambling game during the Ming and Qing dynasties. The fact that Li Huowang quickly learns to spot the cheater shows his survival instinct has evolved into sharp, practical social observation—a crucial skill in a world where everyone might be lying.

Finally, the **Black Tai Sui’s sudden snatching of the fried dough** is a masterful bit of body horror integrated into domestic life. Instead of screaming, the little girl giggles, thinking it's a toy. This is pure *Dao Gui Yi Xian*: the mundane and the monstrous are no longer separate categories. Li Huowang doesn’t even react; he just accepts it. That’s the terrifying character development—he’s becoming as much a part of the twisted world as the tentacle inside him.

## Reading Guide

This is a “breather chapter,” but don’t be fooled. In *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, a breather is just the author sharpening their knife before the next cut. Li Huowang’s emotional state here is fascinating. He *should* be excited to see Zhuge Yuan—his only real ally in this hellscape—but the chapter deliberately leaves the reunion shrouded in vague “suspicion.” That’s because Li Huowang has been burned too many times. He can’t trust happiness anymore. Every reunion in this book has come with a cost, and the reader is conditioned to expect the other shoe to drop.

Pay attention to the wider worldbuilding. The Great Liang warship isn’t just a set piece. It tells us the state is mobilizing massive resources to “find something” overseas. This dovetails perfectly with the Supervisory Heavenly Office’s hunt for the Heart-Element and the Heart-Turbid. What is the court building? What is the Siming system preparing for? Li Huowang senses it, but he’s smart enough to file it away under “not my problem right now.”

Also, the little girl. She’s a mirror of Li Huowang’s own lost innocence. She sees the Black Tai Sui’s tentacle and laughs. She’s too young to know she should be terrified. Li Huowang’s silence as the dog licks her handprint off his robe is a quiet, aching moment. He’s not just desensitized to horror—he’s become something that children find *fun*. That’s a new kind of lonely, and it hits harder than any monster chase.

Get ready. The reunion with Zhuge Yuan is coming, and in this book, reunions are never just happy. They’re traps, tests, or goodbyes in disguise.
