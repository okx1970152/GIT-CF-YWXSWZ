## Chapter Overview

In this harrowing chapter, Li Huowang is forced to confront the limits of his dual-reality existence. Danyangzi introduces the Black Tai Sui—a writhing, parasitic entity that is both a tool of suppression and a grotesque emblem of the cave world's horror. As the substance is forced down his throat, Li Huowang desperately escapes into his modern reality, only to find that his words inadvertently shatter that safe haven. The chapter culminates in an extended sequence of raw grief and helplessness, as Li Huowang realizes that neither world offers him true sanctuary. This is a turning point where his identity as both patient and disciple becomes a prison, and the Black Tai Sui becomes the symbol of his inescapable affliction. The stark contrast between the sterile hospital and the crude cave amplifies the psychological terror, leaving the reader to question what is real and what is madness.

## Key Plot Points

1. Danyangzi reveals the Black Tai Sui, a slimy, animate entity that was previously seen inside the black cauldron eating people. He orders Li Huowang to swallow it to suppress his madness.
2. Li Huowang resists, but two disciple Daoists, Chang Ming and Chang Ren, hold him down. In desperation, he uses his mind to force a transition back to his modern reality.
3. He finds himself in the hospital, embraced by Yang Na and his mother. The doctor prescribes antipsychotics (haloperidol and perphenazine), which Li Huowang takes eagerly.
4. Relieved, Li Huowang jokes that the voice in his hallucination claimed that world was real and this was the illusion. This statement triggers a catastrophic collapse of the hospital environment: colors drain, people freeze, and the sky turns black.
5. Yang Na and his mother disappear like bursting soap bubbles, leaving Li Huowang alone in pure despair.
6. The hospital walls recede like a tide, and he is pulled back into the cave, now with the Black Tai Sui lodged in his throat.
7. He tries to scream but cannot; all the pain rebounds onto him. Danyangzi declares him cured and leaves with his disciples, ordering him to report monthly.
8. The other human ingredients in the room stare in confusion. After half an incense stick of time, the Black Tai Sui finally slides down, and Li Huowang breaks down in wailing grief, asking why the sickness still haunts him even in this strange world.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

"Tai Sui" (太岁) is a term deeply rooted in Chinese folk religion and astronomy. Traditionally, it refers to the star Jupiter (the Year Star) and the associated deity believed to govern fortune and misfortune for the year. The phrase "to disturb the Tai Sui" (太岁头上动土) warns against acting recklessly in the face of powerful forces. In this novel, however, the Black Tai Sui is a grotesque, living parasite—a physical manifestation of corrupt alchemy. It consumes flesh and suppresses madness, inverting the protective or ominous role of the traditional Tai Sui into a tangible horror. The "human ingredients" (人药引) concept draws from the alchemical practice of using rare human constitutions as raw materials for elixirs. Historically, such practices existed in more symbolic or metaphorical forms, but the novel literalizes them to create visceral body horror. Li Huowang's transformation from "ingredient" to disciple does not free him from being treated as a subject; he remains a vessel for Danyangzi's experiments.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in emotional whiplash. Li Huowang swings from terror to relief to utter despair within a few pages, and the reader is forced to ride that wave with him. Pay close attention to the sensory details: the sticky, gritty texture of the Black Tai Sui and the capsules; the clean white hospital versus the dark, earthy cave. The moment where Li Huowang jokes about the hallucination being real is the pivot point—his words, spoken in a moment of casual relief, unmask the fragility of his safe reality. The hospital collapse is not caused by external forces but by his own cognitive dissonance. This reinforces the novel's central theme: the boundary between worlds is porous, and belief is a dangerous weapon. Danyangzi's final line, "The madness is gone," is chilling because we know it is a lie—the Black Tai Sui does not cure madness; it only silences it, forcing the pain inward. Li Huowang's final scream is the sound of a man who has lost all hope of escape.

## The Symbolism of Black Tai Sui {#black-tai-sui}

The Black Tai Sui in this chapter serves as the nexus of several key themes: body horror, corrupted alchemy, and the crushing weight of Li Huowang's illness. In traditional Chinese belief, Tai Sui is often depicted as a powerful spirit that must be appeased. Here, it is a parasitic mass that is simultaneously medicine and curse. Its texture (slimy, squirming) and its behavior (eating people, blocking throats) make it a perfect symbol for the Dao-Twisted World: everything sacred is twisted into something repulsive. The fact that Danyangzi uses it to "cure" Li Huowang highlights the moral inversion of this world—the cure is indistinguishable from poison. For readers unfamiliar with Chinese mythology, understanding the original Tai Sui concept intensifies the revulsion: the familiar is made monstrous.
