## Chapter Overview
Chapter 268, “On the Road,” finds our weary Daoist protagonist, Li Huowang, settling into a rhythm of grim pragmatism. After the chaos of Qingqiu, he’s now a traveling troupe leader, trading on whispers and his own fractured instincts. A chance overheard word—“plague”—snags his attention, setting his sights on the mysterious Pi County. The chapter is a masterclass in the novel’s unique brand of quiet horror: the supernatural threat is a nebulous, off-screen unease, while the real, bleeding wound is the grinding poverty of the world. We see a brief moment of humanity—a small, charitable act—that starkly contrasts with Li Huowang’s survival-driven, almost predatory focus. Get ready for a chapter that’s less about flashy fights and more about the weight of a single decision on a long, dusty road.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang overhears two men discussing a “plague” in Pi County, a place he’s never heard of before. His interest is piqued by the potential to find the Supervisory Heavenly Office there.
- He bribes a local with silver to learn more. The man describes the people of Pi County as “jumpy” and having sickly complexions, which is why rumors of a plague started—even though there isn’t one.
- Li Huowang checks his map and confirms that Pi County is on his route. With no other options, he decides to go investigate the strange mass behavior.
- He informs Lü Zhuangyuan that they will stop performing after today and resume their journey tomorrow. The troupe has a new, prosperous energy after their successful shows.
- As the group makes camp, Li Huowang notices a pair of desperately poor migrant harvesters who can only afford to eat dry cornbread and cold stream water. He orders Bai Lingmiao to bring them two hot bowls of rice stew, a rare moment of empathy.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Migrant Harvester (麦客):** These are a real historical phenomenon in China, especially in the northern provinces. During the harvest season, landless peasants would travel in groups to areas where the wheat was ready, hiring themselves out for a few days of back-breaking labor before moving on to the next field. They carried their own sickles and bedding, often sleeping in the open. Their existence is defined by extreme poverty, constant movement, and vulnerability. In this novel, they serve as a visual reminder of the brutal, non-magical layers of suffering that define the Dao-Twisted World—a suffering that is even more pervasive, if less dramatic, than the supernatural kind.

**The “Plague” as a Social Marker:** In pre-modern China, the fear of plague was a powerful and real force. However, here the “plague” label is applied to behavioral strangeness—people being “jumpy” and “sallow.” This reflects a fascinating social dynamic: unusual collective behavior, whether psychological or supernatural, would immediately be pathologized as a disease. It’s a perfect example of how the world’s inhabitants try to rationalize the inexplicable horrors of their reality.

## Reading Guide
After the epic set-pieces of the Zhengde Temple and Qingqiu arcs, this chapter acts as a necessary, inhalatory pause. It’s like the moment in a mountain climb when you stop on a ledge to check your gear and drink water. The tension isn’t in a sword fight, but in the quiet choice Li Huowang makes. He doesn't go to Pi County to save it, but to *use* it as a lead. Keep an eye on this as a key character beat: he has become a true survivor of this world, seeing people and problems as tools or stepping stones. Still, his final act is telling. That small gesture of feeding the harvesters is a flicker of the old Li Huowang, a man who, despite everything, still chooses to see the hungry. It’s a small, beautiful moment of grace in a story that so often denies it.
