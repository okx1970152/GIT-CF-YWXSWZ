## Chapter Overview

Our weary crew, still nursing the wounds of their last escape, hits a very mortal wall: the pantry is empty. With food and funds running on fumes, Li Huowang faces a familiar devil’s bargain—stoop to banditry or fall to starvation. This chapter trades the screaming horror of last volume for a quieter, grimmer grind. A desperate gamble from one of the rescued women leads them up a punishing climb to a flat-topped hamlet that looks like a paradise… but this is the Dao-Twisted World, and no peace comes without a price. Pack your hiking boots, fellow Daoists—this one’s a slow burn with a view.

## Key Plot Points

- The group halts to rest, suffering from severe exhaustion and dwindling supplies. Bai Lingmiao tries to give Li Huowang her share of flatbread, but he firmly refuses, showing his protective awareness of her larger appetite.
- Li Huowang mentally rejects both robbing bandits (fear of the convict-soldiers) and preying on civilians, hardening his identity as “not Danyangzi.” His resource dilemma is his chief obstacle.
- Gouwa openly complains about the rescued women as useless baggage, sparking a bitter argument. This moment reveals growing strain over limited resources.
- A large-mouthed woman among the rescued begs not to be abandoned, offering to lead the group to her fourth aunt’s place for grain.
- The group follows her up a brutal, pathless climb to a flat-topped mountain, which, upon reaching the summit, reveals a surprising green village—a rare pocket of fertile life in Shu.
- The chapter closes on a sour note: the fourth aunt’s family shuts their door upon learning their relatives came to borrow grain, and the large-mouthed woman weeps in terror of being cast out.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Universal Fear of Borrowing Grain (借粮):** In a pre-modern agricultural society, especially one plagued by heavy taxes and conscription, “borrowing grain” was a loaded request. It wasn’t just asking for food—it was a public admission of desperation that could shame the borrower and strain the lender’s own precarious household. The aunt’s door-slamming isn’t just selfishness; it’s survival instinct. For Western readers, think of it as a subsistence farmer refusing a loan they can’t afford—except the penalty for refusal here could mean watching someone starve.

**Door Gods (门神):** The detail of the gate being “pasted over with Door Gods” is a classic Chinese folk practice: images of fearsome generals (often Wei Chi Gong or Qin Qiong) or deified heroes are pasted on doors during Lunar New Year to repel evil spirits and misfortune. Here, the presence of these protective deities contrasts bitterly with the woman’s rejection—the gods guard against demons, but they can’t open a door for a starving relative. It’s a jarring bit of folk-horror irony.

**Taxes and Conscription in Shu (后蜀):** The novel’s setting draws on the historical context of the Later Shu kingdom (934–965 AD), a period of fragmentation and heavy rural exploitation. Characters reference “avoiding taxes and military service” as the reason for the hilltop existence—a direct window into the suffering of common folk in this period. Lü Zhuangyuan’s smug line about his hometown only taking 50% is black comedy; 50% was still brutal.

### Borrowing grain (借粮)

In pre-modern China, a direct request for food from another household was a heavy social plea that could bring public shame to the borrower and strain the lender’s own survival. It was not a casual favor.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in “mundane horror.” No monsters, no rituals, no screaming—just the slow, grinding dread of a group running out of food and trust. Pay attention to *how* everyone cracks: Gouwa’s passive-aggressive entitlement, the rescued women’s fear, and Li Huowang’s cold, calculated refusal to become Danyangzi’s shadow. Also note the geographical tension—the climb to the “paradise” hamlet is physically punishing, and the lush green at the summit almost feels like a trick. Any fan of survival-horror (like The Walking Dead or The Road) will feel right at home in these frictions. The real horror here isn’t a flesh-ghoul—it’s a closed door and a sobbing woman who knows she might be abandoned for being inconvenient.

## Backwoods Realpolitik

The chapter also drops some delicious, uncomfortable politics: Lü Zhuangyuan measures his own worth by comparing himself to *even poorer* peasants. It’s a small, almost throwaway moment, but it reveals a crucial survival psychology in the Dao-Twisted World—hierarchy isn’t just about magic or rank; it’s about who has *less*. And Li Huowang’s absolute refusal to prey on civilians (“I am not Danyangzi”) is his moral anchor in a world that constantly tempts him to cut corners. These quiet scenes are the psychological scaffolding that makes the big horrors matter.
