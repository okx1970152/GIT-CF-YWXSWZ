## Chapter Overview
Ah, a moment of rare peace in the Dao-Twisted World—but you *know* that never lasts, fellow traveler. This chapter is a quiet breather, a slice-of-life interlude where the Lü troupe celebrates a newborn, and Li Huowang gets a moment to just *breathe* without the world trying to eat him. He's feeling the Black Tai Sui's stabilizing effect, enjoying the absence of hallucinations, and even admitting—with real, aching nostalgia—that he kind of wished the modern world was real. But that peaceful feeling? It's a trap. A tiny black speck on a mountaintop starts moving, and by the chapter's end, that speck is getting closer. The calm before the storm is over.

## Key Plot Points
- Lü Zhuangyuan drops the act and reveals his true motive for being so deferential: he sees Li Huowang as the Lü family's "big break," hoping his son can also get a divine ability granted to him.
- During a midday rest, the troupe practices their opera basics, showing their professionalism even in displacement.
- Luo Juan goes into labor; the women assist while the men keep their distance, and Bai Lingmiao volunteers to observe, hinting she's thinking about her own future with Li Huowang.
- Li Huowang enjoys a moment of hallucination-free peace, reflecting on how the Black Tai Sui is working, and admits to his spectral companions (the monk, Peng Longteng, and Jin Shan Zhao) that he kind of wishes the modern world was real.
- He experiences a sudden auditory spike after pulling a scab from his ear, forcing him to stuff his ears with wool to block out the overwhelming noise.
- The baby is born—a healthy boy—and Li Huowang is confused by how ugly newborns look until Chun Xiaoman explains it's normal.
- Just as the group celebrates, Li Huowang spots a black speck on a distant mountaintop that has moved closer since he first saw it. He orders an immediate evacuation.
- As night falls, the black shape continues to close in, ending the chapter on a note of impending dread.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Red-Dyed Eggs (红鸡蛋)**: Lü Zhuangyuan's promise to hand out red-dyed eggs is a classic Chinese tradition for celebrating a newborn. The eggs are boiled and dyed red with beetroot or food coloring, then distributed to friends and family as a symbol of good fortune and the "rebirth" of the family line. In the Dao-Twisted World, even this joyful custom carries an edge—he promises to "make up for it" later, as if the party can't even stop for a proper celebration.

**Newborn Appearance**: Li Huowang's shock at the baby's looks is a universal joke, but Chun Xiaoman's explanation that "all newborns look like this" grounds it in real biology. Human babies are born with soft, moldable skulls and wrinkled, slightly blueish skin, and they look nothing like the cherubic three-month-olds we see in media. The author uses this moment to show Li Huowang's *lack* of normal life experience—his only reference point for babies is the sanitized, post-natal version.

**Ear scab and sensory overload**: The scene where Li Huowang pulls a scab from his ear and becomes overwhelmed by sensory input is a subtle reference to the novel's ongoing horror of the body. The scab isn't a normal piece of wax—it's a physical manifestation of his contamination, something that was *blocking* the world's noise, and removing it unleashes a torrent of sensation he can't handle. He stuffs his ears with sheep's wool as a crude, practical solution, showing his growing resourcefulness in managing his own broken biology.

## Reading Guide
This is one of those "calm before the storm" chapters that the *Dao-Twisted World* does so well. The entire first half is deceptively peaceful: a baby is born, the old man is scheming for his family's future, and Li Huowang is actually *happy* for a moment. But the horror fan in you knows what's coming. The black speck on the hill is a classic horror trope—a distant threat that only the protagonist notices, growing steadily closer while everyone else is distracted by joy. Li Huowang's instinct to run immediately, without waiting to confirm the danger, shows how far he's come. He's no longer the confused victim; he's the paranoid leader who trusts his gut over appearances. Pay attention to the way the author uses sensory details—the sound of grass growing after the scab is removed, the wool in his ears—to build a world that is *too* real, too overwhelming for a normal mind. And that final line, where the black shape is getting closer as night falls? That's your cliffhanger. Whatever's coming, it's going to be bad.
