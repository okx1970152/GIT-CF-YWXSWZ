## Chapter Overview
Back on the move, Li Huowang finally catches a break from the constant supernatural terror… only to find his paranoia has upgraded to a professional skill. A quiet day of rest turns into an information-gathering operation when he interrogates the village simpleton and gets a *very* interesting piece of news: one of the wedding guests from the feast was a soldier stationed nearby, the brother of the groom. This chapter is a masterclass in folk-horror worldbuilding through mundane conversation. No demons, no blood rituals—just a weary escort chief learning how the world’s power structures work, one stealthy question at a time.

## Key Plot Points
- The group enjoys a rare rest day; Li Huowang notes with quiet satisfaction that they can finally cook a proper meal.
- Li Huowang debriefs Gao Zhijian on the night watch; no supernatural incidents are reported, but Li Huowang’s unease about the wedding feast persists.
- He sends Dogwa, Gao Zhijian, and Chun Xiaoman to collect more dry rations from the village, a sign of his growing logistical discipline.
- Li Huowang practices the *True Sutra of the Fire Vestments* but still struggles to gather “pity” as a mental resource.
- He notices someone watching his lodging and discovers it is the village guardian (the simpleton from the wedding feast).
- Through careful bribing with steamed buns, he gleans critical intel: the man with the gong was “Hou the Second,” a soldier who had returned for his brother’s wedding.
- Li Huowang puzzles over how the official world deals with the supernatural horrors of the Dao-Twisted World.
- The groom’s father approaches Li Huowang with a request to deliver a message to his daughter in Ji City.
- During the conversation, the old man proudly reveals that his son serves as a “short-sword”—an elite bodyguard to a general, a position of significant local power.
- The chapter closes on a note of creeping unease about the real-world military presence and the upcoming journey to Ji City.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Short-Sword (短兵, duǎn bīng):** In ancient Chinese military terminology, “short-sword” doesn’t just mean a short weapon; it specifically refers to the *personal guard* of a commanding general, the soldiers who fight at his side in close quarters during pitched battles. The idiom “短兵相接” (short-swords clash) literally means “fighting at close quarters” or hand-to-hand combat, describing the moment when two armies’ generals finally meet in the chaos. When the old man says his son is a *short-sword*, he is bragging that his son is an elite warrior trusted to protect a high-ranking officer—a position of genuine prestige, not just a random soldier.

**Village Guardian (守村人, shǒu cūn rén):** A real folk tradition in certain parts of rural China. It’s believed that a village can “keep” (养) one mentally or developmentally disabled person who acts as a spiritual lightning rod—bearing misfortune on behalf of the entire community. In exchange, the village tolerates and feeds them. Li Huowang’s skepticism (“A real disaster? Can a simpleton really stop it?”) echoes the pragmatic reality behind the superstition, while Gao Zhijian’s earlier mention shows how even cynical people in the Dao-Twisted World hedge their bets with folk beliefs.

**Coarse Grain Steamed Buns (杂粮馒头, zá liáng mántóu):** A staple food of the common people in historical and rural China, made from mixed grains like sorghum, millet, or buckwheat instead of refined wheat. They’re denser, less sweet, and more filling. Li Huowang’s satisfaction at eating “comfortable hot food” and his use of these humble buns as interrogation currency tells you something about the material reality of this world: food is survival, and a single steamed bun is a fair price for a secret.

## Reading Guide
This is a *palate cleanser* chapter, and a deceptively important one. It does three things beautifully: First, it shows Li Huowang’s growing competence as a leader—he’s resting his people, gathering intel, and managing logistics, all without panic or spectacle. Second, it deepens the world’s political texture. The soldier’s existence raises uncomfortable questions: What does a “normal” military do in a world where gods and monsters are real? Are they just meat for the grinder, or are there countermeasures Li Huowang doesn’t know about yet? Third, the old man’s casual boast about his son being a general’s bodyguard lands as a low-key power flex. Li Huowang is now carrying a message to a household connected to someone who fights *right next to* a general. That’s not nothing. Get ready, fellow readers—the road to Ji City just got a little more complicated.
