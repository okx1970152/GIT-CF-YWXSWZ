## Chapter Overview
Get ready, dear readers, because Li Huowang is back on the hunt, and this time the target is the ever-elusive Zuowandao. Our favorite paranoid protagonist arrives at Ganyuan Village, a place that looks *too* normal after the horrors of Pi County. And as any veteran of the Dao-Twisted World knows, "normal" is the biggest red flag of all. Slipping into his invisible phantom form, Li Huowang stalks the village and witnesses a deeply unsettling ritual: a faceless mud Bodhisattva being paraded around, the crowd's joy a thin veneer over something rotten. The chapter builds atmosphere like a master—quiet dread seeping in through every detail—before locking Li Huowang in a dark shrine, alone with a melted deity and the creeping uncertainty of whether the voice he just heard was real or just another crack in his own mind.

## Key Plot Points
- The group arrives at Ganyuan Village following a tip about possible Zuowandao activity. The village appears idyllic and cheerful, a stark contrast to the trauma of Pi County, which immediately puts Li Huowang on high alert.
- Li Huowang opts to scout ahead alone using his phantom dislocation ability, leaving the rest of the group behind. Bai Lingmiao notices his growing paranoia but hesitates to press him.
- He observes a village-wide celebration centered around a mud Bodhisattva statue. The statue's face has completely melted into featureless streaks, leaving only two black pits. The villagers parade it around the village three times, draping it in red fabric.
- As night falls, Li Huowang breaks into the shrine. Inside, he finds the faceless Bodhisattva flanked by the Eighteen Arhats and two attendant statues (a boy and a girl), both of which appear to have moved on their own.
- In the oppressive silence, Li Huowang hears a garbled, chaotic voice that may be a hallucination or the shrine's own inhabitant. He is left frozen, unable to trust his own senses.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
Let's break down the "Mud Bodhisattva" (泥菩萨)—this is a *heavy* piece of Chinese folk-religion imagery. In common parlance, the phrase **"mud Bodhisattva crossing the river"** (泥菩萨过河) means "can't even save itself," implying one is too vulnerable to help others. This proverb frames the entire ritual with irony: a powerless god being exalted by a fearful community. But the novel weaponizes the image further. The melted, faceless face is pure body-horror, stripping the Bodhisattva of its traditional compassion and turning it into an abject, silent witness. The three circuits of "in and out" of the shrine are also significant—this mimics the "three petitions" structure used in some folk rituals to invite a deity's presence, suggesting the villagers are actively calling something *into* that statue. Finally, the **Eighteen Arhats** (十八罗汉) are standard Buddhist temple guardians, but here their fully-rendered, expressive faces intentionally contrast with the blankness of the central deity, creating a visual uncanny valley. In Dao-Twisted World terms, this whole setup screams "trap baited with folk piety."

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in *atmospheric dread*. There’s no explicit monster, no battle scene—just an invisible man watching a disturbingly cheerful village perform a ritual that feels *just* wrong enough to make your skin crawl. Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s mental state here: he’s not acting out of pure paranoia; he’s actively weighing his options ("Should I just cut the statue?") and testing his abilities (the phantom forms). The real horror lies in that final moment of silence—the inability to distinguish between the enemy's voice and the illness's voice. This is the Dao-Twisted World's most insidious weapon: making the protagonist distrust his own diagnosis. If you loved the slow-burn tension of the Pi County arc, this is a refined, tighter version of that same energy. Keep your eyes on those attendant statues. *They moved.*
