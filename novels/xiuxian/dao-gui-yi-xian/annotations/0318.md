## Chapter Overview

Welcome back, fellow seekers of the twisted path! This chapter is a masterclass in *atmospheric dread*—the chill before the fever breaks. Our boy Li Huowang has traded zombie-bashing for something far more unsettling: *paperwork*. Yep, the Supervisory Heavenly Office experience is NOT all monster guts and screaming cultists. Sometimes it’s a magistrate’s office, a cup of cold tea, and a pile of case files that reek of a quiet, spreading horror. Cangshui County looks normal—a little tense, perhaps—but beneath the curfew and the watchful constables, a pattern emerges that turns a simple crime spree into a systemic nightmare. And when the clues run dry, Li Huowang does what any self-respecting Daoist demon-hunter would do: he asks for a human skull and starts drawing talismans. Get ready to see the Master of the Office pivot from detective to diviner, and the result is as grim as it is fascinating.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang arrives in Cangshui County, a town under curfew due to recent crimes, and is escorted directly to the magistrate’s yamen.
- Magistrate Lou presents the initial case file: nine missing infants and five violated women, though he admits the real number of violations is likely much higher due to underreporting.
- Li Huowang sees through the surface—this kind of isolated crime wouldn’t trigger an Office response. He presses the magistrate, forcing him to reveal the *real* picture.
- A map is unfurled: the pattern does not stop at Cangshui. Fifteen surrounding counties are experiencing identical crimes, with a total of fifty-seven infants lost and growing, plus spreading civil unrest and rumor-mongering.
- Li Huowang attempts to interview a victim, but she is too traumatized to provide any useful details: no memory of the violator, no timeline. He feels the limits of his investigative skill.
- Turning to his talisman manual, Li Huowang requests a human skull from the local constables to perform a bone-divination ritual. The skull arrives, freshly dug from a potter’s field.
- The chapter ends with Li Huowang painting a blood talisman, pressing a copper coin onto the skull, and interpreting the fracture patterns as a hexagram—suggesting a supernatural *pattern* is at work.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Bone Divination (卜骨/Bǔgǔ) & Talisman Magic (符箓/Fúlù):** Li Huowang’s final ritual is a fascinating hybrid. In historical Chinese practice, oracle bone divination is ancient—dating back to the Shang Dynasty, where turtle plastrons or ox scapulae were heated until they cracked, and the pattern was read as a message from ancestors or spirits. Here, Li Huowang is using a skull (a very *Dao-Twisted* touch) and a *talisman* (fúlù)—a Daoist paper charm inscribed with calligraphic commands meant to invoke divine power. The combination tells you everything about this world: the old ways are still powerful, but the materials have gone rotten. The copper coin pressed onto the skull also mirrors “copper coin sword” magic—the coin is both currency and a geomantic tool, bridging the world of the living and the dead.

**The Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司):** This chapter reveals the Office’s *political* logic beautifully. Li Huowang’s job isn’t to catch one criminal—it’s to diagnose a spreading *systemic* anomaly. The fact that fifteen counties are all suffering the same pattern screams “supernatural outbreak” to the Office, even if the local magistrate sees only petty crime. The Office operates like a combination of an imperial censorate and an interdimensional public health agency: they don’t care about justice in the small sense, only about *stabilizing the macro-threat*. A single child-snatching ghost is a constable’s problem; *fifty-seven* missing children across an entire circuit is a *Heavenly Calamity waiting to hatch*.

**Infant Abduction in Chinese Folk Horror:** The “ghosts stealing babies for rebirth” angle has deep folkloric roots. In Chinese folk religion, a spirit who cannot be reborn may attempt to take a living child’s body or soul. The specific detail that all the missing children are “under one year old” (未满周岁) is crucial—infants are considered particularly vulnerable to spiritual possession because their souls are not yet fully anchored to their bodies. This isn’t just crime; it’s a *soul-theft operation* on a mass scale.

### Oracle bone divination

An ancient Chinese practice of interpreting cracks in heated bones (typically turtle plastrons or ox scapulae) as messages from ancestors or spirits; here repurposed with a human skull.

### Under-year-old infants

In Chinese folk belief, children under one year old are spiritually vulnerable; their souls are not yet fully anchored, making them targets for possession or soul-theft.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a *palate cleanser*—a slow, cold inhalation before the plunge. The horror here is not the jump-scare kind; it’s the creeping realization that a pattern is repeating across fifteen counties and no one knows why. Li Huowang’s frustration is our frustration: he’s a monster-slayer forced to be a detective, and the system is designed to hide the truth behind shame and silence. Watch how his *instinct* shifts from interrogation to divination—the moment he gives up on human testimony and reaches for the skull is the moment the chapter admits that this world’s logic has already slipped beyond rational investigation. The final scene, with the blood talisman and the cracking bone, is a promise: the answer is coming, but it will be written in the language of the dead. You’re not supposed to *know* what the hexagram says yet—you’re supposed to feel the weight of that silence. Buckle up. This investigation is going to get very, very cold.
