## Chapter Overview
Li Huowang puts his invisibility trick to the test in the damp, paranoid streets of Pi County. His attempt to gather intel from the locals and the yamen archives uncovers not a single monster but a whole town rotting from the inside—a place where even parent-child love has been twisted into a ritual of agony. And the biggest clue? The magistrate’s records are two years old and polished clean, leaving only a vast, screaming silence where the truth should be.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang tests his incorporeal state, discovering its limitation: rain exposes his invisible outline, nearly causing a panic.
- The inn’s cook is observed in a private, frantic act, confirming the pervasive psychological strangeness of the town.
- Lü Xiucai’s brutal, dismissive attitude toward his father marks his ongoing mental corrosion; Lü Zhuangyuan’s helplessness shows a man out of tools.
- Li Huowang infiltrates the county yamen, reads the gazetteer, and finds a sanitized record—two years of silence where horror should be written.
- The chapter ends with a harrowing found-footage scene: parents binding a child to a pillar and torturing him with an iron clamp, their faces twisted with reluctance and resolve.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**”Sky-dog devours the sun” (天狗食日):** This is the folk Chinese name for a solar eclipse. In traditional belief, a celestial dog gnaws at the sun, causing it to disappear—an omen of chaos and yin energy overwhelming yang. In the Dao-Twisted World, it’s probably a literal event with real consequences, not just an astronomical observation. The fact it’s recorded without comment in the gazetteer tells you how normalized the abnormal has become here.

**”Exchanging children for food” (易子而食):** This phrase appears in Chinese historical records during times of extreme famine. It’s not hyperbole—it was a documented survival horror of the ancient world. The gazetteer entry being a single line, without emotion or judgment, is itself a form of narrative dread.

**County Gazeteer (县志):** Chinese imperial counties kept detailed written records of local events: harvests, disasters, eclipses, rebellions. They were the official memory of a place. A gazetteer that stops two years ago and only mentions a “great harvest” is more damning than any confession—it’s a cover-up written by someone with power and something to hide.

## Reading Guide
Pi County is one of the best examples of the novel’s ability to build dread from atmosphere rather than monsters. There is no Flesh Buddha here, no Zuowandao tricksters—just sickly people, a silent magistrate, and a child being tortured by his own parents. The horror is *communal* and *cultural*: everyone knows something is wrong, but they keep doing it. Li Huowang’s invisibility is useless here because the problem isn’t enemies he can see—it’s a whole society that has accepted pain as normal. Note also the tragic father-son dynamic between Lü Zhuangyuan and Lü Xiucai: one can no longer beat his son into shape, and the other has burned away all filial piety. This is not a chapter about winning; it’s about realizing that sometimes the horror isn’t hunting you—it’s just living its life, right next door.

## Further Reading: The Social Anatomy of Horror
The novel frequently uses small-town dynamics as a horror engine. Pi County is another entry in a series of “sick communities” (like Qingfeng Temple or Zhengde Temple) where the collective has normalized the grotesque. What makes Pi County different is the *institutional* silence—the magistrate’s gazetteer isn’t full of lies or propaganda; it’s just empty. That bureaucratic emptiness is a powerful horror trope: when the system itself refuses to witness what’s happening, the victims become invisible. Li Huowang, despite being able to become physically invisible, has never been more seen than when the child screams and he can only watch through a hole in the paper.
