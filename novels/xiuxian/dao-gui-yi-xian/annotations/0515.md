## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang digs in his heels. Hard. This chapter is a masterclass in psychological trench warfare, as he faces down the most insidious trap the Dice has laid for him: the idea that cultivating the True *requires* cultivating the False. It’s a classic Zuowandao poison pill—a premise that, if accepted even for a second, starts rewriting your reality from the inside out. Li Huowang’s response? A bone-deep, almost violent refusal. He doesn’t debate; he *denies*. The chapter pivots from this heated internal standoff to a much quieter, more tender scene with Li Sui, who tries her best to prove she’s more than just a parasitic passenger. It’s a brief breather, but the tension from the philosophical battle still hangs in the air like smoke.

## Key Plot Points

The hallucinatory Zuowandao (Red Center’s remnant) tries to persuade Li Huowang to practice the False cultivation method stored in his mind, warning that pure True cultivation will lead to Qi Deviation. Li Huowang physically attacks him twice—corporealizing him just to silence him. Zhuge Yuan intervenes as a voice of reason, pressing Li Huowang to explain his staunch refusal. Li Huowang reveals his logic: the “True requires False” doctrine came from the Dice himself, a Zuowandao. For a Heart-Element, accepting such an idea could turn it into reality. Therefore, his only defense is to reject it absolutely, stamping it out as a lie before it can take root. Zhuge Yuan understands and approves of this countermeasure. Exhausted, Li Huowang rests, sleeping through until the next afternoon. He wakes to find Li Sui has cleaned and sharpened his torture implements without being asked, a gesture of love that both warms him and draws a bitter, mocking comment from the Zuowandao hallucination.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The core conflict here twists a foundational xianxia principle: **Cultivating the True (修真)**. In standard xianxia, this means seeking the genuine Dao, cutting through illusion and falsehood. The Zuowandao, true to their name (“Sitting in Forgetfulness”), propose its opposite: **Cultivating the False (修假)**. Their power comes from making lies real. The Dice’s trap is a classic example of a *self-fulfilling prophecy* weaponized by a Heart-Element's reality-bending nature. For a normal cultivator, ignoring half a technique might just be dangerous. For Li Huowang, even *entertaining* the idea is a risk. His head-down refusal isn't stubbornness—it's a survival mechanism. Also notable is the dynamic with Li Sui. Her desire to help by cleaning and sharpening his tools is a deeply human gesture, but her identity as the Black Tai Sui—a grotesque parasite—casts it in a poignant, unsettling light. The Zuowandao hallucination’s snide commentary reminds us that even in moments of peace, corruption is always watching.

## Reading Guide

This is a chapter you feel in your gut, not just your head. Li Huowang’s stance is a powerful character beat—he’s not just fighting monsters and cults anymore; he’s fighting the very *framework* of the beliefs that define his world. Watch how he handles the debate with Zhuge Yuan. It’s not the calm reasoning of a scholar; it’s the frantic barricading of a man besieged by ideas. He’s not trying to be right; he’s trying to stay himself. Also, pay close attention to the quiet aftermath with Li Sui. This is a rare moment of peaceful connection in a life of horror. The fact that it’s immediately punctured by a sarcastic comment from the very embodiment of his internal conflict shows that for Li Huowang, there is no rest, only a ceasefire.

## The Mechanics of a Heart-Element Defense

Let’s get into the weeds on why Li Huowang’s strategy works. Standard cultivators suffer consequences from their own spiritual deviations—bad qi flow, damaged meridians. But a Heart-Element’s power is to *make the intangible tangible*. The Dice’s lie isn’t just a persuasive argument; it’s a seed that, if planted in Li Huowang’s fertile conceptual ground, could grow into a real spiritual disaster. By categorically refusing to accept the premise—essentially shouting “That’s a lie!” with all his being—Li Huowang is performing a kind of exorcism on his own thoughts. It’s crude, it’s violent, and it works. Zhuge Yuan’s approval is crucial here: he recognizes that for this specific constitution, the simplest solution is often the most effective. Sometimes, the best way to win a battle against reality-warping lies is to refuse to play the game.

## Li Sui’s Coming of Age

This chapter is a quiet but important milestone for Li Sui. Her eager, almost childlike desire to help—“Dad, did I do good?”—is a clear sign of her developing independence and sense of self. She’s no longer just a passive tumor; she’s an agent trying to carve out a role and earn approval. The act of cleaning the grisly torture implements is symbolically rich. She’s not just tidying up; she’s taking care of her father’s tools of survival, domesticating the monstrous aspect of their shared life. Her pride in her work is heartbreakingly innocent, a stark contrast to the cynical commentary of the Zuowandao hallucination, who immediately twists her good deed into a weapon of mockery. It’s a reminder that even Li Huowang’s most genuine connections are subject to the world’s relentless corruption, but also that those connections are his strongest bulwark.
