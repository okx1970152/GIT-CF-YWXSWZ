## Chapter Overview

Our boy Li Huowang finally catches a break, and it feels almost as strange to him as a monster attack. After the grueling journey, the group is welcomed into a Qingqiu tribe’s feast as heroes for saving Sun Baolu. Li Huowang is treated to the first truly enjoyable meal he's had in either world, surrounded by music, dance, and genuine smiles. But old habits—and paranoia—die hard. His sharpened senses pick up on the undercurrents: a mother's uneasy silence, a child's drunken pleas to stay, and the nagging certainty that every piece of happiness in this world is just the calm before some new, unspeakable horror. The chapter is a masterclass in building tension through the *absence* of violence, letting the reader’s own dread do the work.

## Key Plot Points

- Sun Baolu’s mother, Wu Yue, formally thanks Li Huowang by performing a full kowtow, revealing she is from Liang and thus an outsider in Qingqiu, just like the reader.
- Li Huowang’s instinctive suspicion of religion is triggered; he questions Wu Yue about which specific Bodhisattva she prays to, and is disturbed by her vague, uneducated answer, mistaking blind faith for ignorance.
- The chapter reveals the complex family dynamics of Sun Baolu's household: his mother is from Liang, his father is a native Qingqiu man, and his betrothed is his own half-sister (from his mother's side), a custom of ‘double-bonds’ that strikes Li Huowang as odd but is accepted as normal.
- A drunken, heartbroken Sun Baolu begs to leave his newly found family and continue traveling with Li Huowang, forcing Li Huowang to dismiss the emotional plea as the ramblings of a drunk.
- Amidst the feast, Li Huowang finally allows himself a moment of disarmed peace, but this is shattered when he notices Wu Yue’s expression has shifted from gratitude to deep, concealed worry.
- In the final, chilling beat, Wu Yue warns Li Huowang directly: do not go to Liang. “That place is no good land.”

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Qingqiu (青丘): A Land of Fox Spirits in Name Only?** In Chinese mythology, especially texts like the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* (Shanhaijing), Qingqiu is the legendary homeland of the nine-tailed fox spirits. It’s often depicted as a lush, magical land. In this chapter, we see it as a real, functioning human society with its own distinct culture, language, and customs, completely stripped of any overt mystical wonder. The name is a constant, chilling reminder of what might lurk beneath the surface of this “normal” feast.

**The Vague ‘Boddhisatva’ (菩萨娘娘):** Wu Yue’s inability to name a specific Bodhisattva speaks volumes about the folk religion in the Dao-Twisted World. In traditional Chinese folk belief, the average person often prays to a generalized “Compassionate Bodhisattva” (救苦救难菩萨娘娘), a syncretic figure often conflated with Guanyin. The lack of doctrinal specificity is the point: Li Huowang sees it as dangerous ignorance, but from a world-building perspective, it shows that real, corrupt power hides in the gaps of casual faith.

**Eternal Sky (长生天 - Changsheng Tian):** The Qingqiu father’s toast invokes the Eternal Sky, a common term found in the Tengrist traditions of various Central Asian steppe peoples. Its use here reinforces the idea that Qingqiu has a distinct, non-Han cultural root, further alienating Li Huowang (and the reader) from a sense of familiar safety.

## Reading Guide

This is, hands down, the most “normal” chapter in a long, long time. And that is exactly what makes it so terrifying. Trust the author: this is not a happy vacation. This chapter is a trap baited with good food and kind smiles. Pay very close attention to Wu Yue. She’s an outsider in Qingqiu, and her final warning lands with the weight of a falling guillotine. The author is giving us a direct piece of information: Liang is bad. But the question is, how bad? And is it bad enough to make staying in a potentially “safe” Qingqiu worth the risk? Li Huowang’s momentary peace is our own peace being lulled—brace for the rug pull.
