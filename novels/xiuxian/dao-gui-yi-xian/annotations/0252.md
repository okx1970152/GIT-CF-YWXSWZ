## Chapter Overview
Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this chapter is a dense lore drop disguised as an interrogation scene. Li Huowang has just carved a terrified Nascent Soul out of Han Fu’s alchemical experiment, and he’s wasting no time using every tool in his grim kit to squeeze information from it. The torture isn’t graphic; it’s psychological, clinical, and utterly chilling. And the payoff? A massive expansion of the Dao-Twisted World’s political landscape. We finally get a proper look at the **Supervisory Heavenly Office**—the imperial bureau that polices the supernatural—and learn that its real mission isn’t hunting small-time cultists. It’s fighting **Heavenly Calamities**. Li Huowang also has a startling revelation about just how low the “evil” sects really rank on the world’s threat ladder. Get ready to rethink everything you thought you knew about the power structures of this broken world.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang interrogates Han Fu’s separated Nascent Soul, using the threat of pain to extract detailed information. The creature, genuinely terrified, pivots to bargaining by offering a new target: the Supervisory Heavenly Office.
- The Nascent Soul explains that the Zuowandao are the Supervisory Heavenly Office’s **sworn enemies**, especially after a Zuowandao agent swapped out the Dongxia emperor two hundred years ago and caused the entire country to collapse.
- Li Huowang learns that the various regional Supervisory Heavenly Offices (Qingqiu, Daliang, Siqi, Sichuan) share intelligence when it comes to the Zuowandao, making it a potentially rich source of information about the mysterious **North Wind**.
- Li Huowang has a critical moment of self-reflection: he realizes that groups like Zhengde Temple and the Ao-Jing Sect, while horrific, are **tolerated** by the authorities because they don’t threaten the social order. They’re less dangerous than common bandits.
- The biggest revelation of the chapter: the Supervisory Heavenly Office’s **primary mission** isn't hunting monsters or cults—it’s dealing with **Heavenly Calamities**. Li Huowang slaps his forehead, realizing the name itself should have made that obvious.
- The chapter ends with the Nascent Soul trying to bargain for its life by praising the value of the **Seven-Star Bronze Coin Sword**, describing its auspicious ritual construction.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Supervisory Heavenly Office (监天司)** – This is a brilliant piece of worldbuilding. The name literally translates to “Office of Supervising Heaven,” and as Li Huowang belatedly realizes, the name is a dead giveaway of its purpose. In Chinese bureaucratic tradition, “司” (Si) is a classical term for a government ministry or bureau (e.g., the Ministry of Rites or the Court of Judicial Review). The novel fuses this with the concept of “天” (Heaven)—not in a purely spiritual sense, but as a systemic, cosmic force that can break down. This makes the Office feel less like a fantasy monster-hunting guild and more like a dusty imperial department with official seals, budgets, and a centuries-old grudge.

**The Zuowandao’s Emperor Swap** – The Nascent Soul mentions a historical event where a Zuowandao replaced the emperor of Dongxia, leading to the kingdom’s destruction. This echoes real Chinese anxieties about court impostors and false emperors, a theme found in everything from classic novels like *Water Margin* to folklore about shape-shifting fox spirits infiltrating the palace. The horror isn’t just in the assassination—it’s in the slow, bureaucratic decay of a kingdom led by a stranger wearing the sovereign’s face.

**The Seven-Star Sword and Ritual Timing** – The Nascent Soul’s description of the sword’s construction is packed with Daoist ritual numerology: the Year of the Monkey, the noon hour of the noon day of the noon month, the use of one hundred and eighty-four coins, and references to the Eight Nodes (八节) and Three Prime Days (三元日). In traditional Daoist alchemy and talisman-craft, specific celestial and calendrical alignments were believed to concentrate spiritual power. A weapon forged at such an exact moment would be considered a potent artifact, capable of dispersing the malevolent earth energies of “Two Blacks and Five Yellows” (二黑五黄)—terms from feng shui and the Flying Star school, referring to specific inauspicious star influences.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a **worldbuilding feast**, but it’s served cold and clinical. Li Huowang isn’t trembling with fear or rage; he’s processing information with the flat, pragmatic focus of a soldier taking stock of enemy positions. Notice how his anger at Zhengde Temple and the Ao-Jing Sect is deflated not by lack of evidence, but by a simple cost-benefit analysis: they’re not the problem right now. This is a man learning to triage his hatred. The real tension here is intellectual: the revelation that Heavenly Calamities are the ultimate enemy reframes the entire scope of the story. Li Huowang isn’t just fighting to survive a cult or a curse. He’s caught in a broken cosmic system. Also, keep an eye on the “North Wind” thread. The Nascent Soul’s testimony is our first proper lead on a figure who might know how to escape Bewilderment—but given the source, we all know that lead could be a rotten rope. Get your tinfoil hats ready, folks.
