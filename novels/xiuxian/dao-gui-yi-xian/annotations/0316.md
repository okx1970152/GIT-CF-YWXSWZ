## Chapter Overview
Welcome back, Dao-Twisted disciples! Chapter 316 gives us one of those rare, quiet afternoons in the Dao-Twisted World—which, if you've been reading this far, you know means something's about to get *weird*. After yesterday's intense interrogation session with Tuoba Danqing, Li Huowang finally gets a moment to breathe. And where does he choose to spend it? A bustling City God Temple, naturally. But this isn't just a sightseeing trip. Our boy is making a pit stop to collect a debt from Blind Chen, the fortune-teller we met earlier who had his sacred Eight Trigrams Mirror stolen by a Zuowandao impersonator. The chapter serves up a beautifully melancholic slice of supernatural Chinese folk life: devout prostitutes wearing grooves into stone with their prostrations, a cynical blind seer who reads fates he won't explain, and a mysterious, deeply unsettling talisman manual that comes with a very steep activation cost. Li Huowang, in classic fashion, accepts the deal with a deadpan "That's acceptable," before Blind Chen drops a cryptic fortune-poem that lingers in the air like incense smoke.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang returns to the tavern to find Hongzhong mocking the evasive answers they gave the Office agent. The mood is light until a waiter triggers Li Huowang's temper by offering to pack his leftovers—a clear sign he's still raw from the previous day.
- The next day, Li Huowang visits the towering, incense-shrouded City God Temple of Yinling City. The narrative lingers on the temple's grandeur and the irony that such a majestic building houses a civic deity, not a major religious figure.
- A major observation: the most devoted worshippers are prostitutes from the Pagoda of Delicate Beauties, recognizable by their dark-blue ceramic facial patterns. Their desperate, whispered prayers for rescue or reincarnation as men paint a stark picture of their social hopelessness.
- Li Huowang approaches Blind Chen's stall. The transaction is brisk: he returns the Eight Trigrams Mirror, reports the Zuowandao imposter's death, and demands the promised recompense.
- Blind Chen, delighted by the news of the trickster's death, hands over a thread-bound manual of talisman arts. The book is detailed and practical, but Li Huowang notices every talisman has a distorted human face hidden in the script—a red flag.
- Blind Chen reveals a critical taboo: the talismans can only be used three times a day, and the user must *never look at them* while drawing or using them. If he does, the talisman "comes to life" and will target him, forcing him to gouge out his eyes like Blind Chen.
- Li Huowang accepts the restriction with chilling pragmatism. As he tries to leave, Blind Chen lunges forward to "read" his shadow, then delivers a cryptic, poetic fortune about a fading moon, cold stars, and an empty pouch.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**City God Temple (城隍庙):** In traditional Chinese folk religion, the City God (Chenghuang) is a tutelary deity responsible for the welfare of a city and its inhabitants—supervising the mortal realm, judging the dead, and maintaining order between the human and spirit worlds. Each city had its own temple, and offerings were made to appease the god and ensure civic protection. The novel uses this setting brilliantly: the "black-faced" statues, the crescent-moon tablets, and the flanking Ox-Head (牛头) and Horse-Face (马面) attendants are all standard iconography from Chinese underworld mythology. The irony that prostitutes—among the most marginalized people in feudal society—are the temple's most fervent worshippers adds a layer of tragic social commentary.

**Ox-Head and Horse-Face (牛头马面):** These are the two iconic demon guards of Diyu (the Chinese underworld), tasked with escorting souls of the dead to judgment. Their presence in a City God Temple reinforces the temple's role as a bridge between the living and the dead. In the Dao-Twisted World, their "oppressive" presence suggests they are not just statues but potentially active watchers.

**Talisman Taboos (符箓禁忌):** The restriction Blind Chen describes—never looking at the talisman while drawing or using it—is a classic horror trope drawn from real folkloric taboos. In Chinese folk magic, talismans are contracts with spiritual forces. Looking at the inscription directly is seen as a form of "awakening" the spirit within. The hidden face in every talisman is a signature of this contamination. The cost (gouging out one's eyes) echoes the ancient sacrifice required for forbidden knowledge in many supernatural traditions.

**The Fortune Poem (命诗):** Blind Chen's closing poem follows classical Chinese *qiyan jueju* (seven-character quatrain) structure. Its imagery—a round moon becoming waning, cold stars, an empty pouch—suggests loneliness, isolation, and a life lived in comfortable poverty but emotional barrenness. In the context of Li Huowang's journey, the poem feels like a prophecy of his future: a life spent wandering, never truly at home.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in "atmospheric quiet before the storm." On the surface, it's a simple errand: return a mirror, pick up a reward. But the *texture* of the chapter is what makes it sing. Lingering on the prostitutes' prayers, the worn stone floor, the incense smoke—it's the kind of grounded, lived-in worldbuilding that makes the Dao-Twisted World feel real and haunted at the same time. Li Huowang's deadpan acceptance of the eye-gouging taboo is a perfect character beat. He's not brave, he's *used to paying prices*. And the final fortune poem? That's not just atmosphere. That's a thread that will weave itself into future chapters. Keep a mental bookmark on it.
