## Chapter Overview

Lu Xiucai has hit the jackpot—or so he thinks. Freed from the brutal survival pressure of the road, he’s now living the low-rent dream in Yinling City: morning feasts, afternoon brothels, evening gambling, and a retinue of sycophants hanging on his every embellished word. But beneath the bravado and the swagger, this chapter is a character study in how quickly a desperate survivor can turn into a petty tyrant. The moment you give a street-smart conman a little cash and a shred of status, you get *this*. It’s ugly, it’s uncomfortable, and it’s laying the groundwork for a fall that feels both inevitable and richly deserved.

## Key Plot Points

- Lu Xiucai holds court at Drunken Immortal Tavern, telling wildly exaggerated tales of his adventures with Li Huowang. The stories are stolen from Gouwa; Lu just paints himself as the hero.
- He leads his followers to Rouge Alley, spending freely on women and wine.
- Back home, his purchased wife Tao'er tends to him like a servant—washing, feeding, doting—while he treats her with casual contempt.
- Lu discovers Tao'er's menstrual blood and flies into a rage before she explains basic female biology to him. He grudgingly gives her silver for meat.
- At the gambling house, local gang boss Kui Sanjin flatters Lu and invites him to a private dinner.
- Kui reveals his "problem": a sworn brother has gone mad after being cursed, and neither monks nor Daoists could help. He offers Lu a staggering eighty percent cut to fix it.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Lu Xiucai's Social Ascent**  
This chapter is a masterclass in showing, not telling, how Chinese web novels dramatize class mobility. Lu was a beggar-level performer not long ago. Now he has a *wife* (bought cheap), a rented house (through a gangster's pull), and a table full of lard-stir-fried dishes (a sign of sudden wealth). Every detail—the seven plates and eight bowls, the donkey-meat dumplings from the brothel, the "slowly" waking at dusk—screams *nouveau riche* with no taste to match. His contempt for the same food he once craved is a perfect little knife-twist.

**Rouge Alley (胭脂胡同)**  
A historical euphemism for the red-light district. The "buy the girl, get the donkey-meat dumplings free" bit is both vulgar and historically grounded—pleasure houses often bundled food or services to attract customers. The raw, transactional tone of this whole section is deliberately grating, meant to make the reader cringe at Lu's coarseness.

**The Curfew Trap**  
Yinling City has a curfew (宵禁). Lu needs to reach the gambling house before it starts or he'll be trapped outside. This is a small but crucial detail: even when he's flying high, the world still has rules that can trip him up. It foreshadows the larger trap that Kui Sanjin is about to spring.

### Lunar taboo (月事)

A euphemism for menstruation; in traditional Chinese society, open discussion of menstruation was considered private or even shameful, which makes Lu Xiucai's ignorance and Tao'er's quiet explanation both awkward and revealing.

## Reading Guide

This is a *grind-your-teeth* chapter, fellow reader. You are supposed to be uncomfortable. Lu Xiucai is not an anti-hero you root for; he's a warning. Watch how easily the victim becomes the bully when given a few days of comfort. His rage at Tao'er's period, his obliviousness to the disdain of other patrons, the way he *listens to his own lies* and believes them—it's all deliberate. Kui Sanjin's flattery is so transparent you can see through it from the first pour of wine, but Lu can't. He's drunk on status, not just alcohol.

The real tension isn't "will Lu succeed?" It's "how badly will he fail?" And the eighty-percent offer? In this world, when someone offers you that much, the *cost* is always paid in something worse than silver.
