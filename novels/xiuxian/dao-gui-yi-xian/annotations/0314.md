## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang has mastered the art of feeding Black Tai Sui to himself—quite literally. But this chapter isn't about explosive battle sequences or face-slapping revelations. Instead, it's a quiet, atmospheric breather that lets the horror sink in through routine: buying a room, eating *your own symbiotic parasite* for breakfast, and trying to teach it the *Three Character Classic* from your belly. The mundane setting (an inn, the market, a reunion with Tuoba Danqing) serves as a stage for two things that matter most right now: Li Huowang's slow, desperate attempt to domesticate the monster inside him, and a world-building conversation about that bagua mirror's fate. Laugh a little, because the absurdity of reciting "Men at their birth are naturally good" while a tentacle peeps out of your navel is exactly the kind of dark-comedy tone this novel nails.

## Key Plot Points

- **Black Tai Sui feeding and training**: We open with a surprisingly casual scene: Li Huowang feeds the trussed-up Black Tai Sui dried meat, then cuts off a piece of it and eats it himself. The creature's body doesn't shrink; it's as if Li Huowang is taking a sample, not depleting a resource. He then attempts to "teach" the Black Tai Sui literacy by reading the *Three Character Classic* out loud, wondering if it can only learn from inside his stomach. A black tentacle briefly emerges from his navel—then retreats. This is our first confirmation that the Black Tai Sui is now a living part of his internal ecosystem, not just a meal.
- **Tuoba Danqing reunion**: Li Huowang visits the same restaurant where he previously met Tuoba Danqing. The host is expecting him, and the feast is laid out immediately. Tuoba Danqing greets him warmly ("ear-brother!") and notices the pink and black patches on Li Huowang's copper-coin mask, but says nothing.
- **The bagua mirror dilemma**: After several cups of wine, Tuoba Danqing reveals that the bagua mirror was swindled by the Zuowandao from a blind Daoist surnamed Chen. The Daoist refuses to give the mirror's activation technique but offers a full set of talisman manuals in exchange. Li Huowang sarcastically realizes that Hongzhong's "Blind Chen" identity was stolen too.
- **Tuoba Danqing's advice**: The veteran advises Li Huowang to take the deal—the mirror is useless for the Ao-Jing Sect's self-harming style, but the talisman manuals could fill their gaps. He warns that the Daoist might come looking if the mirror isn't returned, and adds a pragmatic note about keeping a potential ally.
- **The mission hook**: Drunk and casual, Tuoba Danqing pivots to business: there's a job to the south that needs Li Huowang's attention. The chapter ends on a call to action.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **《三字经》 (Three Character Classic)**: This is not some random children's rhyme; it's one of the most foundational Confucian primers in Chinese history, written in the Song dynasty by Wang Yinglin. Every line is exactly three characters long, making it a rhythmic mnemonic for teaching classical virtues. The fact that Li Huowang uses this to "teach" the Black Tai Sui is a wonderful bit of absurdist horror—he's trying to instill the core Confucian virtue of innate goodness into an entity that is literally a parasitic lump of flesh. "Men at their birth are naturally good" becomes unintentionally hilarious (and chilling) dialogue.
- **"山、医、卜、命、相" (Mountains, Medicine, Divination, Fate, Physiognomy)**: These are the five traditional arts of the *Daoist magical-technical canon*, sometimes called *The Five Arts* or *The Five Paths of the Daoist Siddha*. They cover: alchemy/geomancy (Mountain), herbal medicine (Medicine), divination (Divination), fate-calculation (Fate), and face-reading/physiognomy (Phase). A manual that teaches all five is considered a priceless heirloom, but in this novel, the claim is obviously inflated.
- **"多一个朋友就多条路" (One more friend, one more road)**: This is a classic Chinese martial-world (jianghu) saying. It reflects a survival philosophy where personal connections (*guanxi*) are the real currency of power—not strength alone. Tuoba Danqing is reminding Li Huowang that the martial world runs on favors, not just blades.

### Five Arts (山、医、卜、命、相)

The five traditional Daoist technical disciplines: Mountains (alchemy/geomancy), Medicine (herbal healing), Divination (prognostication), Fate (mansion-calculus), and Physiognomy (face-reading).

## Reading Guide

Get ready, fellow wanderers of the Dao-Twisted World, because this chapter is a masterclass in *atmospheric creep*. The horror isn't in a sudden monster attack—it's in the casual way Li Huowang draws a knife, cuts a piece of Black Tai Sui, and chews it like a piece of jerky. The scene is mundane, but the details are wrong: the Black Tai Sui doesn't shrink; it just keeps oozing. The tentacle emerging from his navel is played for amusement, not shock. This is the novel telling you, in its own sick way, that Li Huowang has officially reached the point where parasitism feels like routine maintenance.

Pay attention to Tuoba Danqing's performance here. He's a *brother in the Office*, but his every move is a calculated social ritual: the laughter before entering, the pouring of wine, the casual gossip before business. The bagua mirror subplot isn't just a gear upgrade—it's a test of Li Huowang's strategic thinking. Should he trade a powerful artifact for something unknown? Tuoba Danqing's advice is gold: "As long as you're willing to be ruthless with yourselves, you don't need a crappy mirror." That's the Ao-Jing Sect philosophy in a nutshell: *pain is power, and tools are crutches.*

And finally, that hook at the end—"there's a job to the south"—is the novel's way of saying the training wheels are off. Li Huowang is now an active agent in the Office, and missions will come whether he's ready or not. The Black Tai Sui, the talisman manuals, the mirror, the connections… they're all preparation for what's coming next.
