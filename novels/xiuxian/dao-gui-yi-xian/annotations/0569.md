## Chapter Overview
Alright, buckle up, friends. This chapter isn’t here to make you feel good. It’s here to punch you in the gut and leave you staring at the ceiling at 3 a.m. We pick up with Li Huowang in the modern hospital, scarfing down fast food like a normal person—and for a moment, you almost believe it. The illusion of peace is cruel and brief. When Yi Donglai casually drops the name Zhuge Yuan, the entire chapter detonates into a raw, screaming breakdown. This isn’t a thoughtful debate about reality versus delusion. This is Li Huowang finally breaking, right there in the white-tiled room, and the sound is horrible.

The chapter is a masterclass in emotional whiplash: from a fry dipped in ketchup to a shattered game console, from a mother’s gentle touch to a headbutt that draws blood. Yi Donglai plays the role of the rational tormentor—calm, persistent, and utterly devastating—forcing Li Huowang to confront the one thing he cannot escape: the memory of a friend who is *gone*. The final scene, with Li Huowang climbing out of a well back into the Dao-Twisted World, is not a triumphant return. It’s the sound of a man picking up his cross again, knowing he’ll never put it down.

## Key Plot Points
- The chapter opens with a painfully mundane scene: Li Huowang eating a fast-food breakfast in the hospital. His mother, Sun Xiaoqin, is hopeful that his normal appetite signals recovery.
- Yi Donglai asks bluntly if Zhuge Yuan is dead, triggering an immediate, violent meltdown. Li Huowang smashes his game console and physically attacks the doctor.
- Li Huowang’s scream is a torrent of accumulated grief: “Why does it have to be me?” He confesses he has never felt a moment of peace in the Dao-Twisted World and wants to abandon it entirely.
- Yi Donglai refuses to validate the escape fantasy. He coldly insists Li Huowang must return to “fix the dragon vein of Great Liang” and save the people of Great Qi.
- Yi Donglai weaponizes Li Huowang’s own relationships—Bai Lingmiao’s uncertain fate, Li Sui’s need for a father—to force him back. Li Huowang responds by trying to split his own skull open against the window bars.
- After being sedated, Li Huowang wakes at the bottom of a well in the Dao-Twisted World. The chapter ends with him climbing out, realizing Zhuge Yuan is truly dead, and his tears are useless.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
We need to talk about Yi Donglai. On the surface, he is a psychiatrist. But in the structure of *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, he functions as a **metaphysical antagonist** who uses the language of modern medicine to enforce the protagonist’s suffering. His “tough love” is not therapeutic—it is a form of spiritual coercion. He insists that facing the horror is the only cure, but the novel has been arguing for 568 chapters that there is no cure, only survival. The “dragon vein” reference is key: in Chinese political cosmology, the dragon vein (龙脉) is the geomantic energy line that legitimizes a dynasty. Cutting it means the end of a kingdom. Li Huowang has been told he is the only one who can fix it. This isn’t healing—it’s a cosmic draft notice.

Also notable: the well. Traditional Chinese wells (水井) are often symbols of connection between worlds or gateways to the underworld. Li Huowang waking at the bottom of one is a literal “return to the depths” before he must climb back into the light of pain.

## Reading Guide
This is one of the purest emotional gut-punches in the novel so far. Read it in one sitting—the power is in the pacing. Notice how the author builds a small, warm bubble at the start (mother, fries, ketchup) just to shatter it with a single sentence. The destruction of the game console is not random; it is the destruction of the last coping mechanism. After that, Li Huowang fights with his bare hands and his forehead.

Pay attention to the recursion of the Yi Donglai figure. He is not just a doctor; he is the voice that forces Li Huowang to care. The words “Bai Lingmiao” and “Li Sui” are not suggestions—they are weapons. Yi Donglai knows that Li Huowang cannot abandon them, and he uses that knowledge to drive him back into the crucible.

When you reach the well scene, let the silence sit. There is no dramatic music here. Just a man shaking tears from his eyes, grabbing a rope, and climbing back into a world he hates. The final line—“Dead was dead”—is not resignation. It is the only truth he has left. If you’ve ever wondered why this novel is called a horror story rather than a cultivation story, this chapter is your answer.
