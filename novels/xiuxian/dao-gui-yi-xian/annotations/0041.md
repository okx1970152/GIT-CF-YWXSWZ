## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang's ragged crew finally reaches the gates of Xijing City, and our traumatized Daoist gets his first real glimpse of organized religion in the Dao-Twisted World. But before the city walls come into view, this chapter serves up a quiet but devastating roadside conversation with the old beggar-monk. What starts as casual curiosity about his faith turns into a philosophical gut-punch: can someone be a true believer if they don't know *what* they believe? Li Huowang, the fake Daoist with a stolen scripture and a head full of lies, suddenly finds himself out-argued by a man who defines "monk" through a checklist of surface behaviors. It's a deceptively tender scene that lingers long after the sandalwood smoke of Zhengde Temple hits your nostrils.

## Key Plot Points

- The group camps overnight after narrowly surviving the bandit attack; Li Huowang takes first watch and reflects on the brutal logic of this world where humans are as dangerous as monsters.
- The old beggar-monk catches up after burying the dead all night, damp and shivering; Li Huowang's hostility softens after remembering the man's selfless rescue.
- A probing conversation unfolds: the old man insists he is a monk because he shaved his head, avoids women, abstains from meat, and chants Amitabha—but he cannot name *which* Buddha he worships.
- Li Huowang is struck speechless when asked to define what a real monk *is*; he sees a disturbing likeness to Danyangzi—ignorance mixed with fierce, stubborn commitment.
- The old man reveals his origin story: a monk once saved his life, so he swore to become one himself, pursuing holiness through mimicry.
- After ten uneventful days of travel, Xijing City rises before them—an imposing, walled metropolis that dwarfs Jianye.
- Lü Zhuangyuan warns about predatory inns; Li Huowang cuts him off to demand directions to Zhengde Temple, the main reason for his journey.
- The temple's overwhelming popularity is confirmed by thick crowds and the heavy scent of incense; worshippers enthusiastically praise its efficacy and the monks' charity.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter quietly deconstructs a question that runs through the entire Dao-Twisted World: **what makes a religious identity real?** The old beggar's definition of being a monk (剃光头, 不吃肉, 不讨婆娘, 念阿弥陀佛) is pure surface-level mimicry drawn from local gossip—Scholar Sun says this, Tailor Wang says that, Shopkeeper Liu says the other. He has no doctrinal knowledge, no scripture, no lineage. Yet his sincerity and gratitude toward the monk who saved him are unmistakably genuine. In a world of corrupt abbots and flesh buddhas, this man may be the most honest "monk" we've met.

Li Huowang's inability to answer the old man's question is significant. As a "fake Daoist" himself—posing as Danyangzi's disciple while secretly planning his escape—he realizes that intellectual knowledge (knowing *which* Buddha) and lived faith (deciding to *be* a monk through action) are not the same thing. This ambiguity is central to the novel's theme of whether belief shapes reality or reality shapes belief.

Zhengde Temple (正德寺) is introduced with all the trappings of a legitimate, thriving Buddhist institution: efficacious bodhisattva, charity porridge, overflowing incense offerings. But readers who remember the grotesque Vase-Girl ritual and the Flesh Buddha from earlier chapters know better. The temple's public piety is a mask. The more popular and "virtuous" it appears, the more sinister its hidden practices likely are. This is the Dao-Twisted World's signature move: take something sacred and familiar, and rot it from the inside out.

### Charity porridge (布施粥)

A traditional act of Buddhist merit-making where temples provide free food to the poor. In this world, even such kindness may be a cover for darker exchange.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a palate cleanser before the storm. After the relentless action and horror of previous chapters, *The Monk* slows everything down to walking pace. It gives you room to breathe, to watch Li Huowang be genuinely stumped by an old beggar's innocent questions, and to feel the relief of finally seeing a city gate that doesn't promise immediate death. But don't let the calm fool you. Every time this novel introduces a bustling religious center with happy worshippers, you should feel your stomach tighten. The sandalwood is covering something.

Pay special attention to the mirroring between the old monk and Danyangzi. Both are defined by a kind of blind, stubborn faith—one in the Buddha, one in his own immortality scheme. Both are illiterate. Both operate on fragments of knowledge repurposed into a system. The difference is that the old monk's faith leads him to selfless rescue, while Danyangzi's led him to alchemical murder. That contrast is doing a lot of thematic heavy lifting.

Also, note that Li Huowang *sees* the resemblance but doesn't fully process it. That blindness is his character flaw for this arc: he recognizes patterns of ignorance without yet understanding that his own "fake Daoist" act also fits the same mold. The old monk's question—"Then tell me, what should a monk be like?"—is one Li Huowang cannot answer for himself either.
