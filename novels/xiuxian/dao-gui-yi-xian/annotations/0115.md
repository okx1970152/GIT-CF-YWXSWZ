## Chapter Overview

Silver. Cartloads of it. Lü Zhuangyuan’s eyes nearly pop out of his skull when he realizes what the heavily-laden wagons are carrying, but his greedy daydream is cut short by a looming threat: the wagon’s owners, a pack of grotesquely obese, filthy nuns from Anci Nunnery, who shamble over to mooch salted duck eggs with the table manners of a garbage grinder. Just as our ragtag troupe inches closer to the boiling point, a new force of nature appears—elite cavalry from the Siqi army, led by the fierce and pragmatic You Zixiong. In a tense standoff between iron discipline and feral gluttony, the army commandeers the silver under a “promissory note” that reeks of wartime expediency, leaving the nuns to gloat over a single officer’s sword and the Lü family trembling in the dust. The chapter is a masterclass in *shifting power dynamics*: mortal greed, institutional decay, and the raw, sudden intrusion of “big picture” politics into a small-time survival story. Hold on tight, fellow Daoists—this world just got a lot bigger, and a lot more dangerous.

## Key Plot Points

- Lü Zhuangyuan and his troupe are traveling alongside Anci Nunnery’s nuns, who are hauling several wagons of silver. The matron of the opera troupe realizes the weight implies a fortune and grows visibly anxious.
- A skinny-but-still-enormous nun ambles over, slobbering over Zhao Wu’s salted duck egg. She pays with rancid copper coins, eats the eggs shell and all, and shows signs of a monstrous appetite and total disregard for social norms.
- Elite cavalry—the Dark Peak Riders—arrive with thunderous speed, led by You Zixiong, an officer carrying four long blades and wearing black iron armor. He immediately commandeers the silver for “border defense,” promising a promissory note from the Ministry of Revenue.
- The nuns refuse to yield the silver, escalating the tension to the point of bloodshed. You Zixiong suppresses his killing intent and throws his own personal sword at them as a token, telling them to bring it to collect payment—a clear gesture of compromise under duress.
- The cavalry divides the silver among riders and departs in an orderly fashion, leaving the troupe shaken. Lü Xiucai admits to wetting himself. Lü Zhuangyuan, his legs weak, resolves to find “that little Daoist” (Li Huowang) and flee Siqi before the whole region erupts in war.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Anci Nunnery (安慈庵) – The Other Side of the Buddhist Coin**  
If you’ve been reading through the Dao-Twisted World, you’ve already met the *polished* evil of Zhengde Temple—the liturgical flourishes, the three-eyed abbots, the flesh-buddhas hiding beneath gold leaf. The nuns of Anci Nunnery represent the opposite end of the corruption spectrum: **rural, slovenly, and frighteningly direct**. They are filthy, gluttonous, and completely indifferent to Buddhist precepts on diet and deportment. In Chinese rural folk religion, this kind of depiction is a brutal satire on “fake piety”—clergy who take in offerings and occupy land but offer nothing spiritual in return. Their acceptance of You Zixiong’s sword (a martial object) instead of silver says everything: they understand the **language of power, not the language of faith**. They are not believers; they are territorial predators draped in gray robes.

**Cavalry Culture & “Swift as the Wind, Steady as the Pines” (行如风, 站如松)**  
This eight-character phrase is a classic Chinese military compliment, often used to praise elite drill and iron discipline. The novel uses it to contrast the **clean, organized brutality of the state** with the *squalid, organic terror* of the folk-horror nuns. You Zixiong’s sudden shift from killing intent to pragmatic compromise reflects a key xianxia reality: in a collapsing kingdom, even a hardened officer must bow to *any* power that costs too much to crush. His surrender of his own sword is a calculated bargaining chip—the sword is a symbol of his martial honor, but in the Dao-Twisted World, honor is just another resource to trade.

**Siqi (四齐) – A Kingdom on the Brink**  
The fact that Siqi is now in chaos and the border is “in crisis” confirms what readers have suspected: the Dao-Twisted World’s political map is fracturing. The Nine Classes system (九流) that organizes everyone from bandits to the Supervisory Heavenly Office is starting to crack under the weight of war, famine, and supernatural corrosion.

## Reading Guide

*So… this is what a world war feels like when you’re too poor to join, too weak to run, and too unlucky to be ignored.*

If you’ve been feeling like Li Huowang’s personal nightmare is the *only* thing happening in the Dao-Twisted World, this chapter is a cold bucket of reality to the face. The cavalry doesn’t care about the main cast. They don’t care about e do *monsters* or *curses*. They have a deadline and a war to fight. That’s unsettling in a very grounded way—because in a world where gods are lies and truth is a negotiable asset, the most terrifying force might just be a human army with a schedule.

Notice how Lü Zhuangyuan, our grizzled and pragmatic old fox, instantly recognizes a threat he can neither fight nor flee from. He tries to pawn responsibility off on Zhao Wu (“You have a powerful senior brother, right?”) because he still thinks of Li Huowang’s group as a *trump card*. He doesn’t know half of what Li Huowang has actually been through. That’s classic worldbuilding: the *perception* of power is often more useful than power itself.

Also, let’s take a moment to appreciate the “nuns eating eggs with the shell on” detail. That’s a fantastic piece of body horror-lite. It’s not tentacles or bleeding eyes—it’s just *wrong enough* to make your skin crawl. In the Dao-Twisted World, the sacred is always twisted, but here it’s twisted into something boringly, disgustingly **animal**. That’s a different kind of fear: not a monster, but a person who has stopped caring about being human.
