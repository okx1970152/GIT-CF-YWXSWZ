## Chapter Overview

Alright, fellow daoists, strap in, because Chapter 417 is a wild, breathless, *and* existentially horrifying ride. After the chaos of the previous chapter, Li Huowang fully commits to his Zuowandao “Red Center” disguise, pulling off the ultimate false flag operation right in the heart of the capital. This isn't just a frantic escape—it’s a masterclass in psychological warfare and opportunistic nihilism. Li Huowang burns every bridge he can find between the Supervisory Heavenly Office and the Zuowandao, laughing and bleeding in equal measure. But the real gut punch? When the dust settles and he reaches for his own face, he finds **nothing where his head should be**. This chapter turns a tactical retreat into a body-horror mystery that leaves the reader just as lost as the protagonist.

## Key Plot Points

- **The False Flag:** Li Huowang, still wearing the “Red Center” face, publicly kills the Supervisory Overseer (监丞) with the *Six Luminary Almanac* in hand. He taunts the Office, screaming that the treasure belongs to the Zuowandao. His goal is to irrevocably frame them for the murder, forcing the two factions into an all-out war.
- **A Desperate Escape:** Blind and dependent on Li Sui for guidance, Li Huowang phases through rooftops and uses his full arsenal of tricks (invisibility, talismans, bodily contortion) to flee the pursuing Heavenly Office officers.
- **The Overseer Rises:** Just as he thinks he’s clear, the dead Overseer’s body stands back up. A new, unknown presence now inhabits it—something that locks onto Li Huowang and only stops when a black object (possibly a bronze coin sword or a falling roof tile) hits the ground. This confirms the Overseer's death was only the beginning of a larger supernatural affair.
- **Zuowandao Rear Guard:** The real Zuowandao on the scene, completely fooled by Li Huowang’s disguise, shout their support for “Boss Red Center,” effectively becoming an unwitting rearguard that holds off the Office so Li Huowang can escape.
- **The Vanished Head:** After his successful exit from the city and recovery in the woods, Li Huowang scares a woodcutter half to death. When he tries to peel off the “Red Center” mask to revert to his “Ear Nine” identity, he discovers—with chilling, surreal finality—that his head is gone. Only Li Sui’s tentacles and her eyeballs remain where his skull should be.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **The Six Luminary Almanac (六曜通书):** This is the *real* prize of the battle. In Chinese folk tradition, an almanac (通书 or 老皇历) is more than a calendar; it dictates auspicious and inauspicious days for all major life events. It’s a tool of cosmic order. Here, it’s a chaotic artifact of immense power, one that the Zuowandao explicitly covets.
- **“The Boy Who Cried Wolf” as a Taoist Strategy:** Li Huowang explicitly references this fable. He knows the Zuowandao have cried “wolf” so many times (spreading misinformation, illusion, and lies) that even when they tell the truth, no one believes them. He weaponizes their own brand of ontological chaos against them. This is a clever inversion of the usual xianxia intelligence game.
- **Phantom Body / Headless Survival:** The chapter ends with a shocking reveal: Li Huowang’s head is physically gone, yet he still thinks, moves, and speaks. This is a classic trope in high-level xianxia horror—a body that no longer requires conventional anatomy. It suggests his cultivation (or his parasitic passenger, Li Sui) has fundamentally altered his physiology. In Daoist internal alchemy, this could relate to the “reversal of the Five Phases” or the “replacement of the viscera,” where the body becomes a vessel no longer bound by mortal flesh.

### False Flag Operation

A covert operation designed to deceive; the perpetrator falsely attributes an action to another group to incriminate and manipulate them.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a victory lap for Li Huowang’s character development—but a deeply unsettling one. He’s not the panicked, confused victim from the early chapters. Here, he’s cold, calculating, and savagely efficient. The sheer audacity of murdering an Overseer in broad daylight and blaming it on the Zuowandao is something the old Li Huowang would never have dreamed of. And yet, the price is staring him in the face: he has lost his own head.

The tonal whiplash here is deliberate. We go from the gritty, tactical thrill of an escape chase, to the grimy realism of scaring a poor woodcutter, and then immediately into a cosmic body-horror mystery. The author wants you to feel triumphant for Li Huowang’s success, only to yank the rug out from under you with a question that has no easy answer.

For readers, the key takeaway is the contrast between **blatant, external chaos** (the street fight, the false flag) and **subtle, internal horror** (the missing head). The real cost of Li Huowang’s path isn’t just pain—it’s the slow, creeping erosion of his own physical identity. This chapter is a masterful example of *Dao Gui Yi Xian* turning a “standard escape sequence” into a philosophical puzzle about what it even means to be human anymore.
