## Chapter Overview

Welcome back, fellow travelers in the Dao-Twisted World! Hot off the heels of cave-diving horror, Li Huowang emerges from the underground nightmare carrying more than just rescued children—he's hauling questions, anger, and a surprising heap of literal *lifespan*. Chapter 327, titled "Good Deeds," is a curious, contemplative breather that wraps up the Cangshui County incident but refuses to let you feel good about it. Li Huowang's encounter with the Renxiao leaves him with a disturbing haul: seventy-nine lifespan pellets, a mini alchemy cauldron, and yet *another* Heavenly Scripture that's just a Buddhist sutra in disguise. The Renxiao is dead, but its actions—and the complicity of the old people—leave a rancid taste that no amount of clean laundry or dried meat can wash away. The chapter is a quiet, almost domestic cleanup after the body horror, where the biggest battle is whether to accept a feast invitation or just GTFO back to Yinling. Between the magistrate's icy justice, the town's tearful gratitude, and the monk's relentless optimism, Li Huowang sits at an uneasy crossroads where doing good feels more like completing a chore than earning redemption.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang finds seventy-nine lifespan pellets in the bloody water left by the Renxiao, a deeply ambiguous gift—was it a confession, an offering, or just leftovers?
- He dismembers the Renxiao corpse as a precaution (no more zombie surprises), then forces the terrified elderly accomplices to keep moving.
- Li Huowang recovers a small cauldron, realizing he can now practice alchemy to replenish his supplies—an important upgrade from pure survival mode.
- He finds a stone Heavenly Scripture identical to Danyangzi's—a "good deeds" Buddhist sutra—and summarily tosses it into the underground river, much to Hongzhong's theatrical dismay.
- The return to Cangshui County triggers a chaotic, emotional reunion: found families kowtow, the childless collapse in grief.
- Magistrate Lou sentences the elderly accomplices to death by chariot-splitting; Li Huowang suggests beheading instead, but Lou argues that age does not absolve sin.
- Back at the inn, Li Huowang sleeps for a full day and night, wakes to find five new identical Daoist robes prepared by the magistrate, and a cleaned, gift-laden carriage ready to go.
- Mantou is fat and happy, gnawing on a bone—a small visual gag that contrasts with the chapter's weightiness.
- Li Huowang refuses the magistrate's farewell feast and prepares to depart for Yinling, prompting the monk's punchline: "Good deeds bring good rewards."

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Renxiao (人魈)** – In Chinese folklore and Dao-twisted cosmology, a "Renxiao" is a creature born out of extreme, prolonged suffering in old age. It is blind, covered in long hair like a shell, with curled nails, and only speaks in "ghost tongue." In this novel, it lurks somewhere between a failed ascetic and a natural calamity—a horrifying sentinel made from human despair. Its use of a *small* cauldron for de-boning before alchemy suggests a refinement process different from Danyangzi's gruesome methodology, but equally monstrous.

**Heavenly Scripture (天书) – Redux** – The repeated appearance of the Heavenly Scripture (identical in content to Danyangzi's, a Buddhist sutra preaching kindness) continues the novel's central mystery. This "scripture" seems to be a mass-produced cognitive trap: a text that *looks* like a transcendent treasure but is actually just a mundane, even pious text. The horror is not what it contains but what it *represents*—the gap between the world's promise of salvation and the twisted reality of its rituals. Li Huowang's decision to throw it into the river is a statement: he's done chasing false revelations.

**Death by Chariot-Splitting (车裂)** – Also known as the "Five Horses Tear the Body Apart," this was an ancient Chinese execution method reserved for the most heinous crimes. Magistrate Lou's insistence on it for the elderly accomplices reflects not just the novel's harsh legal system (based on Great Liang law) but also his personal fury at the atrocity of infant alchemy. Li Huowang's suggestion of beheading is a rare moment of pragmatic mercy, but Lou's rebuttal—that "age does not erase crime"—is a brutal reminder that in this world, justice is absolute, not sentimental.

## Reading Guide

**On "Good Deeds" and the Weight of Virtue** – This chapter's title is dripping with irony. Li Huowang *did* do good—he saved children, killed a monster, and brought justice. But he feels none of the warmth the monk keeps insisting on. His refusal of the feast, his dismissive "yes, yes" when the monk asks if he's happy, and his mechanical departure all point to a man running on fumes, not virtue. The real theme here isn't "good deeds feel good"—it's "good deeds add to the ledger of things you have to carry." Li Huowang walks away with lifespan pellets, a cauldron, and clean clothes, but none of that fills the emptiness of knowing how easily the world can turn children into ingredients.

**The Monk's Cheerfulness as a Foil** – The monk's relentless positivity is deliberately annoying. It's not that he's wrong—he *is* happy because they saved lives—but his joy feels inappropriate in the face of what Li Huowang has seen. This contrast is a quiet character beat: Li Huowang has lost the ability to feel good about his victories, while the monk (who has not experienced the same level of contamination) still sees the world in clear moral colors. It's a subtle but effective way to show how far Li Huowang has fallen from the simple calculus of "right vs wrong."

**What Li Huowang Leaves Behind** – He doesn't stay for the feast. He doesn't wait for the execution. He doesn't even ask the families' names. This is a protagonist in full "move on" mode, treating closure as an unaffordable luxury. The carriage, clean and fully provisioned, is a symbol of the story's forward momentum: there's always another road, another horror, another "good deed" that feels like a debt, not a reward.

**Classified Note for the Pipeline** – Pay attention to Li Huowang's flattened affect when processing the Renxiao's death and the lifespan pellets: he is moving from reactive horror to a cold, almost bureaucratic acceptance of his own monstrous circumstances.
