## Chapter Overview

Li Huowang pulls off something he never could have done in the Dao-Twisted World—he heals. And he does it not with alchemy, chanting, or blood sacrifice, but with cold, hard psychiatric knowledge from White Tower. This chapter is a curveball of pure empathy, showing that sometimes the only “exorcism” needed is sunlight, dignity, and a little bit of human touch. It’s a quieter, more emotional breather after weeks of raw horror, and it lets Li Huowang finally feel like something more than a cornered animal.

## Key Plot Points

Li Huowang’s improvised “treatment” of Zhao Qin’s wife works. Her eyes clear for the first time in thirty years. He explains to a stunned Bai Lingmiao that the woman wasn’t possessed by a demon—she suffered a psychological break after a massive trauma and was locked in a dark room for three decades, which only made things worse. He gives Zhao Qin practical advice: don’t lock her up, let her hold things (kids, fake dolls, dogs), get her sunlight, and stabilize her emotions rather than try to “cure” her overnight. Zhao Qin, overwhelmed with gratitude, throws a lavish banquet and offers Li Huowang anything he needs. Li Huowang seizes the opportunity to ask for a good sword manual, both for Chun Xiaoman’s growth and his own fighting capability. There’s also a quiet moment where he confronts Chun Xiaoman for acting without his permission, asserting his authority as the group’s leader.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

This chapter is a rare inversion of the genre’s standard “exorcism” trope. In traditional xianxia, mental illness is almost always literal demonic possession, cured by chanting scriptures or overpowering an evil spirit. Here, Li Huowang does the opposite: he reframes the “curse” as trauma, and the “cure” as psychiatric care. This is a brilliant authorial choice that reinforces the novel’s core tension—Li Huowang’s two worlds (modern psychiatry vs. supernatural folklore) are not mutually exclusive. Sometimes, a locked room is just a locked room. The novel also touches on the idea of *“jiu bing cheng yi liang”* (久病成良医), a Chinese proverb meaning “long illness makes the patient a doctor.” Li Huowang’s past suffering directly becomes a skill, which is a powerful character moment in a story where his madness is usually his greatest weakness.

### jiu bing cheng yi liang

A Chinese proverb meaning "long illness makes the patient a doctor"; refers to the hard-earned insight gained from personal suffering.

### Bang Bing Jue

A traditional northeastern Chinese spirit-summoning verse; its rapid, urgent rhythm belongs to the chuma spirit medium tradition.

## Reading Guide

This is not a chapter to skim for action. It’s a character-building interlude that pays off Li Huowang’s backstory. If you’ve been wondering if his hospital stay was “wasted,” this is the answer. The empathy he shows is raw, earned, and doesn’t lean on flashy cultivation. Zhao Qin’s breakdown is a masterclass in how the Dao-Twisted World treats its mentally ill—locked away, forgotten, and feared. And that moment where Li Huowang scolds Chun Xiaoman? It shows he’s growing into a leader, not just a survivor. Plus, the banquet scene is the closest we’ll ever get to a “happy meal” in this world.

## Cultural Deep Dive: The Role of Sunlight and Pets in Healing

Li Huowang’s advice to “get more sun” and “get a dog” might sound like modern common sense, but it has deep roots in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and psychiatric folk care. In TCM, sunlight is associated with yang energy, which can “warm” a cold, stagnant spirit. Keeping a pet, especially a dog, was historically seen as a way to ground a disturbed patient—dogs live in the present, demand routine, and give unconditional affection. Li Huowang isn’t just spouting modern psych; he’s finding the intersection where ancient folk healing and modern psychiatry agree.
