## Chapter Overview

Get ready, fellow truth-seekers… or rather, *immortal-seekers*? This chapter is a skull-splitting masterclass in identity torture. Danyangzi—fully in the driver's seat now—has reached a terrifying, logical conclusion: Li Huowang never existed. He was just a chunk of Danyangzi's own past, a heart-demon, one of the Three Corpses to be severed. In a dazzling display of mad certainty, Danyangzi laughs, kills, and performs a grotesque surgery using human heads as replacement organs. But the real gut-punch? A voice from *nowhere* calls him a waste. And then the bright, sterile lights of a psychiatric hospital flood the scene. The woman in modern clothes? She calls him "son." The Dao-Twisted World just got a visitor from the other side, and Danyangzi—for all his arrogance—cannot understand what he's seeing.

## Key Plot Points

- Danyangzi fully integrates his identity with Li Huowang's body, declaring the boy a heart-demon he has successfully "severed" as part of his Three Corpses cultivation.
- He kills the remaining survivors in the cavern and uses forbidden alchemy to replace his missing five viscera with five human heads (each corresponding to one of the Five Elements: Metal, Wood, Water, Fire, Earth).
- Danyangzi struggles with an invisible voice cursing him—a taunting presence neither he nor his Wandering Lords can locate.
- After a tense standoff, a modern woman (Li Huowang's mother) walks into the cave, dressed in completely anachronistic clothes, speaking of hospital procedures.
- As she addresses him as "son," the hallucinatory cave collapses into a bright, clinical reality—hinting that Danyangzi's consciousness has been pulled into the modern world.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

The "Three Corpses" (三尸, *San Shi*) are a core concept from Daoist internal alchemy. They are three parasitic, demonic entities residing in the head, chest, and belly, said to crave death, desire, and excess. A cultivator seeking immortality must "cut them off"—starve them of worldly attachment. In *Dao Gui Yi Xian*, this process is literalized and weaponized: Li Huowang wasn't a separate person, but Danyangzi's own discarded demon, given flesh and a life of its own. The ritual head-implant replacing the five viscera (五脏) draws from both the Five Phases / Five Elements (五行) theory and the horrifying folk tradition of "borrowing fate" from the dead. Danyangzi believes he's solved the puzzle; we readers know he's about to be shattered by a hospital bed.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a *trap*. The prose lulls you into Danyangzi's triumphant rhythm—the laugh, the kill, the surgical grafting of heads. You almost *believe* him. Then the curse hits from nowhere, and the woman in modern clothes enters. The shift is deliberately jarring: no warning, no magical portal. Just a tired mother and her paperwork. This is the novel's signature move: pitting the visceral, "real"-seeming horror of the Dao-Twisted World against the silent, undeniable gravity of psychiatric medicine. Danyangzi's "immortality" is about to meet a doctor's clipboard. Pay attention to his confusion—he can recognize silk, a box, a headscarf, but he cannot *categorize* this woman. That failure is the crack through which Li Huowang might return.
