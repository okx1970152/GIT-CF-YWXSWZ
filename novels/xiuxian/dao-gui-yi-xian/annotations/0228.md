## Chapter Overview
Just when you thought the Dao-Twisted World couldn’t get any fouler, this chapter lands like a cold slap. After a rare moment of warmth—shared meat, drunk laughter, and a full moon—Li Huowang’s curiosity gets the better of him. A casual question to Sun Baolu’s mother about the wealthy Liang Kingdom unravels into something far darker than any monster he’s faced so far. This is not a chapter of ghosts or rituals, but of human cruelty so mundane and so thorough that it makes the flesh Buddhas of Zhengde Temple look almost quaint. Get ready, because the horror here isn’t supernatural—it’s social.

## Key Plot Points
- During the celebratory feast, Sun Baolu’s mother, Wu Yue, reacts with visible nausea when Li Huowang praises the Liang Kingdom, calling it a place “where they don’t treat people like people.”
- Bai Lingmiao, who grew up in a small village in Liang, describes it as a paradise—a clear contrast to Wu Yue’s account, raising a red flag for Li Huowang.
- Unable to wait until morning, Li Huowang drags a drunken Sun Baolu outside and forces him to talk with a chilling display of sword intent.
- Sun Baolu reveals that his mother, at age fourteen, was employed as a “Beauty Paper”—a young woman used as a living piece of toilet paper for noble households.
- He further alludes to the even more grotesque “Beauty Spittoon” practice, confirming that the Liang Kingdom is a place of systemic, casual dehumanization of the poor and beautiful.
- Li Huowang’s initial disgust gives way to a grim, weary acceptance: in this broken world, there is no escape, only varying degrees of horror.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter introduces a pair of terms that hit harder than any sword: “Beauty Paper” (美人纸) and the hinted “Beauty Spittoon” (美人盂). These are not made-up inventions by the author—they are historical references to cruel practices in China’s imperial past. A “Beauty Spittoon” was a young, beautiful maidservant whose job was to stand beneath her master as he slept, holding a basin to catch his spittle, sometimes all night. The “Beauty Paper” is an even more degrading variation: a person used as a living piece of toilet paper, washed and reused. These were real, documented exploitations rooted in the extreme class stratification and commodification of women’s bodies in pre-modern Chinese society. The name “Liang Kingdom” is itself a crafty bit of irony—it sounds like “good” or “fine,” but in the Dao-Twisted World, it’s anything but.

## Reading Guide
Brace yourself. This chapter’s horror is quiet, personal, and totally without monsters. The punchline of “they used my mother to wipe other people’s asses” is meant to land with the same visceral force as any dismemberment in the *Thousand Greats Record*. Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s reaction: he shifts from shock to nausea to a flat, exhausted pragmatism. That’s the character arc right there—the moment he stops hoping for a clean escape and accepts that this world is rotten to its core, everywhere. Bai Lingmiao’s innocence about her homeland isn’t a plot hole; it’s a shield. She only saw her tiny village, not the full beast. And Sun Baolu? His final confession, delivered through gritted teeth and drunken terror, is one of the most vulnerable moments in the story so far. Painful, necessary, and unforgettable.

## The Social Horror of the "Beautiful" (新增深度小节)
This chapter doesn't just dip into historical cruelty—it weaponizes beauty itself. In most xianxia and wuxia stories, a woman’s beauty is either a tool of seduction, a sign of spiritual cultivation, or a tragic magnet for villainous attention. Here, beauty is a *liability* in the most degrading way imaginable. Wu Yue wasn’t sold into a brothel; she wasn’t taken as a concubine. Her beauty was valued only as a luxury consumable—an object for the most intimate and humiliating of bodily functions. This reframes the entire power structure of the Liang Kingdom: wealth there isn’t about owning the most land or the strongest army. It’s about having so much that you can afford to use a person as a rag and then wash her for tomorrow. The system itself *is* the monster, and that’s far harder to stab.
