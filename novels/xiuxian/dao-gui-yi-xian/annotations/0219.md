## Chapter Overview

Welcome to Qingqiu, folks—a land where the hills look like sleeping giantesses, the locals have their own language just to make life difficult, and the signature dish is noodles that look like a green curtain from a fever dream. Our favorite barely-functioning cultivator, Li Huowang, limps into a nomadic trading post with his blindfolded crew, hoping to restock supplies before they're reduced to eating the ox. The good news: they find food. The bad news: everyone stares at them like they're a walking plague banner. Also, Mantou the dog brings back a horse bone as a gift. It's a slice-of-morbid-life chapter that lets us breathe before the next horror drops.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang and his group arrive at the Qingqiu market nestled in a series of low, grass-covered hills called Woman Mountain. The landscape is described as abstractly feminine, but Li Huowang can't see it.
- Sun Baolu's excitement peaks: he confirms this is his ancestral territory and reunites with a fellow tribesman, speaking in a local dialect that Li Huowang cannot understand at all—a frustrating new obstacle for their journey.
- The chapter introduces the Qingqiu people as a distinct ethnic/cultural group: red-faced from sun exposure, stockier build, dressed in rough leather and bone ornaments, with a material culture heavy on grass-woven goods and horse products.
- Li Huowang prioritizes logistics over sentiment: he commands the group to rest and eat at a local noodle shop, revealing his pragmatic, survival-first mindset even as Sun Baolu buzzes with personal hope.
- The chapter ends on a darkly comic domestic note: Mantou returns from scavenging with a horse bone, prompting Sun Baolu to casually explain that yes, Qingqiu people eat horses. The mundane horror of "pet brings home the skeleton of someone's meal" is left hanging.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Qingqiu (青丘)**—The name "Qingqiu" has deep roots in Chinese mythology. In the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* (Shanhaijing), Qingqiu is a legendary land where nine-tailed foxes dwell. The novel reuses this name for a sparsely populated nomadic region, inverting the mythic into the mundane while keeping the sense of an alien, culturally distinct frontier. The famous nine-tailed fox from later folklore (Daji in *Fengshen Yanyi*) was said to be from Qingqiu.

**青丘话 (Qingqiu Language)**—The sudden language barrier is a notable shift in the novel's worldbuilding. Throughout the story, all regions have shared a common tongue for the reader's convenience—but here, Li Huowang is confronted with an actual linguistic divide. This is unusual even in xianxia fiction, where translation conventions usually paper over such differences. It makes Qingqiu feel genuinely foreign and difficult, adding a layer of practical helplessness for the protagonist beyond supernatural threats.

**女人山 (Woman Mountain)**—Place names in Chinese culture often use anthropomorphic or gender-coded descriptions. "Woman Mountain" follows a naming pattern common in folk geography, where a landscape's silhouette is read as a reclining female form. The trope appears in various regional Chinese folktales, often tied to tragic love stories or goddess figures. The novel's matter-of-fact naming hints that the local folk culture treats this as an ordinary landmark, drawing no supernatural attention to it—which, in *this* book, is almost a relief.

**Moon Year (月亮年)**—Sun Baolu mentions "Moon Year" in relation to his family's nomadic cycle. In many Chinese minority and herding cultures, seasonal migrations were timed by lunar phases or traditional animal-based calendars. The term here is a worldbuilding detail suggesting that Qingqiu society operates on a different temporal rhythm than the settled agricultural regions Li Huowang has passed through, further emphasizing its cultural isolation.

### Red-faced spots (脸斑)

Sun-induced complexion marks common among the Qingqiu people, marking them as visibly distinct from other regions.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is one of those rare "breather" segments where the horror is mostly economic and logistical. No demons, no flesh Buddhas, no blood sacrifices—just the quiet, grinding tension of being foreigners in a strange land with empty bellies and no shared language. Li Huowang's vigilance here is turned onto the social world: he notes the stares, the dialect, the strange customs, filing everything away as potential threat data. The scene at the noodle shop is almost cozy, if you ignore the fact that half the group looks like walking medical anomalies under their veils. And Mantou bringing back a horse bone is a perfect microcosm of the novel's tone: just when you think things are normal, the dog drops a piece of someone's dinner at your feet, and no one even flinches. Enjoy the calm before the storm, reader. Qingqiu is not done showing its teeth.
