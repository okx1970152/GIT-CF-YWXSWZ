## Chapter Overview

After the relentless chaos of recent chapters, "The Village" offers a momentary—and deeply deceptive—breathing spell. Li Huowang leads his group to a nameless mountain village for the night, where the mundane rhythms of peasant life (pigweed, summer heat, drafts from kicked-off blankets) mask the slow creep of two parallel horrors: one spiritual, one human. A child misidentifies a sinister flower with charming confidence. Chun Xiaoman secretly steals the *Thousand Greats Record* and begins forcing herself to endure its bloody knowledge. And in the final moment, the Second Spirit delivers a quiet omen that shatters the illusion of peace: the village has work for her drum. This chapter is a masterclass in quiet dread—every detail feels normal, but every normal detail is a wire waiting to be tripped.

## Key Plot Points

- The group arrives at a quiet village (Qian Family Village) and negotiates lodging with a guileless local boy.
- Li Huowang warns the group not to touch a strange red flower he identifies as "bǐ'àn," but a village boy cheerfully corrects him—it's "crab flower," harmless to humans but bad for pigs. The moment is ambiguous: is Li Huowang's superstition correct, or is the boy's folk knowledge simply more grounded?
- Chun Xiaoman is caught vomiting and secretly studying the *Thousand Greats Record*, which she retrieved from the cave without telling anyone. Her internal monologue reveals her driving motivation: she refuses to be useless and is determined to pay her own debts, even at the cost of enduring the text's horrific rituals.
- Bai Lingmiao misinterprets Xiaoman's symptoms as morning sickness and engages in a flustered, private speculation about who the father might be—a moment of almost comedic misunderstanding that underscores the group's hidden tensions.
- The chapter ends with the Second Spirit's chilling directive: "There's work to do in the village." The quiet is over.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**"Crab Flower" vs. "Bǐ'àn" (彼岸)**: This chapter plays a subtle folk-knowledge game. In the novel, "bǐ'àn" (彼岸, "the other shore") is a Buddhist term for nirvana or the far side of existence, and in Chinese folk religion it is also a name for the spider lily (*Lycoris radiata*), a flower associated with death and the underworld. The village boy's dismissive "crab flower" (螃蟹花) is actually a real local name for the same plant. The joke is layered: Li Huowang's esoteric knowledge is technically correct, but the boy's pragmatic peasant knowledge is just as valid. Neither is "wrong"—which makes the flower's potential danger all the more ambiguous. This is classic Dao-Twisted worldbuilding: the supernatural and the mundane share the same words, and trusting either can kill you.

**The *Thousand Greats Record* as Forbidden Knowledge**: Chun Xiaoman's decision to study the *Record* is a significant character turn. In traditional xianxia, secret manuals are tools of empowerment. Here, they are visceral tests of endurance—the text causes physical revulsion (vomiting) simply from reading it. This aligns with the novel's core aesthetic: power in the Dao-Twisted World is never free, and even *knowing* the price is part of the price. Xiaoman's determination to "pay her debts" by studying it mirrors Li Huowang's own trajectory, setting up a parallel thread of self-destructive resilience.

**The Second Spirit's "Work"**: The Second Spirit's final line—"there's work to do in the village"—is a classic folk-horror trope rooted in the chuma medium tradition. In real-world practice, a spirit-dancer is called upon when a community senses a spiritual disturbance (a restless ghost, a broken taboo, a lingering curse). The fact that the Second Spirit senses work *before* any visible sign appears means the village's danger is already present, just not yet visible to the living. The reader is now waiting for the shoe to drop.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in the "calm before the storm" structure—a structural staple of Chinese web novels, but executed here with unusual restraint. There is no monster, no chase, no battle. The tension comes entirely from what *isn't* said: the flower that might be nothing, the text that makes you sick just to read it, the Second Spirit's cryptic warning. If you're a reader used to non-stop action, resist the urge to skim. Every single detail in this chapter—the boy's laughter, Xiaoman's gagging, Bai Lingmiao's blush—is a thread that will be pulled taut later.

Also, take a moment to appreciate the PS at the end: the author confirms that his grandmother really did warn him about feeding "crab flowers" to pigs. This is what grounds the novel's horror in the real—the scariest line in the chapter might just be a piece of authentic folk wisdom.
