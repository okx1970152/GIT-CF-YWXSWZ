## Chapter Overview

Alright, fellow Daoist traveler, take a deep breath. Chapter 117 is a somber, lonely hike through a landscape of death and unanswered questions. We leave the Anci Nunnery behind and set off with Li Huowang on a two-day journey to find the mysterious Ao-Jing Sect. But the trail leads through a **massacred town** and collapsed temples, and our boy’s gut tells him something is *very* wrong. This is a slow-burn horror chapter, heavy on atmosphere and light on action, where the *absence* of a sect is more terrifying than meeting them face-to-face.

## Key Plot Points

- **A New Companion**: Li Huowang formally names the stray dog “Mantou” (Steamed Bun). He admits that, on top of wanting company, the dog is his first “test subject” to see if his hallucinating self will hurt other living beings.
- **The Dead Town**: After a two-day march, Li Huowang hits the mountain’s base, only to be blocked by a town that has been completely slaughtered. Skeletons, collapsed houses, and an eerie silence greet him.
- **First Signs of Ruin**: He passes through the dead town and begins climbing the mountain. He finds a collapsed Temple of the Cross belonging to the Ao-Jing Sect, with its unique, withered-leaf symbol.
- **Second Temple & The Headless God**: Li Huowang finds another ruined outpost. Inside, a charred, flame-wrapped statue of their deity sits on a black cross—its entire head has been shattered.
- **The Gou God**: Brushing away cobwebs, Li Huowang finds an inscription that names the deity: the **Gou God**. The lines are a twisted version of a classic Daoist text from the *Daodejing*.
- **The Unanswered Question**: The chapter ends with Li Huowang’s dread solidifying. The Ao-Jing Sect, a group powerful enough to survive the Anci Nunnery’s hostility, seems to have been destroyed by an unknown force.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Ao-Jing Sect (袄景教):** As hinted by the nuns, this is a fictional fusion of two very real, historically influential religions in China: **Zoroastrianism** (袄教, also known as Xianism, the ancient Persian faith of Ahura Mazda) and **Nestorian Christianity** (景教, the Church of the East that reached Tang Dynasty China). The name itself is a portmanteau. Their symbol, a “withered black lucky leaf” that looks like a cross, is a clever hybrid of the Christian cross and the aesthetic of a religion fading away.

**The Inscription & The Gou God:** The text Li Huowang finds is a corruption of the *Dao De Jing* (Chapter 6): “The spirit of the valley never dies / This is called the mysterious female / The gate of the mysterious female / Is called the root of heaven and earth.” Here, it is rewritten to deify “The Gou God” (牯神, ‘Gu Shen’—the god of a male ox or bull). The substitution of a virile, sacrificial beast-god for Laozi’s abstract “female/mother” principle is a classic *Dao Gui Yi Xian* move: taking a quiet, philosophical concept and making it raw, bloody, and monstrous.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in **negative space horror**. The terror isn’t in what Li Huowang finds, but in what’s *missing*. The Ao-Jing Sect was built up as a dangerous alternative to Danyangzi, so stepping into their empty, desecrated territory is profoundly unsettling.

Pay close attention to Li Huowang’s emotional rhythm. The warmth he shows Mantou—naming him, feeding him, admitting he wants a friend—makes the cold silence of the dead town hit harder. The chapter builds a quiet, predatory dread, leaving you with a single, awful question: if something powerful enough to destroy the Ao-Jing Sect is out there, what chance does a lone, half-mad Daoist with a dog have?
