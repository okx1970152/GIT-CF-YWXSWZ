## Chapter Overview
Pack your bags, folks — Li Huowang is finally ready to roll. After resolving the Black Tai Sui issue and scrubbing Han Fu’s Nascent Soul out of his system, our favorite paranoid cultivator has no more business in Qingqiu. But before he heads out, he’s got one last errand: fixing Sun Baolu. Not just a goodbye chat — he actually **corrects the body horror** Danyangzi inflicted on him, moving misplaced organs back where they belong. It’s a rare moment of healing in a world that only knows how to break. But of course, the Dao-Twisted World doesn’t let anyone leave without planting seeds of unease. Beneath the celebration, warning signs are already crawling up the walls.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang announces his departure from Qingqiu. Sun Baolu begs him to stay longer, but Li is firm.
- Li Huowang gives Sun Baolu a serious warning: don’t tell anyone about the Lion-Masks’ true nature, and act as if nothing is wrong. He suspects the Great Khan tacitly approves of them.
- Li Huowang asks if Sun Baolu’s romantic partner knows about his body’s deformity. When Baolu confirms she does and accepts him, Li offers to fix him.
- Using his experience from his own self-repair, Li Huowang corrects Baolu’s Danyangzi-induced organ misplacement. The result is crooked but functional — and Baolu is ecstatic to finally be “a person.”
- The Qingqiu people witness the correction and regard Li Huowang with awe and reverence.
- At the farewell feast, Gouwa suggests staying in Qingqiu. Li dismisses him, but Gao Zhijian ominously warns that something is *wrong* with this place — though he can’t articulate what.
- The chapter ends with the group departing Qingqiu, the Black Tai Sui safely bound, and an uneasy feeling lingering.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
This chapter presents a rare but potent contrast: **healing vs. systemic unease**. Li Huowang’s correction of Sun Baolu is a literal act of restoration — moving misplaced flesh and organs back to where they *should* be in a human body. It’s a quiet, almost surgical intervention rather than a flashy miracle, which fits the novel’s aesthetic of visceral, practical body horror. The fact that the result is “a bit crooked” reinforces that Baolu is not a cultivator — he can't force perfection on himself like Li Huowang can.

The ritual tool Li uses is described as a **white tassel (白穗)** — in Daoist and folk practice, tassels or brushes were sometimes used in talismanic or “adjustment” rituals, aligning energy or physical placement. Here, it’s used for straight-up **reconstructive fleshcraft**, keeping the novel’s pattern of grounding supernatural aid in gruesome bodily reality.

Gao Zhijian’s unease — a stuttering, unformed intuition that “something is wrong” — is a classic horror trope refined in this novel. He’s the character whose *instinct* catches patterns the rational mind can’t. His distrust of the feast and the cheerful Qingqiu hospitality taps into a deep folk-horror principle: a celebration that feels *too* happy in a twisted world is almost certainly missing a dark price tag.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a **breathing episode** — a moment of calm after the storm, but one that carries the smell of something foul underneath the fresh grass. Pay attention to Gao Zhijian’s warning: he can’t say *why* Qingqiu feels wrong, but the novel is training us to trust the animal gut over the smiling face. The Lion-Masks being “the orthodox sect” is a chilling detail — if the monsters are the establishment, then the entire city is built on a foundation of corrupted rituals and silent complicity. Li Huowang is smart to leave. But the question is: did he leave in time, or did some of that wrongness slip into the oxcart with him?
