## Chapter Overview

Get ready, because the storyteller finally tells his story—and it's a doozy. This chapter is a massive lore drop disguised as a quiet rooftop chat. Zhuge Yuan lays out the true state of the world, and it's so much worse than anyone imagined. Li Huowang, meanwhile, pulls no punches in showing just how desperate his situation really is. It's a chapter of brutal honesty: about the body, about the empire, and about the impossible choice between death by madness and death by parasite. Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this is the kind of exposition that changes everything.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang bluntly rejects Zhuge Yuan's offer of a clean path to True Cultivation. To prove his urgency, he slices open his own arm, revealing the Black Tai Sui tentacles actively corroding his body. This visceral display forces Zhuge Yuan to understand the stakes.
- Li Sui cries in Li Huowang's mind, upset by the harsh truth that her presence is harming her "dad." Li Huowang has to comfort her even as he argues for his own survival.
- Li Huowang speculates that Bei Feng might also be using a form of True Cultivation, which makes Zhuge Yuan's dilemma even more complicated.
- Reluctantly, Zhuge Yuan agrees to let Li Huowang follow him, knowing that refusing would only make Li Huowang stalk them anyway. He decides it's safer to keep him in the open.
- Zhuge Yuan then explains his motive for stealing the Heart-Turbid: the kingdom of Great Qi is in chaos. The Grand Empress Dowager has reanimated a dead six-year-old emperor as a walking corpse, and the eunuchs have given it a false mind.
- The false emperor is issuing edicts to select maidens of pure Yin birth and conscript massive labor forces. Zhuge Yuan deduces they are building a massive tomb, not just a wall.
- Zhuge Yuan reveals his plan to use the refined Heart-Turbid scroll (an artifact of memory-erasure) as a key tool for his upcoming infiltration of the capital to stop the eunuchs and their Zhengde Temple allies.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Great Qi / Great Liang Puzzle:** A major source of Li Huowang's confusion in this chapter. In the novel's lore, "Great Qi" is not a historical Chinese dynasty but a corrupt, decaying empire that persists in the Dao-Twisted World. Li Huowang, coming from a modern context, remembers a timeline where it became "Great Liang," but the locals don't use that name. This isn't a retcon—it's a deliberate, ongoing hint that the historical timeline of the Dao-Twisted World is fractured or different from what Li Huowang remembers. It's another layer of "can't tell what's real."

**The "Walking Corpse" Emperor:** This concept draws from Chinese folk horror and Daoist necromancy. A walking corpse (xíng shī) is distinct from a normal zombie or ghost. It is a body animated by external force (here, alchemy) without any of the proper soul components (the Three Souls and Seven Spirits, 三魂七魄). An emperor who is a walking corpse is the ultimate symbol of a broken Mandate of Heaven—the dynasty is a hollow shell, going through the motions without any true life or legitimacy. The horror is political and metaphysical.

**Yin Year, Yin Month, Yin Hour:** This refers to a person born at a time when all three temporal pillars—the year, month, and hour (the day is not specified here)—fall under the Yin branch of the Chinese zodiac calendar. This is believed to create a person of extreme, pure Yin constitution. In folk horror and xianxia, such individuals are often sought as perfect sacrifices, mediums, or vessels for dark rituals, especially involving communication with the dead or the underworld.

**Corvée Labor (徭役, Yáo Yì):** In ancient China, this was an unpaid or minimally compensated labor tax that commoners owed to the state. It was used for massive public works like building canals, palaces, walls, and imperial tombs. A sudden, massive conscription for "tomb construction" is a classic sign of a dynasty's death throes—a megalomaniacal project that bleeds the land dry while serving no practical, living purpose.

### Three Souls and Seven Spirits (三魂七魄)

A core concept in Chinese metaphysics. A living person possesses three hun souls and seven po spirits; their absence means the body is a soulless husk, capable of only mechanical or corrupt action.

## Reading Guide

This is a "calm before the storm" lore dump, but it's a very specific kind of lore dump. It's not just exposition; it's Qi/real-world dissonance. Pay close attention to how Li Huowang's understanding of the world (Great Liang) clashes with Zhuge Yuan's (Great Qi). This isn't a mistake in the text; it's a direct clue about the broken state of reality. Also, notice the contrast: Zhuge Yuan is operating on a grand, political stage—stabilizing an empire. Li Huowang is fighting a war for the integrity of his own flesh and mind. Their alliance is fascinating because their problems are so different in scale, yet they share the same root: a world where the line between true and false has been poisoned.
