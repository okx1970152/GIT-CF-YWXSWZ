## Chapter Overview
Li Huowang descends into the flooded underbelly of a doomed ship and finds himself face to face with eight living root-carving demons styled after the Eight Immortals—except these are no benevolent folk heroes. They twist, move, laugh, and attack with uncanny coordination. Forced into a desperate underwater brawl, Li Huowang pushes every trick at his disposal: fire, pain-sharing, rift-cutting, and yes, his own tentacle-filled throat. The chapter is a raw showcase of the Dao-Twisted World at its most viscerally deceptive: even wood carved into gods can be hungry.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang discovers a set of root-carved Eight Immortals in an emptied cargo hold; they come to life when he tries to pass them.
- The immortals speak in a strange, layered chorus and attack with coordinated, disorienting movements.
- Li Huowang uses a steel striker to set himself ablaze, then contagiously ignites the carvings—only for seawater to flood in and extinguish the flames.
- He uses his rib–pain-sharing technique to stun the enemies.
- Attempts to cut open the ship’s hull are thwarted by the self-repairing wood.
- The charred, healed immortals pursue him underwater as oxygen runs out.
- At the brink of blackout, a hallucination of taking a deep breath and diving back down gives him a second wind.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Eight Immortals (八仙)** are a well-known Taoist folkloric pantheon: Li Tieguai, Lü Dongbin, He Xiangu, Han Zhongli, Zhang Guolao, Han Xiangzi, Cao Guojiu, and Lan Caihe. Each has their own emblem and story (lotus, flute, gourd, etc.). In standard lore, they symbolize common people achieving transcendence. Here, “Twisted” versions of them becomes walking root carvings that move, speak, and attack—a perfect example of how *Dao Gui Yi Xian* corrupts every familiar celestial icon into something malevolent. The phrase “semi-immortal” (半仙) in Chinese folk tradition often refers to beings on the cusp of immortality but not yet fully divine; here it is used to belittle the failed copies.

**Root carving (根雕)** is a traditional Chinese art form that uses gnarled tree roots as the medium, often shaped into human figures or animals. The uncanny effect of them coming to life taps into a deep fear of the inanimate suddenly animating—especially when they parody the familiar, comfortable Eight Immortals.

**Pain-sharing rib (肋骨插胸)** is Li Huowang’s graphic adaptation of the Ao-Jing Sect’s techniques: he physically inserts his own rib into his chest to broadcast his suffering to everything around him. This is not a “skill cooldown” but a real, grisly medical-magical procedure.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a pure survival sequence: close-quarter combat underwater with infinitely regenerating enemies. Notice how Li Huowang operates: no grandstanding, no witty one-liners—just hyperfocused tactics. He doesn’t waste time wondering what the Eight Immortals are; he cuts first, asks never. The ending is a classic cliffhanger: he’s drowning and hallucinating, but the hallucination of surfacing actually recharges him. Is that luck? Or is his mind literally reshaping reality again? Stay tuned.

## Deep Dive: The Ominous “Sutra” Recitation
The line the immortals speak—*“The superior scholar has no quarrel…”*—is a distorted fragment from  a classical Daoist text (often from the *Zhongji Jing* or similar). It sounds like a moral teaching, but in context, it is used to mock Li Huowang, suggesting that his struggle is “inferior contention.” The Dao-Twisted World loves to twist scripture into taunts.
