## Chapter Overview
Alright, buckle up, fellow Daoists, because this chapter is a masterclass in *wrongness that feels right*. Li Huowang has just landed in Qingqiu, a place that everyone insists is safe and blessed by the Eternal Heaven. And at first, it seems fine! There's a local festival tradition, the people are friendly, and Sun Baolu is his usual enthusiastic self. But our boy Li Huowang is a finely-tuned instrument of paranoia now, and he can *feel* the discordant note under the cheerful surface. The chapter is a slow-burn dread-fest that starts with a bizarre headless lion dance and ends with a desperate, middle-of-the-night evacuation. It's a perfect example of how this novel can make a "safe zone" feel more terrifying than a battlefield.

## Key Plot Points
*   **The Headless Lion Dance:** Li Huowang witnesses a local Qingqiu tradition—a "lion dance" performed under a large, dirty white cloth. The key detail? No lion head. Sun Baolu explains this is because they are apprentices, not master dancers.
*   **The Wrong Numbers:** The hallucination-monk points out a terrifying discrepancy: there were far more hands holding up the cloth from the inside than there were feet walking underneath. This single observation shatters the illusion of normalcy.
*   **Immediate Night Flight:** Without a word of explanation to the group, Li Huowang decides they must leave *immediately* in the dead of night. They buy supplies (sheep, barley) in a hurry and hit the road under the moonlight, leaving "Woman Mountain" behind.
*   **The Group's Confusion:** The other characters, especially Chun Xiaoman and Sun Baolu, are completely baffled by Li Huowang's sudden, frantic behavior. They whisper amongst themselves, wondering if he's having another "episode" of his illness.
*   **The Uneasy Rest Stop:** The group stops by a river. While Gouwa cheerfully collects grasshoppers and wild vegetables, Li Huowang sits apart, silent and grim, his face a mask of sleepless paranoia. The contrast between his dread and the mundane foraging is deeply unsettling.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Lion Dance (舞狮):** What Li Huowang sees is a perversion of one of China's most recognizable and auspicious folk traditions. The traditional Lion Dance is performed during festivals (especially Lunar New Year) to bring good luck and drive away evil spirits. The lion costume is vibrant, with a large, expressive head manipulated by one dancer, while a second dancer forms the body. The head is the soul of the dance. Seeing a *headless* version being performed at night with funeral tassels is a masterful inversion of a positive symbol into a source of deep, uncanny horror. It's like seeing a Christmas carol sung in a minor key while mourners carry the tree. The "clacking" sound of wooden poles is also a real percussive element in some Chinese folk dances.

**Lion Dance Palace (舞狮宫):** This is a new piece of lore for the Dao-Twisted World. Sun Baolu mentions a specific institution—the "Lion Dance Palace"—that teaches these dances. In a conventional setting, this would be a harmless vocational school. In this story, the very name feels like a ticking time bomb, hinting at a secretive, organized supernatural force or a corrupted sect that uses the innocent facade of folk art as a cover for something far more sinister.

**The Hallucination-Monk's Arithmetic:** This moment is pure, beautiful brilliance. In a world of cosmic horrors and unthinkable gods, it is a *counting error* that reveals the truth. The monk isn't seeing ghosts or reading auras; he's just doing the math and noticing the numbers don't add up. This fits perfectly with the novel's theme of "cognitive horror"—the terror comes not from a monster revealing itself, but from a flaw in reality's logic. The simple observation that there are "more hands than feet" is far more chilling than a full-on monster reveal would have been.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a textbook example of Li Huowang's "trust the feeling, not the evidence" survival philosophy. Qingqiu **looks** safe. **Feels** safe... to everyone else. But Li Huowang has learned that his madness is often the only thing that can see the cracks in the world. Pay close attention to how he doesn't even bother to explain himself anymore. He just gives an order to leave, and the group, who have seen him be right too many times, obeys out of pure, terrified respect.

Also, keep your eye on that "Lion Dance Palace" in the back of your mind. This is the kind of throwaway lore drop that *Dao Gui Yi Xian* loves to plant and then harvest for maximum horror fifty chapters later. It’s a name that triggers a faint bell of recognition in Li Huowang's memory, and that's never a good sign. The real horror here is the *absence* of overt horror—the dissonance between what everyone tells him is safe and what his gut screams is a trap.
