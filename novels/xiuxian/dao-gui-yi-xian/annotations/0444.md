## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow Daoists, because this chapter is a header! After the soul-crushing revelations about his identity as Hong Zhong, our favorite cognitive mess Li Huowang gets a new lead that reignites his manic energy. Zhuge Yuan drops a bombshell about the nature of reality itself—the past might not be as fixed as we think!—but this only serves to supercharge Li Huowang’s obsession. Instead of panicking, he does what he does best: exploits the situation. The chapter takes us on a moonlit journey to a creepy town during Qingming Festival, where there’s some good old-fashioned “Villain Beating” (打小人) happening, leading to a hilariously tense and weirdly specific mahjong game. But the goal is clear: to unearth a real cultivation manual hidden inside the false memories of Hong Zhong. It’s a wild ride of existential dread, folk horror, and a singular moment of triumph that feels way too good to be safe.

## Key Plot Points

- **Zhuge Yuan’s Hypothesis:** Zhuge Yuan explains to Li Huowang that the very nature of reality is mutable. Using the example of Great Qi’s history being overwritten by Great Liang’s false records, he argues that a person’s own past could be subject to the same kind of change. This directly supports the idea that Li Huowang’s “Hong Zhong” past is a fabrication installed by Doumu.
- **Li Huowang’s Shift in Focus:** After the initial shock, Li Huowang makes a critical decision. He chooses to set aside the existential horror of the “Hong Zhong” identity and the question of Doumu’s ultimate purpose. Instead, he prioritizes a tangible gain: the authentic cultivation manual hidden within those false memories. This marks a key shift from victim to opportunist.
- **The Journey and the Setting:** The chapter uses the Qingming Festival as a backdrop. The cold, wet weather, the flickering fires where people burn spirit money, and the creepy, rhythmic chanting of a woman performing “Villain Beating” create an atmosphere of mundane, visceral folk horror. It firmly grounds the high-concept revelation in the gritty reality of the Dao-Twisted World.
- **The Mahjong Gambit:** Li Huowang tracks down the old woman who was entrusted with the manual. The coded negotiation is bizarre and perfect: he declines her offer to “beat the villain” for a fee and instead asks to play mahjong—with a twist. He slaps a “Red Center” (Hong Zhong) tile on the table and demands that the missing fourth player be “written out.”
- **The True Manual Acquired:** The old woman and her daughter-in-law lay a huge sheet of rice paper on the table. As they write on opposite sides, the ink bleeds through, forming the text of the cultivation manual. Li Huowang reads the beginning lines and, driven by desperate hope, immediately prepares to cultivate it.
- **The Final Caution:** Just as Li Huowang is about to begin, Zhuge Yuan stops him with a chilling warning: this manual might be the trap, the next step in Doumu’s plan. It was placed inside the false memories specifically so Li Huowang would find it and cultivate it. He reminds Li Huowang that he is not Hong Zhong; that past is not his.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **Villain Beating (打小人, *dǎ xiǎorén*):** This is a genuine Chinese folk ritual, most famously performed at the Goose Neck Bridge in Causeway Bay, Hong Kong, but with variations across the country. It’s most commonly done during the “Awakening of Insects” (惊蛰, *jīngzhé*), a solar term believed to be when all creatures, including malicious spirits and “little people” (gossipers, backstabbers), stir from hibernation. The ritual involves using a shoe or slipper to beat a paper effigy of a person, accompanied by a curse-chant. The goal is to drive away bad luck and neutralize the negative influence of one’s enemies. Here, it’s perfectly repurposed as a cover for exchanging a forbidden text.
- **Qingming Festival (清明节):** Also known as Tomb-Sweeping Day, this is a major traditional Chinese festival for paying respects to one’s ancestors. People visit graves, clean them, make offerings, and burn paper money and paper effigies (*zhizha*). The timing of this chapter during Qingming is masterful—it normalizes the presence of fire, spirit money, and eerie rituals in the public space, making Li Huowang’s clandestine meeting blend right in with the mournful, somber atmosphere. The burning of “yellow spirit money” (黄纸) is a standard part of this ritual.
- **Hong Zhong (红中) as a Gambit:** The central mahjong tile of the game, the “Red Center,” becomes a powerful narrative symbol. Li Huowang slamming the physical tile onto the table is a declaration of his and the entity’s identity. Using the mahjong code to retrieve the manual is a brilliant, in-world solution. It highlights Li Huowang’s growing mastery of the Dao-Twisted World’s twisted logic and how he bends its own rules for his survival.

## Reading Guide

- **The Trap is a Feature, Not a Bug:** Zhuge Yuan’s final warning is the chapter’s sting in the tail. Everything about this feels too perfect—a real cultivation manual dropped into Li Huowang’s lap just when he needs it. The reader should feel the same chilling doubt as Li Huowang. Is this a genuine treasure he’s extracting from the false memories, or is Doumu simply baiting him with exactly what he wants most? The answer is probably both.
- **Li Huowang’s Mania is Back:** Watch how quickly Li Huowang goes from existential crisis to manic, focused action. He’s no longer the confused victim; he’s a predator on a hunt. His eyes “gleaming” and his dismissal of the identity problem in favor of the tangible reward show a ruthless pragmatism forged by suffering. It’s a powerful character moment, but it’s also a little scary. Is this Li Huowang, or the ghost of Hong Zhong guiding his hand?
- **The Folk Horror is Peak:** If you’re here for the atmosphere, this chapter delivers. The wet, cold night, the cackling old woman, the rhythmic curse-chant, the burning spirit money… it’s pure uncanny valley. This is a masterclass in using a real-world folk tradition to build a sense of creeping dread. It’s not a monster in the dark that’s scary; it’s the ritual itself, the fact that this is all perfectly normal in this world.
- **Don’t Trust the Happy Ending:** Li Huowang gets the manual. That’s a victory. But in the Dao-Twisted World, no victory is clean. The chapter ends on a note of frozen, agonizing decision. He has the key to power, but turning it could be the final step of a trap he walked right into. The real tension is in that pause, in the silence after his hands start to shake.

## The Mahjong Connection: More Than Just a Game

The use of mahjong here is genius, not just a quirky reference. In Chinese popular culture, especially within the "river and lake" (*jianghu*) underworld, code-speaking and using games as a front for criminal or clandestine deals is a classic trope. The mahjong table is a neutral ground, a social ritual that anyone can participate in. Li Huowang hijacks this neutral space. By slapping down the “Red Center” tile, he forces the game into a new set of rules—*his* rules. The act of the mother and daughter-in-law writing on opposite sides of the paper is a wonderfully visual metaphor for the “two truths” of the manual: one side is the objective text, the other is its purpose as a potential trap. It’s a layered, intelligent scene that rewards readers who know the cultural touchstones.
