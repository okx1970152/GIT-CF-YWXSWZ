## Chapter Overview
Our Daoist outcast is trying so hard to be a responsible leader—giving parting instructions, delegating authority, acting like he’s got it all figured out. But the cracks are already showing. A phantom murmur in his ear, one that the Black Tai Sui can’t silence, proves his calm is a mask that keeps slipping. And if that wasn’t enough to ruin his day, a royal parade forces him to kneel—and reminds him exactly whose boot he’s under in this empire. The Zuowandao might be the target, but Li Huowang is fighting more than one enemy.

## Key Plot Points
- Li Huowang splits the party, leaving Lü Zhuangyuan in charge of the main group and ordering Xiucai to stay behind and keep studying.
- He explains the threat of the Zuowandao clearly to his allies: shape-shifting liars with supernatural tricks who can fool anyone if you lose focus.
- The auditory hallucination reappears, and Li Huowang privately realizes the Black Tai Sui cannot suppress it—a serious setback for his mental stability.
- A prince of Yinling City holds up traffic; Li Huowang is surrounded by cavalry for not kneeling, but his Supervisory Heavenly Office token forces a hasty apology.
- The encounter sours him further—he sees the Heavenly Office as nothing more than a prop for imperial tyranny, and wonders why *these* people remain untouched by the world’s madness.
- The group reaches Gan Yuan Village, which appears surprisingly normal—a sign in this world that often means something is very wrong below the surface.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Four-Clawed Dragon & Royal Precedence** – In imperial Chinese sumptuary law, an emperor’s robe bore five-clawed dragons (*wuzhao long*), while princes and high nobility used four-clawed (*sizhao long*). Li Huowang’s quiet study of the banner is a casual display of how deeply this world’s rules are etched into every visual detail, even for an outsider. The dragon count is a cheat sheet to who outranks whom without a single proclamation.

**Kneeling as Social Gravity** – In pre-modern China, failing to kneel before a royal procession wasn’t just rudeness; it was a legal offense that could get a commoner beaten or arrested on the spot. The fact that a Daoist token lets Li Huowang *bypass* this law is itself a powerful reminder of how the Supervisory Heavenly Office sits above normal society—which makes his disgust at the system’s real purpose all the more bitter.

**Linglong Pagoda Face Paint** – The celadon-blue patterns on the prince’s attendants come from Linglong Pagoda, a nod to the novel’s own lore about tattooed slaves or marked servants. These are not just ornaments; they are caste markers, branding the women as property of a specific elite institution—a chilling detail that underscores how bodily autonomy has been completely erased.

## Reading Guide
The noise in Li Huowang’s head won’t stop. That’s the real threat in this chapter—not the prince, not even the Zuowandao, but the fact that his go-to medicine has failed him. He’s trying to manage it with rational pep talks (“just occasional hallucinations, nothing major”), but the repetition betrays the lie. The encounter with royalty is almost a relief by comparison: at least the prince is a concrete problem that can be solved with a token. For an invisible enemy inside his own skull, there is no token. The setup is perfect: send the crowd away, arm everyone with information, enter the village looking normal… and wait for the ground to open.
