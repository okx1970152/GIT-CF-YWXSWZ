## Chapter Overview

A night of supposed rest turns into a front-row seat for a culture clash. Our dusty crew, fresh off stocking up on coarse grain in a small village, finds their evening shattered—or enlivened—by the arrival of a rival opera troupe. While Li Huowang just wants to finish his cornmeal flatbread in peace, the Lü Family Troupe’s hackles are raised, and the rest of the gang sees a rare chance for entertainment. The chapter ends with Li Huowang on a rooftop, realizing that this “Great Liang” opera isn’t just a different dialect—it’s a different kind of strange.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang’s group buys a bulk lot of coarse grain from a reluctant village elder, secured only after a nod from the “Little Daoist Priest,” solidifying his role as the final authority in the group’s logistics.
- The group settles into borrowed grain silos for the night; Lü Zhuangyuan demonstrates his practical value by arranging a night watch for their valuable sheep, assigning his son and the dog Mantou.
- A commotion outside draws attention; Gouwa reports a traveling opera troupe setting up at the village entrance.
- Lü Zhuangyuan’s competitive instincts as a performer ignite instantly; he drops his dinner and marches out to scrutinize his rivals, revealing the deep-seated pride and tension of the itinerant performer class.
- The rest of the group follows, but Li Huowang initially refuses, only to be coaxed out by Bai Lingmiao’s pleading and the monk’s enthusiasm, a rare moment of social capitulation.
- From their rooftop perch, Li Huowang watches the opera and quickly discerns that despite the surface similarities, the performance’s style and content feel fundamentally “wrong” to him, signaling a potential threat or the world’s persistent weirdness bleeding through.
- The chapter’s conclusion is a distinct narrative hook: “he noticed something was off,” pivoting from a cultural observation into a direct source of unease.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Opera as a Mirror and a Minefield:** Traditional Chinese opera isn’t just a performance art—it’s a highly codified, regional signifier. For the Lü Family Troupe, an itinerant group, any other opera troupe is an automatic rival, a threat to their pride and their livelihood. The detailed description of the general’s costume (command flags, pheasant feathers) and the song lyrics identify this as a wu sheng (martial male) role, filled with bombastic, patriotic energy. However, for us, the real tension isn’t the artistic critique; it’s that in the *Dao-Twisted* World, a performance can be a summoning, a disguise, or a trap. Li Huowang’s final realization isn’t just about a missed note—it’s his survival instincts detecting that the mask might be more than just wood and paint.

**Coarse vs. Fine Grain:** The old man’s refusal to sell white flour (白面, *báimiàn*) isn’t just stinginess; it reflects a real historical burden on commoners. Fine white flour was a luxury and a tax commodity, often reserved for paying official grain taxes. Being forced to eat coarse grains like sorghum (高粱, *gāoliang*) and cornmeal (棒子面, *bàngzi miàn*) is a constant reminder of their low social and economic standing in this world.

### Rivalry in performance (同行是冤家)

A traditional Chinese saying meaning that people in the same trade are natural enemies; it embodies the fierce competition and pride among itinerant performers.

## Reading Guide

Welcome to a “slice of horror” chapter! Sure, no guts are spilled, and no fleshy gods are making house calls, but don’t let that comfort food fool you. This episode is a masterclass in building the *Dao-Twisted* World’s texture. Pay attention to the small details of power: Li Huowang’s silent nod authorizing the grain purchase, and his simple correction of the sheep-watch plan without debate. He’s no longer just a survivor; he’s the group’s silent anchor. The real treat, however, is watching Lü Zhuangyuan bristle with pride. His character gets a much-needed injection of life beyond being a grumpy old man. But the candy coating is bitter. That final line, “he noticed something was off,” is the book’s signature move. It reminds you that in this world, even a night at the theater is a reconnaissance mission. Enjoy the song, fellow Daoist, but keep one hand on your weapon. The moment you start enjoying it too much is when the mask decides to smile back.
