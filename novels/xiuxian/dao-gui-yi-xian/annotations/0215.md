## Chapter Overview

This chapter serves as a quiet, character-driven interlude that pulls the camera away from the cosmic horror and focuses on the mundane—and how the mundane has become *strange* in the shadow of Li Huowang’s collapse. We see the world through the eyes of Yang Xiao Hai, the young boy who has clawed his way from street urchin to functioning member of the crew. Through his daily chores—fetching water, lighting a fire, practicing his swordplay—we get a painfully normal slice of life. But this normalcy is a fragile crust over a pit of dread. The real payload is the distant, unnerving figure of Li Huowang, now reduced to a half-skinless, delirious wreck who talks to ghosts no one else can see. The chapter masterfully uses Yang Xiao Hai’s liminal perspective—too old to be innocent, too young to be hardened—to measure how far the group’s pillar has fallen, and how far the rest of them still have to go just to survive.

## Key Plot Points

- **Daily routines anchor the scene.** Yang Xiao Hai wakes up to fetch water, wash, and prepare the communal meal. We see a new, stronger version of the boy: no longer the starving waif, now muscled from travel and better fed.
- **Sword training with Chun Xiaoman.** Yang Xiao Hai practices with her by mimicking her form with a piece of firewood. She corrects him, offering real instruction. His motivation is pure and desperate: if he learns the sword, even as a beggar, no one will dare bully him.
- **Chore-turf war.** Yang Xiao Hai returns to find a new woman—a survivor from the bandit raid—has taken over fire-starting. He reclaims his job brusquely. The woman, sycophantic and eager to “help the benefactor,” tries to bond with him over a fake shared hometown.
- **The dawning horror of Li Huowang.** Curious, Yang Xiao Hai approaches the oxcart and overhears Li Huowang pleading with a ghost he sees—a monk who was killed by Peng Long Teng. When Li Huowang fixes his single eye on the boy, the description is visceral: half his face lacks skin, his lips are burned away, and his teeth clack as he talks to empty air.
- **Gouwa’s grim diagnosis.** Back at the camp, Yang Xiao Hai reports what he saw to Gouwa. They discuss the only known cure: the Black Tai Sui, which the “old scabby-head” once used to suppress the madness. But that substance is impossible to obtain now.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **Corpse Solution via Decay (尸解仙):** The characters briefly lament that Li Huowang’s “illness” isn’t something a doctor can cure, and that only the Black Tai Sui could suppress it. This echoes the novel’s recurring theme that powerful, sacred cures in the Dao-Twisted World are always found in grotesque, taboo materials. The “Mushroom Corpse Immortal” (尸解仙) referenced in the incremental glossary is a corrupted Daoist concept where rot and decay become paths to transcendence—a dark mirror of the traditional “Corpse Solution,” where an immortal sheds their body like a cicada’s shell. Here, the solution is not to shed the body but to fill it with parasitical rot.
- **Black Tai Sui (黑太岁):** This entity was introduced earlier as a parasitic, writhing black mass used in corrupt alchemy to suppress madness. The traditional cultural concept of “Tai Sui” (太岁) is that of a revered spirit of the year, but in this novel it is grotesquely inverted into a living, flesh-eating substance. The fact that the characters now crave this “cure” highlights how survival has twisted their sense of normalcy.
- **Hierarchy in a survival group:** Yang Xiao Hai’s fierce protection of his chores (snatching the flint, ordering the woman to fetch water) is not just childish possessiveness. In a world where every task defines your value to the group, losing your function means losing your right to stay. This is a survival instinct, not pettiness.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a brilliant palette cleanser that does more work than a hundred action scenes. Buckle up, fellow Daoists—because when a story forces you to slow down and watch a kid cook noodles, you know the other shoe is about to drop. Yang Xiao Hai’s POV is a lens of emotional clarity. He’s young enough to be scared, but old enough to understand *why* he should be scared. His observation that Li Huowang “keeps his cart deliberately far away” is a devastating bit of social geography: the leader is quarantining himself because he knows he’s becoming a monster. Watch how the new woman’s fawning servility contrasts with the old crew’s grim practicality. And pay attention to the final conversation between Gouwa and Yang Xiao Hai. The Black Tai Sui was a nightmare when it was inside Li Huowang’s body—now it’s the only thing they miss. That’s the Dao-Twisted World: it keeps moving the line of what’s “acceptable” until you’re praying for a parasite to come back.
