## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow travelers, because this chapter is a masterclass in corrosive frustration. Li Huowang is *seething*. After being tricked into killing an innocent person by the monk Xinchi’s faulty identification, he’s forced to swallow his rage and continue the mission. The target: flushing out the Zuowandao from the Bone Buddha Temple. But our protagonist isn’t just angry; he’s getting sharp. While the rest of the crew follows the Chief Recorder’s lead like ducklings, Li Huowang starts noticing the cracks. The temple is *too* quiet. The eunuch isn’t looking for people; he’s looking at beams and pillars, tapping that golden abacus like it holds a secret. It’s a chapter about the horror of being a cog in a machine that doesn’t explain itself, and the slow, cold realization that the person giving orders might not be hunting the enemy—they might be looking for something else entirely. Oh, and the knife-pulling cliffhanger? Pure, agonizing tension.

## Key Plot Points

*   Li Huowang is mentally and emotionally reeling from killing the wrong person based on Xinchi’s word. This isn't shock; it's a cold, bone-deep disgust directed at the monk.
*   The other monk, the one who was also a participant, is having a full-blown meltdown, crying and trying to physically attack Xinchi, only to pass harmlessly through his projection.
*   The dynamic within the group shifts. Xinchi is now seen as a liability and a moral abomination, but Li Huowang pragmatically acknowledges they can't afford to break up the team.
*   The Chief Recorder’s behavior becomes the central focus. He ignores everyone’s comments and ignores the quest to find people, instead tapping his golden abacus and scanning the *architecture* of the temple halls.
*   The temple's own monks confront the party, but are quickly cowed by Tuoba Danqing’s official threat.
*   Li Huowang voices his suspicion that the Zuowandao are not here for mere chaos; they have a deeper, hidden agenda. The Chief Recorder’s dismissive “Just do your job” confirms the eunuch *knows* what that agenda is.
*   The chapter ends with Li Huowang on high alert. He's drawn his sword based on Xinchi's sudden, urgent warning, but the target is a seemingly innocent worshipper. By the time he hesitates, the moment is lost.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Golden Abacus as a Diagnostic Tool:** This is a fascinating and deeply culturally specific detail. In traditional Chinese culture, the **abacus (算盘, suànpán)** isn't just a counting tool; it’s a symbol of calculation, commerce, and bureaucratic precision. The Chief Recorder, a eunuch official, uses his golden abacus not to tally money, but to “calculate” the metaphysical state of a location. This is a classic *xianxia* trope: powerful artifacts and beings don't just *detect* magic with glowing lights; they *compute* it, aligning with the Daoist concept of a universe governed by intricate patterns, numbers, and cycles. He’s not looking for a *person*; he’s looking for a *discrepancy in the cosmic accounts* of the temple. The abacus is his personal interface with the Dao of bureaucracy and order, making him a terrifying blend of civil servant and sorcerer.

**Eunuchs in the Supernatural Bureaucracy:** The Chief Recorder is described with **Ming-era eunuch** features: a powdered white face, bejeweled robes, and an effeminate, avuncular manner that can turn icy in a heartbeat. In Chinese history, eunuchs often held immense power within the palace, controlling information and access to the emperor. By placing a eunuch in this role, the novel weaponizes that historical archetype. His power is silent, bureaucratic, and absolute. He doesn’t need to shout or fight; he holds the official token and the golden abacus. He is the system itself, and Li Huowang is discovering that the system is just as opaque and terrifying as the chaos it's meant to police.

**The Unspoken Rules of the Office:** Tuoba Danqing’s shift in demeanor is a perfect example of jianghu social intelligence. He’s playful and casual with his subordinates, but the moment he addresses the Chief Recorder, his tone becomes sycophantic and servile. When confronting the temple monks, he is authoritative and threatening. This isn't being fake; it’s survival. In the *xianxia* world, rank is everything, and navigating the hierarchy is a skill deadlier than any sword. Li Huowang’s failure here is that he treats the Chief Recorder as a person to be reasoned with, when he should be treated as a force of nature to be obeyed or circumvented.

## Reading Guide

This is *not* a chapter about action. It’s a chapter about the suffocating feeling of being a tool. Li Huowang did a terrible thing, and the man who caused it is a walking absurdity they have to protect. The tension here is brilliant because it’s entirely psychological. You're supposed to feel frustrated right along with Li Huowang. The real fight isn’t against the Zuowandao (yet); it’s against the system—against the eunuch who won't explain, the monk who is wrong, and the mission that feels like a wild goose chase. Pay close attention to Li Huowang's shift in focus. He stops watching the enemy and starts watching his own allies. That’s the sign of a survivor who knows something is rotten. The cliffhanger is a masterful gut-punch. That single word, “Sorry” followed by “Too late,” doesn't just mean he missed a chance to kill a monster; it suggests he has just made a fatal mistake in failing to act.
