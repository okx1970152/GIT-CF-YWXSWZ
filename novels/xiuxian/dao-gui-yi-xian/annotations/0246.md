## Chapter Overview

This chapter is a quiet, visceral interlude of aftermath, recovery, and grim determination. After the desperate escape from the cave, Li Huowang wakes from a ten-day coma to find his group alive, the Black Tai Sui secured, and his own body a patchwork horror of poorly healed scars. But rather than flinching from the cost, he leans into it with forced cheerfulness—a defense mechanism learned from watching others endure unbearable things. The chapter ends on a knife-edge of intent: Li Huowang holds a piece of the Black Tai Sui to his mouth, ready to consume it, only to stop at the last possible moment. It’s a breath held, a choice suspended, and the silence before the next storm.

## Key Plot Points

- Li Huowang wakes from a ten-day coma in a cowhide tent, his body riddled with poorly healed cuts and disfigurements, but functionally recovering.
- Bai Lingmiao has been caring for him, passing him goat’s milk mouth-to-mouth to keep him alive; her relief at his awakening is profound but understated.
- The group has moved to Sun Baolu’s home on the grasslands; the Black Tai Sui and Han Fu’s severed head are secured in separate tents under watch.
- Li Huowang immediately prioritizes the Black Tai Sui, ignoring further rest to inspect it personally; his single-minded focus betrays a shift toward predatory opportunism.
- He identifies the Black Tai Sui as identical to the one in Qingfeng Temple, cuts a piece loose, and nearly swallows it raw—only to stop himself at the last moment.
- The narrative cuts between his painful self-examination, a brief flashback to a tender rooftop memory with Yang Na, and the cold logistical reality of his current position.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Black Tai Sui (黑太岁)** continues to serve as one of the most unsettling xianxia “resources” in the novel. In Chinese folk religion, *tai sui* (太岁) refers to both a celestial deity associated with years and to a mysterious subterranean or organic growth—sometimes called “flesh mushroom”—believed to have medicinal and alchemical properties. The real-world *tai sui* is often described as a gelatinous, fleshy mass found underground and treated with superstitious awe; this novel takes that folk-entity and grafts onto it a body-horror parasite that whispers, squirms, and regenerates. When Li Huowang cuts into it and it *shrieks*, the horror isn’t just visual—it’s a violation of something that was never supposed to be a resource. It’s alchemy without reverence.

**Mouth-to-mouth feeding with goat’s milk** is a small but significant cultural detail. In traditional Chinese caregiving—especially in rural settings—feeding an unconscious or bedridden patient by pre-chewing or warming food in the caregiver’s own mouth was a common expression of intimate devotion. Bai Lingmiao’s act here quietly mirrors the role of a wife or mother in classical domestic practice, reinforcing her position as the emotional anchor while Li Huowang drifts between life and death.

**The voluntary consumption of raw parasitic flesh** is a deliberate inversion of normal xianxia “elixir-taking.” In standard cultivation stories, a hero would never eat a live, screaming piece of a monster unless it had been refined into a pill. Li Huowang’s willingness to bypass the entire alchemical process and *bite directly into the horror* signals a man who has internalized the novel’s core rule: there is no clean way to power.

### Mouth-to-mouth feeding (口哺)

A traditional Chinese caregiving practice for unconscious patients, often involving warmed or pre-chewed food passed between mouths as an expression of intimate devotion.

## Reading Guide

This is a *recovery chapter* in the worst sense of the word—there’s no rest, no softness, but a careful inventory of everything that’s still broken. Li Huowang’s forced cheerfulness is one of the most uncomfortable things he’s done so far: he laughs to keep others from worrying, and it rings hollow enough to make Bai Lingmiao cry harder. Pay attention to *that disconnect*—he’s learning to be a leader, but he’s learning badly, applying survival logic to emotional wounds.

The rooftop memory with Yang Na is a deliberate emotional spike. After chapters of filth, pain, and parasitic horror, Li Huowang’s subconscious clings to the cleanest moment of his past life. That this memory is *torn apart by voices calling him back to the Dao-Twisted World* is the novel’s way of telling you that he can’t even keep his best self safe anymore.

And then there’s the ending beat: he raises the Black Tai Sui to his mouth, and *stops*. The chapter doesn’t tell you why. It just leaves you staring at that decision. Some readers might see it as a moment of clarity. Others might see the horror of what he’s about to do finally catching up to him. Either way—it’s a man on a very, very short leash.
