## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow Daoists, because this chapter is a masterclass in “don’t underestimate the protagonist.” Ji Ning, having just taken on a disciple, is cruising along the Evil Dragon River when a small army of Firewing Guards—three full squads, each led by a Loose Immortal old demon plus eleven Primal-level and over a hundred Wanxiang-level demons—comes charging in on a giant formation. The Qi royalty, whom Ji Ning is protecting, panic and pull a classic backstab: they seal Ji Ning inside a bamboo formation together with the three thousand-zhang Firewing Centipedes, hoping to slow down the pursuit. But their calculation is based on a fatal miscalculation. Ji Ning doesn’t just *hold his own*; he obliterates the entire demon army in one single sword strike, breaks the formation, and catches up in the space of a few breaths. The violet-robed princess who masterminded the trap? She learns the hard way that you don’t scheme against a Sword Immortal who has a Celestial Immortal ancestor—and that karma is a very, very fast boat.

## Key Plot Points

- The Qi royalty spots hundreds of Firewing Guards approaching and realizes that the previous twelve Guards’ complete annihilation has triggered a massive retaliation.
- The three squads merge their dao-soldier formations, manifesting three thousand-zhang Firewing Centipedes that charge across the river to kill Ji Ning.
- Prince Qi Rufeng and his sisters flee immediately, but the violet-robed princess Qi Ruhui tosses a bamboo formation treasure to trap Ji Ning and the centipedes together, intending to sacrifice him for time.
- Ji Ning, furious at the betrayal, strikes with three sword-lights that transform into black dragons, annihilating every single demon—three Loose Immortals, thirty-three Primal-level, and hundreds of Wanxiang-level—along with their souls and armor.
- He then breaks the formation with a single casual sword stroke and teleports onto the fleeing shuttle.
- Ji Ning utters a single cold line and kills Qi Ruhui on the spot with a flicker of sword-qi, reducing her to ashes. The chapter ends with the remaining royalty frozen in horror.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Dao-Soldier Formations & the Firewing Centipede:** This chapter showcases a classic Xianxia “dao-soldier” tactic: dozens or hundreds of trained soldiers wearing unified armor that links their power. The result is a giant beast phantom that multiplies their strength. Here we get the Firewing Centipede—a thousand-zhang-long monster with wings of fire and a centipede body. In Chinese folklore, centipedes are associated with poisonous, aggressive creatures, and giant demonic centipedes appear in classics like *Journey to the West*. The “thousand-zhang” scale is a deliberate exaggeration to convey overwhelming power. But even this fearsome formation is no match for Ji Ning’s sword comprehension—a reminder that in the Desolate Era hierarchy, raw numbers and formations only work against those who haven’t grasped a Grand Dao.

**The Concept of “Truesoul Destruction”:** When the text says “even gods and souls were annihilated” (神魂俱灭), it refers to the complete erasure of a cultivator’s truesoul, the deepest core of their existence. In Xianxia, killing the body is one thing; destroying the truesoul prevents reincarnation and is the ultimate death. Ji Ning’s sword-art at this level is so potent that it doesn’t just slay the physical forms of the demons but also annihilates their souls—meaning no reincarnation, no escape.

**Betrayal as a Tactic:** The Qi royalty’s decision to trap Ji Ning is a cold but pragmatic move—in the cutthroat world of Xianxia, using a powerful ally as a living shield is considered fair game if it buys time. However, they made the classic mistake of underestimating the ally’s actual power level. This is a recurring trope: a seemingly invincible army gets vaporized by a hidden powerhouse, teaching the schemers that their calculations were based on faulty intel.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is pure catharsis for anyone who loves seeing a protagonist flex. The tension builds when the massive formation appears, then the betrayal adds extra spice—and Ji Ning’s response is one of the most satisfying displays of raw power we’ve seen so far. Notice how the author keeps Ji Ning’s dialogue minimal: his only words are a warning, then a cold “Die,” and finally a sarcastic “Brilliant, indeed” before executing the schemer. That’s the hallmark of a true Xianxia powerhouse—they don’t monologue; they demonstrate.

Also pay attention to the shift in Ji Ning’s demeanor. Earlier, he was relaxed and even amused. After the betrayal, his anger is palpable but perfectly controlled. He doesn’t rant; he just acts. And the single strike that kills hundreds of demons, including three Loose Immortals, is a vivid demonstration of how far he has come since his Zifu days. If you’re keeping score at home, the power gap between a Wanxiang Adept and a top Primal Daoist with a deep Dao comprehension is exactly this wide.

## Deep Dive: The Dao of the Sword vs. Dao-Soldier Formations

One of the subtle lessons in this chapter is the relationship between individual Dao comprehension and military formations. The Firewing Centipedes are a manifestation of coordinated teamwork—each soldier contributes a sliver of power, and the whole becomes greater than the sum of its parts. But Ji Ning’s sword-art is the opposite: an expression of an individual’s complete mastery over a fundamental law of the universe. The formation is a mechanical contraption; the sword-art is a living truth. When a mechanical contraption meets a living truth, the contraption shatters. This is a recurring theme in *Desolate Era*: no amount of equipment or numbers can substitute for true enlightenment. Ji Ning’s ability to one-shot the formation with three simple sword-lights is the universe’s way of saying that the Dao trumps all.
