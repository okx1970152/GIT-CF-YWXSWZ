## Chapter Overview

Welcome back to Winged Serpent Lake, folks. After a long arc of training, trials, and tracking down the first of his parents’ killers, Ji Ning finally returns home—but not before he closes the book on a second enemy. This chapter is a masterclass in controlled catharsis, punctuated by a brutal interrogation and execution that shows just how far Ning’s Dao-heart has hardened. The second half settles into a quieter, more melancholic rhythm as he drifts on the lake where his parents’ ashes were scattered, letting the emotional weight of the journey truly sink in. If you came for non-stop action, the opener delivers; if you came for the feels, the boat scene might just hit you right in the chest.

## Key Plot Points

- Shui Yi, one of the three responsible for Yuchi Snow’s death and the murder of Ji Yichuan, attempts negotiating with his political backing (the Dong tribe)—but this isn’t a political game; it’s revenge. Ji Ning blitzes him with the Windwing Evasion and shatters his Violet Palace in one move.
- Ning doesn’t give Shui Yi a quick death. Instead, he uses a forbidden Heart-Scorching Art—a ritualistic punishment that lets the enemy feel every shred of the pain before burning him from the inside out. He also captures Shui Yi’s soul and destroys it, preventing reincarnation or ghost-cultivation.
- After the execution, Ning releases Shui Yi’s captured tribesmen and collects the fallen treasures. Two of the three enemies are now dead; only Dong Qi remains, and Ning knows he isn’t strong enough to breach Snowdragon Mountain yet.
- Ji Ning and Mu Northson part ways at the Crimson Dragon Mountains; Northson returns to the Black-White College to study golems and spend time with his master, whose lifespan is running out.
- Ning returns to the Yan Mountains in winter, drifts alone on Winged Serpent Lake, and reunites with Autumn Leaf on Myriad Splendor Island—the long-awaited homecoming.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

### The Heart-Scorching Art and Poetic Justice
The Heart-Scorching Art (焚心术) is a torture technique that inflicts excruciating internal agony without killing the victim immediately. In Xianxia morality, using forbidden arts on an enemy isn’t just cruelty—it’s a form of *poetic justice*. Ji Ning forces Shui Yi to *understand* his sin before erasing him, soul and all. The complete soul-annihilation (preventing reincarnation) is a core Xianxia trope: enemies in this genre don't just have their bodies killed; their spiritual continuity must be severed to ensure they can’t come back as a ghost cultivator. Ning’s methodical approach—kidnapping, making the enemy confess, then executing them with ritualistic precision—shows the maturation of his Dao-heart from reactive fury to cold, strategic vengeance.

### Leaving the Hometown / Root (根基)
The concept of *genji* (根基), or one’s root, is a recurring Xianxia theme. A cultivator's homeland, clan, and early emotional bonds are part of their spiritual foundation. Leaving it behind is a form of tribulation, and returning to it after growth and bloodshed is a way to re-center the Dao-heart. Ning’s long absence from the Black-White College and his quiet return to Winged Serpent Lake carries this weight—he is reconnecting with the 'root' that sustained him, even as he becomes more powerful and distant.

### Retired Legionary (退役修仙者)

A cultivator who has survived their service in an elite legion and is allowed to live in peace. Their murder is considered a grave offense against the legion's collective honor.

### One-Breath Bag (一气袋)

A Human-ranked treasure for capturing helpless prisoners via suction; cannot be used offensively and suffocates mortals but not cultivators.

## Reading Guide

This chapter works best if you read the first half as a cathartic purge and the second half as a meditative breather. Pay close attention to Ning’s dialogue with Shui Yi: he doesn’t just kill the man; he makes him *know* why he’s dying. That’s a major character beat that separates Ning from a simple vengeance-berserker. Also, note the contrast with the earlier massacre in the tribe—here, Ning releases the innocent Shui tribesmen, showing his prideful, principled streak. The final scene with Autumn Leaf is deliberately understated; no grand declarations, just a quiet reunion. That restraint is the mark of a cultivator whose Dao-heart is finally settling after a storm.

### Deep Dive: The Soul-Eradication Protocol
One detail that might slip by Western readers: Ning doesn’t just kill Shui Yi’s body. He captures his soul with an incense-burner treasure and erases it. In the Xianxia cosmology, a powerful cultivator’s soul can survive death and either be reincarnated through the Six Paths or, if strong enough, continue cultivating as a ghost. By destroying the soul, Ning ensures that *none* of that can happen—no underworld escape, no future revenge-birth. This is standard practice for serious grudges in the genre; leaving an enemy’s soul intact is considered a naive mistake. The incense burner he uses is a specialized soul-capturing artifact, a common but vital tool in the immortal cultivator’s arsenal.
