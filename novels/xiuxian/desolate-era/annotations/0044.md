## Chapter Overview

This chapter is a quiet, devastating gut-punch disguised as a conversation in a humble stone house. After the brutal Winged Serpent arc, Ji Ning rides to the Blacktooth Tribe expecting to see Chuncao—his loyal maidservant who chose family reunion over staying by his side. What he finds instead is a tribe haunted by recent tragedy, a father drowning in guilt, and a tear-stained letter that serves as both a love confession and a farewell. The chapter masterfully shifts from slow-burn suspicion to raw, cathartic grief, showcasing IET’s ability to wring genuine emotion from a cultivation world that usually runs on blood and violence. The final image of Blacktooth collapsing and screaming his daughter’s name—`Miwa!`—hits like a freight train.

## Key Plot Points

- Ji Ning arrives at the Blacktooth Tribe and immediately senses something is wrong. The tribesmen he questions outside all give eerily consistent answers about Chuncao being dead. His earlier suspicion hardens into cold certainty.
- Blacktooth tries to maintain a fabricated story: Chuncao fell in love with a traveling merchant, married him, and left. He even produces a letter he claims she wrote before departing.
- Ji Ning reads the letter and recognizes Chuncao’s handwriting. The letter is filled with pure joy about her return to her father and her growing feelings for “him”—clearly Ji Ning himself, though she never names him. Tear stains mark the confession.
- The key emotional dagger: Chuncao writes that just watching Ji Ning train made her happy, that she would have married him if only she had spoken. This revelation crushes Ji Ning, who realizes he would have said yes.
- Ji Ning sees through the lie. The letter is not a farewell note from a happy bride—it is a final testament, written in the shadow of death.
- When pressed, Blacktooth’s composure shatters. He drops to his knees, lets out a primal, years-suppressed wail of grief, and finally screams his daughter’s name: “Miwa!”—confirming her death.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

- **The Weight of a Letter in Xianxia**: In a genre dominated by duels, treasures, and tribulations, a handwritten letter on animal hide carries immense narrative heft. Here, it serves as both a death flag and a love confession, allowing Chuncao’s voice to echo beyond her grave. The tear stains are a classic but effective detail—they transform abstract text into physical evidence of heartbreak.
- **“Marrying a Traveling Merchant” as a Cover Story**: Blacktooth’s fabricated tale is not random; traveling merchants (走商) in this setting are solitary figures who move between tribes, making them an ideal “disappearance” excuse. The lie is plausible enough to buy time, but Ji Ning’s sharp reasoning—why would Chuncao leave again so soon after reuniting with her father?—exposes the weakness.
- **The Servant-Master Bond vs. Blood Ties**: Chuncao’s choice to leave Ji Ning for her biological father was a rare moment of personal agency in a world where servants are often treated as property. Ji Ning respected that choice, but the irony is brutal: the father she chose led her to death, while the young master who let her go now grieves her like family. This tension between chosen family and blood lineage is a recurring Xianxia theme.
- **Blacktooth’s Collapse**: The detail of Blacktooth’s knees slamming onto stone and his hoarse, long-suppressed scream is distinctly Xianxia in its physicality. Grief here is not whispered—it is a body-slamming, vocal-cord-tearing explosion that fills the stone room.

## Reading Guide

- **Prepare for an emotional whiplash:** After several chapters of demon-slaying action and breakthrough training, this chapter pulls the brakes hard. The pacing is deliberately slow, letting Ji Ning’s suspicion simmer, then detonating in the final lines. If you came for sword fights, hold on—the real battle here is against grief and guilt.
- **Pay attention to Blacktooth’s earlier behavior:** When he first runs out to greet Ji Ning, his body trembles. That is not just respect for a noble—that is a man bracing for the inevitable. His dialogue is overly polished, too fluent, like a rehearsed script. IET is signaling the lie long before Ji Ning catches it.
- **Chuncao’s letter is worth a reread:** The moment you know it is a deathbed confession, every line hits differently. “Just watching him was a gift from the heavens” transforms from sweet to devastating. This is textbook tragic irony—the reader knows more than the writer (Chuncao) does about her own fate.
- **The final line “Miwa!!!” is the chapter’s payload:** IET trusts the reader to connect the dots. Chuncao’s birth name is finally spoken aloud, not as a tender nickname, but as a raw, animal howl of loss. If you felt a chill reading it, great—the author did his job.
