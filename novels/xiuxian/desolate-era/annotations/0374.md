## Chapter Overview
This chapter opens with a jaw-dropping flex of raw Dao comprehension: Ji Ning, without lifting a finger, freezes a full Primal-led Firewing Guard squad in place using nothing but his *Dao Domain*—and then casually picks them off like flies. The moment is a powerful callback to his encounter with the ancient Fiendgod in Mount Yujue, driving home just how far he has climbed. But the real meat of the chapter? A desperate, high-stakes negotiation with the last prince of the fallen Qi Kingdom. Qi Rufeng is cornered, hunted for decades, and he’s not bargaining for treasures—he’s gambling his family’s survival. The price for taking Qi Xiaoyu as a disciple just skyrocketed: one hundred years of protection. Get ready, fellow Daoists, for a chapter that balances overwhelming power display with raw emotional desperation.

## Key Plot Points
1. **Instant Suppression via Dao Domain**: The twelve Firewing Guards (one Primal, eleven Wanxiang) assume they can crush the “leather-clad youth.” Instead, Ji Ning activates his Dao Domain of the Sword, trapping them in place with pure Heavenly power. This mirrors the ancient Fiendgod’s tactic from the Mount Yujue trial, showcasing Ji Ning’s growth.
2. **Callous Efficiency**: Ji Ning executes the entire squad without hesitation, noting their unsolicited attack as justification. He then reflects on his own progress, explicitly comparing his current power to the Fiendgod that once overwhelmed him.
3. **Identity Exposed**: After the battle, the Qi Kingdom survivors can no longer hide. Prince Qi Rufeng admits their true identities, describing decades of relentless pursuit that have reduced their clan to just three members.
4. **The Prince’s Gambit**: Instead of accepting treasures or flying swords, Qi Rufeng lays down a brutal condition: a hundred years of protection from Ji Ning in exchange for letting Qi Xiaoyu become his disciple. He is emotionally spent, exhausted by the weight of leadership, and willing to die if refused.
5. **Ji Ning’s Dilemma**: Ji Ning is taken aback. He never expected the prince to stake his entire family’s future on this. With his own timeline (a promise to return to Yu Wei and the Grand Xia world), a century-long commitment is impossible—but the prince refuses to yield.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Dao Domain (道域)**: This is not a flashy technique but a high-level application of Dao comprehension. By attuning one’s soul to the power of Heaven and Earth, a cultivator projects a field that suppresses enemies. The *stronger* your understanding, the more Heavenly power you can command. The progression goes: *Dao Domain* → *Dao Realm* (mastering a complete Dao) → *Grand Dao Domain* → *Grand Dao Realm*. Ji Ning is currently operating at a level that surpasses many Earth Immortals, which is why a mere Primal-led squad is helpless. This mirrors the classic Xianxia concept of “absolute realm suppression.”

**Qi Kingdom’s Collapse & Royal Remnants**: A recurring Xianxia trope: the fallen kingdom whose surviving royals carry the burden of vengeance and legacy. The prince’s desperation—being the last male heir, watching siblings die one by one—echoes the tragic fate of noble houses caught in demonic or sect warfare. His request for “a hundred years of protection” is a standard Xianxia contract: instead of a one-time reward, the cultivator pledges temporal service for a fixed period, often used when the clan has no other card to play.

**Karmic Merit (功德)**: Qi Xiaoyu is described as possessing “karmic merit three hundred zhang wide.” In Daoist cosmology, karmic merit is a measurable aura accumulated through good deeds, protection of life, or destined roles. A being with such dense golden light is considered a “blessing to the clan’s fortune.” Powers like the Netherworld Kingdom take note of such individuals, often granting them smoother cultivation paths or protection from calamities. It’s a tangible, almost physical force in this universe—not just a metaphor.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a masterclass in Xianxia pacing: the *incredible power display* serves to establish Ji Ning’s weight in the Star Continent, while the *failed negotiation* subverts the usual “protagonist gets what he wants easily” trope. Pay attention to the emotional weight of Qi Rufeng’s speech—this isn’t a villain bargaining; it’s a broken survivor making a final, desperate stand. The chapter also reinforces Ji Ning’s pragmatic Dao-heart: he doesn’t hesitate to slaughter those who attacked him, but he hesitates when faced with a moral commitment that conflicts with his own timeline. That internal tension—power versus promise—is what makes this setup so compelling for future chapters.
