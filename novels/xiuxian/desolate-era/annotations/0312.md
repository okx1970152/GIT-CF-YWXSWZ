## Chapter Overview

The dust hasn't even settled on the assassination attempt before the real battle begins—the battle for reputation. Ji Ning steps out of the shattered formation as the killer of a hundred-thousand-year-old Loose Immortal, and the whole Grand Xia Imperial Capital takes notice. This chapter is pure payoff: the tension of the prior fight dissolves into a cascade of recognition, praise, and wary respect from the Xianxia world’s movers and shakers. And of course, a loudmouth who talked too much is left eating humble pie.

## Key Plot Points

The Zhuanshui clan's Loose Immortals, led by Zhuanshui Sanfan, immediately recognize Ji Ning and piece together that the assassin was the Shaoyan clan's work. Their praise is genuine but also politically charged—they're delighted to see their ancient enemies take a loss. Zhuanshui Sanfan himself publicly credits Ji Ning for the kill, noting that his Primaltwin was a full realm lower than the assassin and still won decisively.

Xiamang Hong, a Vice-Commander of the Imperial Guard and a royal family member, arrives with his silver-armored troops. After learning the details, he shows Ji Ning genuine respect, invites him to a private banquet, and in true noble-rascal fashion, exposes the loudmouth who earlier mocked Ji Ning—Xiao Lang—forcing him to retreat in disgrace.

Xiao Lang, after earlier boasting and looking down on Ji Ning, is publicly humiliated by Fairy Boyan's pointed remarks and Xiamang Hong's blunt mockery. He leaves without a word, his face a mask of fury and shame.

Zhuanshui Xiaolou, feeling responsible for the security breach, gifts Ji Ning a Lotus Dharma-Vase—a treasure containing lotus mysteries that could deepen Ji Ning's own sword-world technique. Ji Ning accepts and departs, with the chapter closing on the quiet observation from Xiaolou and Quge that Ji Ning has just become a name on everyone's lips.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Cousin-brother (表弟/表姐)**: In this chapter, the bond between Ji Ning and Yuchi Xiyue as maternal cousins is solidified. In Chinese family hierarchy, a 'cousin-brother' or 'cousin-sister' (表亲) specifically denotes a relationship through one's mother's side, distinct from paternal cousins (堂亲). This distinction isn't just trivia—in clan-based Xianxia societies, maternal kinship often carries different political weight and emotional nuances, as it crosses clan boundaries rather than staying within the paternal line.

**Primaltwin (第二元神)**: A second, independent soul body created by splitting one's consciousness and cultivating a separate physical form. In Daoist alchemy lore, the 'divine soul' can be dual-natured; here, it translates into a tactical cheat-code. A Primaltwin can train separately, survive independently, and fight simultaneously with the main body—effectively doubling a cultivator's experience and combat power. Ji Ning's having one at the Wanxiang stage is considered freakishly early, and its performance here (killing a top-tier Loose Immortal while being a realm lower) is the talk of the capital for good reason.

**Political schadenfreude in Xianxia dynasties**: The Zhuanshui clan's delight at Ji Ning's victory isn't just friendly appreciation—it's a billion-year-old grudge in action. In such a setting, an enemy's enemy is an automatic ally, and public acknowledgment of one's prowess can be as strategic as any treasure. The social dynamics here mirror real-world Chinese political factions in miniature.

## Reading Guide

This is a classic Xianxia "fame explosion" chapter—watch how Ji Ning's stock skyrockets the moment he survives and kills. The real treat here isn't the action (we had that last chapter) but the fallout. Every faction recalibrates their view of Ji Ning: the Zhuanshui clan sees a weapon against their enemies; Xiamang Hong sees a rising star worth befriending; and poor Xiao Lang learns the hard way that trash-talking someone before they've shown their hand is a one-way ticket to humiliation city.

Special shoutout to Fairy Boyan, who serves the shadiest, most elegant dish of revenge by casually exposing Xiao Lang's earlier boasts. Xianxia women don't forget.

If you're new to the genre's social politics, note how quickly the tone shifts between the assembled immortals once Ji Ning proves himself. That's the Xianxia way: respect is earned in blood and fire, not given by birthright—unless you're a royal brat like Xiamang Hong, in which case you can afford to be charmingly clueless.
