## Chapter Overview
This chapter is the calm before the storm. Liu Jing’s impromptu alliance finally takes shape: reinforcements arrive in the form of Chen Qiaoqian and her fellow disciples from the Yellow Maple Valley, the battle plan is set, and every role is assigned. But beneath the surface harmony, cracks are already showing—Wu Xuan’s desertion, the looming unknown of the Black Fiend Sect Leader’s power, and Han Li’s own cold, private calculus about what he’s truly after. By the end of the chapter, the group is united in purpose but deeply unequal in commitment. Han Li stays not out of loyalty, but because of a single secret that might inch him closer to Core Formation.

## Key Plot Points
- Wu Xuan explicitly withdraws from the operation, citing his intention to report everything to Master Li Huayuan, and leaves the Qin Estate alone. His departure reinforces the self-preservation principle; no one is surprised.
- Liu Jing formally takes command of the remaining group. Zhong Weiniang is sent to Nanwu City to request aid from Chen Qiaoqian, leveraging an old friendship.
- The reinforcement party arrives: Chen Qiaoqian plus three other men and one woman, all Foundation Establishment cultivators. Han Li’s unease is palpable when he sees Chen Qiaoqian, though nothing is said between them.
- The visiting cultivators recognize Han Li’s name immediately from his reputation at the border confrontation. Song Meng’s glee at finally having kept a secret is evident. Liu Jing and Zhong Weiniang are visibly shocked by the scale of Han Li’s past kills—this recalibrates Han Li’s status within the group.
- A welcome feast is held in the mortal style. After the meal, Liu Jing lays out the plan: strike the Imperial Palace the following night, relying on surprise and overwhelming force.
- The chapter concludes with an extended internal monologue from Han Li. He has no illusions about the danger—he fully acknowledges that some of them may not survive. His motivation is purely self-interested: a secret he learned from the Young Prince that might improve his Core Formation odds. He’s also prepared a failsafe trump card.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The ‘Blood Curse’ as an Institutional Leash**: The Black Fiend Sect’s control structure—where disciples are bound by Blood Curses rather than loyalty—reflects a recurring xianxia trope: the use of bodily poison or soul-binding restrictions as a form of indenture. This mechanic externalizes what in other genres would be an internal moral conflict. In the Mortal Stream, such leashes are a logical extension of the Dark Forest: if you cannot trust, you must control, and the most efficient control is physical. Note that the term itself does not appear in this chapter’s dialogue, but the architecture of the sect (Four Blood Attendants, altar lords, a secluded leader) implies exactly this kind of institutional fear economy.

**Reputation as a Double-Edged Asset in the Cultivation World**: When the visiting cultivators recognize Han Li’s name and praise his “great deed” of killing over a dozen Foundation Establishment cultivators, this is a pivotal development in Han Li’s social standing, but the narrative treats it with cold caution rather than satisfaction. In the cultivation world, reputation is not just fame—it’s *visibility*. A cultivator known for being dangerous will have his every move watched, his every negotiation weighted with suspicion, and his alliances structured around the fear of his power rather than trust. Liu Jing’s immediate thought upon hearing Han Li’s record is to mentally recalculate the team’s odds of victory, not to congratulate him. Han Li himself feels a chill down his spine, because he knows that being perceived as the strongest asset in the room also makes him the most likely to be thrown into the hardest fight.

**The “Disposal” of Wu Xuan as a Strategic Purity Cost**: In xianxia team-building narratives, a character who withdraws from a dangerous operation is often morally condemned. Here, Wu Xuan is indeed condemned—by Zhong Weiniang and indirectly by Song Meng—but the narrative itself does not fully endorse that condemnation. Han Li’s internal monologue explicitly reframes Wu Xuan’s escape as “wise” and “self-preservative.” This is a classic Mortal Stream subversion of the expected heroic narrative. The text does not punish Wu Xuan for cowardice; it simply cuts him off from the group’s resources and information, which is a secular consequence, not a moral one. The cost of principle in this world is not guilt but isolation.

## Reading Guide
This is a transitional chapter, but don’t let the lack of action fool you—it’s doing crucial work beneath the surface. The banquet scene and the mission planning are not there for atmosphere; they are a careful audit of assets, vulnerabilities, and hidden agendas. Pay close attention to how Han Li’s internal narration contradicts his external behavior. He smiles, he nods, he eats the feast—but in his head, he’s already counting how many of these people might die and whether his trump card is enough to guarantee his own escape. This is the Han Li we know: cooperative only when the odds skew just enough in his favor.

The appearance of Chen Qiaoqian is a quiet but loaded addition. The author doesn’t waste words rehashing their history—there is no dramatic exchange, no emotional confrontation. The tension is communicated purely through Han Li’s private unease and through the reader’s memory of what happened between them in the Yellow Maple Valley caves. The Mortal Stream respects its readers’ memory; exposition is treated as a resource to be conserved.

Finally, note the granular realism of the logistics: Zhong Weiniang is sent ahead for three days, the visiting cultivators are given one full day to rest, and the strike is scheduled for the quietest hour of the night. These small details are not filler. They reflect Liu Jing’s competence as a planner. The Mortal Stream rewards careful preparation over bold improvisation, and every hour of delay is a roll of the dice against the unknown timing of the Black Fiend Sect Leader’s seclusion.
