## Chapter Overview

Ever felt the crushing disappointment of unlocking a new skill, only to realize it’s basically a glorified pair of x-ray glasses? That’s Han Li right now with the Celestial Eye Technique. After the raw, combat-ready power of the Fireball Technique, he’s hoping for something equally impressive—but instead, he gets the cultivation world’s most passive perception check. It can see spiritual power, but can it kill a poisonous snake? No. Can it melt a lock? No. All it can do is make him glow faintly white, which is useless unless you’re a moth with a death wish. Meanwhile, the rest of his spell practice is hitting a wall harder than a Spirit-Fixing Talisman hits a cultivator’s body. But just as frustration sets in, an urgent bell tolls from outside the valley: a high-level disciple’s master has been poisoned, and all the other doctors have given up. Han Li is now the sect’s last hope. This isn’t just any rescue mission—it’s a test of his rapidly expanding skill set, and a poignant mirror to his own relationship with the perished Doctor Mo.

## Key Plot Points

Han Li’s exploration of the five basic spells reaches a new baseline. The Fireball Technique is confirmed as the only one with immediate combat utility, blazing through steel and water with satisfying destructiveness. The Celestial Eye Technique, in stark contrast, is a pure auxiliary spell: it reveals spiritual power as a faint white aura concentrated near the dantian but offers no offensive or defensive function. Boredom sets in quickly, as Han Li realizes he’s the only cultivator in the entire Seven Mysteries Sect—who else would he spy on with it?

His frustration with the other yet-unmastered spells (Wind Riding, Telekinesis, Spirit-Fixing) mounts. The chapter shows him sighing more often than he used to, a dry admission of how much the learning curve has steepened since his martial arts days. This isn’t a chosen one breezing through magic; this is a pragmatic farmer forcing his way through unfamiliar territory.

The plot pivots when urgent bell tolls pull him from his studies. Ma Rong, a favored disciple of the Fifth Elder Li, barges in with a desperate plea: Elder Li was poisoned in a fight with a Wild Wolf Gang expert, and no other physician dared to treat him. Han Li’s internal risk assessment immediately kicks in. He knows his own limit—his Clear Spirit Powder isn’t guaranteed to work against rare jungle toxins. But he also knows this is a political necessity; refusing the Fifth Elder’s disciple in plain sight would be a strategic blunder. He accepts, mentally preparing for a likely failure while calculating that even a failed attempt won’t seriously damage his reputation, since no one else has dared to try.

On the way to Elder Li’s residence, Han Li observes Ma Rong’s genuine desperation and compares it to his own bitterly adversarial relationship with Doctor Mo. This silent reflection is the chapter’s emotional core, linking this new poisoning incident back to the unresolved trauma of the valley’s secret history. The chapter ends as they arrive at the elder’s door, with Han Li carrying the burden of both external expectation and internal, unresolved grief.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Celestial Eye Technique (天眼术)**: This low-tier spell is the cultivation world’s spiritual CSI kit. It literally opens the practitioner’s ‘Celestial Eye’ to perceive qi fields, cultivation base depth, and elemental imbalances in living beings. In orthodox xianxia, it’s an essential survival tool for reconnaissance—assessing an opponent’s true strength before a fight. However, in Han Li’s current, isolated context, it’s a dead-end skill: he has no one to spy on but himself. This practical limitation drives home the Mortal Stream’s core theme: even a powerful tool is worthless if it can’t be applied to immediate survival.

**Clear Spirit Powder (清灵散)**: A standard ‘cure-all’ antidote in Jianghu lore, often used as a narrative shorthand for ‘competent but not legendary.’ It can neutralize most common poisons, but against rare, customized toxins from high-level assassins or cultivators—like the one afflicting Elder Li—it’s often completely useless. In the Mortal Stream’s cynical logic, universal antidotes are a dead giveaway that the seller is either naive or lying. Han Li’s doubt about his own Clear Spirit Powder is not just professional realism; it’s a survival instinct telling him that in this world, generic solutions rarely solve unique problems.

**The Bell as an Urgent Signal**: The bell tolling from the valley entrance is a classic Jianghu trope. It is a publicly recognized system for summoning medical aid, with specific patterns for severity. A single, heavy toll signals an urgent but non-life-threatening case; the series of tolls in this chapter indicates a crisis. This system reinforces that the sect, despite its iron hierarchy, has functional emergency protocols—and that Han Li, as the valley’s resident ‘physician,’ is expected to respond without hesitation, regardless of his personal affairs.

## Reading Guide

This is a classic Mortal Stream ‘breather and setup’ chapter. It balances Han Li’s magical progress with a new social obligation that will test his medical skills and force him to interact with the broader sect hierarchy. Pay close attention to Han Li’s exhaustion—not just physical, but intellectual. His sigh count is rising, and his frustration with spell-casting is building. This is a deliberate pacing move: the author is reminding us that Han Li’s power curve is not a straight line, and that even a methodical calculator can hit a bottleneck of diminishing returns.

The emotional mirror between Ma Rong’s desperate loyalty to Elder Li and Han Li’s fatalistic reflection on Doctor Mo is the chapter’s subtle punch. It’s not melodramatic—Han Li doesn’t cry or brood for long—but it plants a seed of unresolved grief that will shape his actions when he eventually faces the poisoned elder. The chapter’s real value is in its reminder that Han Li is still carrying the weight of his first major kill, and that the wounds of that betrayal are not yet fully scarred.

For new readers, this is a great place to appreciate Han Li’s characterization. He’s not a man who feels pity easily—but when someone else’s loyalty reminds him of what he never had, the mask flickers. That’s the Mortal Stream at its finest: not grand battles, but small, quiet moments where the weight of a brutal world sinks in.
