## Chapter Overview

Hold onto your fellow daoist hats, because this chapter is a masterclass in psychological horror on a stick. Han Li stumbles into what looks like a cheesy Jianghu love triangle, only to realize that Dong Xuan’er—the notorious, perpetually sulking enchantress—has been completely mind-jacked by a devastatingly beautiful male cultivator. And we mean *completely*: she’s staring at him like a lovesick puppy, utterly unaware of the world. When Han Li breaks the spell with a single sound transmission, the real dragon rears its head: the mystery man (who’s packing mid Foundation Establishment energy) reveals he’s a cold, calculating predator who doesn’t take kindly to having his prey stolen. It’s the most deliciously tense mix of flirting and death threats we’ve had in a while.

## Key Plot Points

- Han Li observes Dong Xuan’er acting strangely and identifies the presence of a very powerful soul-bewitching art at work.
- The beautiful man, calling himself “Tian,” dismisses Han Li as an unremarkable early Foundation Establishment outsider, but Han Li is not so easily cowed.
- Yan Yu and Senior Brother Feng finally catch on to the bewitchment and prepare to attack, but “Tian” is unfazed.
- Han Li sends a sharp sound transmission directly into Dong Xuan’er’s mind, breaking the spell and shocking her back to her senses.
- Dong Xuan’er panics, flees to hide behind Han Li, and reveals her terror of having been completely mentally enslaved.
- “Tian” warns the three men, memorizes their faces, and leaves with clear threat, but chooses not to fight—suggesting his bewitchment art cannot be used on men.
- At the inn, Dong Xuan’er recounts her horrifying experience of being utterly dominated against her will, while the two senior brothers offer protection and flirtation.
- Han Li deduces that the bewitchment art only works on women and warns that Dong Xuan’er’s own charm technique is similarly dangerous.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Soul Bewitchment vs. Natural Charm**: This chapter draws a fascinating line in the cultivation world’s psychological arsenal. The beautiful man’s technique is an external, invasive soul-control method—essentially a magical roofie that hijacks the victim’s will and turns them into a devoted thrall. It’s a form of spiritual domination that Chinese xianxia often associates with demonic or heterodox paths: violating a person’s autonomy is considered a fate worse than death. By contrast, Dong Xuan’er’s “bewitching charm” is a cultivation-enhanced *natural* allure, something closer to a superpowered version of real-world human magnetism. She doesn’t cast a spell; she *is* one. The narration deliberately parallels the two, suggesting that while one is obviously evil, the other is no less dangerous if you’re not careful. It’s a classic “is it better to be drugged or seduced?” dilemma, and the novel lands firmly on “both are bad for your health.”

**The Sound Transmission as a Pierce-Antidote**: Han Li’s use of a sound transmission to break the spell is a clever nod to cultivator tech. In Chinese cultivation fiction, sound transmission is a private, mana-based whisper that can bypass physical barriers. Han Li weaponizes it here to deliver a sharp, targeted shock—like an electrical jolt to the soul—that snaps Dong Xuan’er out of her trance. It’s not a brute-force dispelling technique; it’s a tactical use of information (mentioning her master’s name) delivered through an invisible channel. This reinforces Han Li’s core combat doctrine: you don’t need a massive fireball when a precisely aimed psychological needle can do the job.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a quiet but important character beat for Dong Xuan’er. Up until now, she’s been the bratty, flirtatious princess who uses her charm as a weapon and a shield. Here, she gets a hard dose of vulnerability: the realisation that someone else’s magic can do the same thing to *her*—but worse, and without her consent. It’s a moment that cracks her arrogant shell and makes her, for the first time, genuinely sympathetic. Watch how Han Li reacts: he doesn’t rush to comfort her. He observes, he calculates, and he draws a cold, logical conclusion about the limits of the bewitchment art. His emotional distance is a deliberate contrast to the other two men’s instant white-knighting. And that final note—about Dong Xuan’er’s charm being equally dangerous—is Han Li’s way of saying: “Don’t forget who you’re talking to, lady. I see you.” Classic.
