## Chapter Overview

Buckle up, fellow Daoists—this is the chapter where the mask finally shatters and the real horror show begins. Han Li, now completely immobilized by the Spirit-Fixing Talisman, is carried like cargo to a freshly built stone room deep in the sect’s back hills. It’s not a torture chamber in the conventional sense; it’s a ritual space, complete with an elaborate powder-drawn pattern and priceless jade embedded in the crude stone floor. And then—just when you think it can’t get any worse—a *second voice* emerges from Doctor Mo’s body, belonging to a young man who claims to be nothing but a remnant soul, a dead man walking (or rather, floating). Talk about a plot twist that completely recontextualizes everything you thought you knew about the kindly old physician.

## Key Plot Points

- Doctor Mo carries Han Li to a newly built stone chamber, clearly prepared for a significant ritual; the Iron Servant is ordered to kill any intruder on sight, establishing the stakes.
- The room is sealed, windowless, but brightly lit with lamps and candles; a large circular pattern is painted in the center with what appears to be medicinal or alchemical powder, studded with rare green jade.
- Han Li is placed at the center of the pattern, unable to move or speak, his consciousness trapped inside his own body by the Spirit-Fixing Talisman.
- Doctor Mo extinguishes some lamps, darkening the room; then he suddenly speaks to an invisible presence, asking for reassurance about a high-stakes plan.
- A young man’s voice emerges from nowhere, claiming absolute certainty about the techniques he taught Doctor Mo—namely the Seven Ghost Soul Devouring Art and the Spirit-Fixing Talisman.
- Doctor Mo accuses the voice of being a dead man he himself killed, questioning whether so-called oaths sworn on family names can truly be trusted; the confession lays bare the brutal history between them.
- The young man, indignant, swears he has already pledged oaths in the names of his ancestors, parents, family, and even his entire clan—a display of moral desperation that Han Li immediately recognizes as the mark of a cold-hearted character.
- Doctor Mo softens his tone, acknowledging the soul’s predicament: no physical shell, no sunlight, barely better than complete annihilation.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Three Iron Laws of Possession (夺舍三大铁则):** This chapter strongly alludes to the possession framework without explicitly naming it. Possession in *RMJI* follows strict cosmic constraints: (1) You cannot possess a mortal; the body rejects the soul and collapses. (2) Only a higher-level cultivator can possess a lower one, and the greater the gap, the safer the process. (3) A cultivator gets exactly one possession attempt in a lifetime; a second destroys the primordial soul entirely. Doctor Mo’s entire plan appears to hinge on leveraging Han Li’s body as a vessel—but the presence of a *second* soul (the young man, Yu Zitong) introduces a terrifying complication: what happens when two parasites compete for the same host?

**Primordial Soul (元神):** The young man describes himself as a primordial soul, a condensed spiritual essence that can survive independently after the body is destroyed. In xianxia cosmology, this is not a ghost in the Western sense; it is a concentrated bundle of consciousness and cultivated essence that exists in a semi-material state, vulnerable to sunlight and requiring a physical anchor to persist. The terror here is that a primordial soul can be interrogated, tortured, and even consumed—making it the ultimate hostage.

**Spirit-Fixing Talisman (定神符):** This is a yellow paper talisman inscribed with silver symbols that completely immobilizes the target’s body and spirit. The victim remains fully conscious and aware but cannot move, speak, or resist. The horror of this state, as experienced by Han Li in this chapter, lies in passive observation: you can *see* and *hear* everything, but you cannot intervene in your own fate. The talisman’s mana is finite; when exhausted, it turns to ash and releases the target.

**Poisonous Oath (毒誓):** The young man swears oaths on his ancestors, parents, family, and entire clan—a gesture meant to demonstrate sincerity. In Chinese folk and martial fiction, a poisonous oath is considered binding because it invites supernatural retribution not just on the speaker but on their bloodline. That the young man is willing to gamble *other people’s lives* for his own survival tells you everything you need to know about his moral character.

### Possession (夺舍)

The forced takeover of another person’s body by a stronger primordial soul, governed by three cosmic laws: no mortal possession, power hierarchy required, one attempt per lifetime.

## Reading Guide

**On the horror of helplessness.** Han Li is not tortured in this chapter; he is simply placed at the center of a room and left to *watch*. The Spirit-Fixing Talisman preserves his awareness, forcing him to witness the entire ritual preparation without being able to scream, struggle, or even close his eyes. This is a very particular kind of psychological pressure: the victim is a fully conscious passenger in their own body, and the narrative makes you sit with that dread.

**On the second soul as a narrative joker.** The sudden introduction of a young man’s voice from inside Doctor Mo completely rewrites the power dynamics. Doctor Mo is no longer the sole antagonist; he is himself a vessel containing a parasitic consciousness, and the two are locked in a tense mutual hostage situation. This adds a layer of uncertainty: if possession is a one-shot mechanic, and there are already *two* souls vying for control, what happens when a third (Han Li) is added to the equation?

**On the ritual as investment.** The elaborate stone room, the expensive green jade embedded in the floor pattern, the careful preparation of lamps—none of this is improvised. Doctor Mo has been preparing this ritual space for months, likely since before Han Li ever reached the fourth layer. The chapter underscores a core Mortal Stream principle: when a powerful cultivator devotes significant resources to a plan, they are not doing it for fun; they are committing capital, and failure is not an option they can afford.
