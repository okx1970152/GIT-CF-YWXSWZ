## Chapter Overview

Ever had one of those awkward standoffs where you’ve just admitted to killing a guy, and his old pal shows up wearing his boots—no, wait, *you’re* wearing the boots? Welcome to Han Li’s afternoon. This chapter is a masterclass in the Mortal Stream’s favorite pastime: not fighting. Han Li runs into Zhong Wu, an ugly, pouch-covered cultivator from Spirit Beast Mountain, who recognizes the Cloud Treading Boots on Han Li’s feet. What follows is not a duel, but a beautifully tense dance of words, wariness, and mutual profit-seeking. By the end, they’re trading information like old business partners, all while keeping a twenty-zhang buffer zone that would make a sniper proud.

## Key Plot Points

- A green-clad figure, Zhong Wu, ambushes Han Li in the central zone, immediately fixating on the Cloud Treading Boots. His shock at seeing them on a Yellow Maple Valley disciple is palpable.
- Han Li coolly admits to killing Feng Yue, the boots’ previous owner. He calculates the odds of a surprise attack with his hidden thread (now only 10%) and decides to try diplomacy first.
- Zhong Wu, a seasoned rogue, shamelessly declares that dead men have no friendship, instantly dropping any pretense of avenging his acquaintance. His caution, however, deepens as he takes several steps back.
- The two engage in a tense negotiation, culminating in an agreement to exchange jade slips containing their respective information on the central zone.
- After the swap, they chat about trivial matters as if best friends, but maintain a comically wide gap—when Han Li steps forward, Zhong Wu takes two steps back. The chapter ends with this fragile, mutually beneficial standoff.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Unwritten Rules of “Talking It Out”**: You will not find a chapter in *RMJI* where two cultivators meet, trade pleasantries, and go their separate ways out of genuine goodwill. There is always a reason—mutual fear, mutual profit, or a tactical calculation that favors waiting. Zhong Wu backs off not because he likes Han Li, but because he cannot figure out how a “mediocre” kid killed a powerhouse like Feng Yue. The uncertainty is a better shield than any magic tool. This is the social face of the Dark Forest: everyone smiles while mentally counting the opponent’s weaknesses.

**Feng Yue’s Legacy**: The dead man still drives the plot. His “Cloud Treading Boots” (踏云靴) are a top-grade movement tool that would make any cultivator a priority target. By wearing them, Han Li is effectively carrying a sign that says “I beat a big boss.” It’s a risk—it provokes curiosity and greed—but it also buys him immediate respect and caution from observant foes like Zhong Wu.

**The Slow Retreat**: Watch how the chapter builds tension through physical distance. Han Li and Zhong Wu never close the gap; in fact, they expand it. In the Mortal Stream, space is power. A sudden close step is an imminent attack. A slow backpedal is a sign of calculating respect. This is a combat language that has nothing to do with magic.

### Mortal Stream Standoff (对峙)

A conflict where both parties are calculating the cost-benefit of violence and choosing a non-violent resolution instead. Usually involves a lot of fake smiles and slow, deliberate stepping.

## Reading Guide

If you came here expecting a flashy magical duel every chapter, this one will feel like a detour. But it’s not. It’s a vital piece of worldbuilding that shows how the cultivation world’s middle-class survivors operate. Han Li finally meets someone who isn’t a suicidal maniac or a helpless lamb—he meets a professional scavenger who knows when to fold.

Pay attention to Zhong Wu’s character design. He’s ugly, he’s coarse, and he carries pouches full of raw meat for his spirit beasts. But he’s not stupid. His instantaneous shift from bluster to negotiation is the hallmark of a survivor. He knows that fighting a man who might be packing Feng Yue’s entire arsenal is a losing bet, so he pivots to information trade. That’s the Mortal Stream way: when you can’t win a fight, win a deal.

Also, note Han Li’s self-awareness. He admits his success rate for a surprise kill is low—ten percent—and adjusts accordingly. That’s not weakness; that’s the core of his survival strategy. He gambles only when the odds favor him, and here, they don’t. So he talks, he trades, and he keeps the thread ready for later.

Finally, enjoy the dark comedy of two killers maintaining a twenty-zhang gap while chatting about local gossip. It’s absurd. It’s perfect.
