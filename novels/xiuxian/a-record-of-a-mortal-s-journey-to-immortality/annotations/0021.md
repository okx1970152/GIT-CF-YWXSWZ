## Chapter Overview
This chapter delivers a masterclass in one of the Mortal Stream’s most cherished principles: never lift a finger without benefit. Han Li has just saved a man’s life, and his reward is a blade to the throat. If you thought the cultivation world ran on gratitude and good vibes, think again. Here, even a life-saving act is treated as a potential liability—because anyone desperate enough to swallow a forbidden pill is also desperate enough to silence the witness. The chapter is tense, darkly humorous, and serves as the explicit birth of Han Li’s signature rule: no rescue without profit. It’s a turning point that cements his pragmatic survival ethos and poisons whatever honest impulses he might have had left.

## Key Plot Points
- **The blade to the throat:** As soon as the worst of his pain subsides, Li Feiyu’s first instinct is to press a blade against Han Li’s neck. This is the Mortal Stream in a nutshell: survival paranoia overrides gratitude every time.
- **Han Li’s response:** He stays calm, points out he just saved Li’s life, and cuts straight to negotiation. No begging, no pleading. He offers a poisonous oath—a binding vow from Chinese folk belief—to buy trust.
- **The oath and the reveal:** Han Li swears the oath, Li sheathes the blade, and the two finally talk. Han Li admits he knew this could happen but saved Li anyway because “I studied medicine.” That line is half-truth, half-performance—and it works.
- **Mutual benefit deal:** Han Li offers Li a painkiller prescription, which he originally developed for Zhang Tie. Li’s agonizing pill addiction is confirmed as his major vulnerability, and Han Li immediately weaponizes that knowledge—but in a way that creates a genuine alliance.
- **Birth of a rule:** The narrator explicitly states that this incident is why Han Li adopts his “no profit, no rescue” policy. It’s the moment a somewhat decent village boy starts turning into the cold, calculating cultivator he will become.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**The Poisonous Oath (毒誓):** In Chinese folk and martial fiction, a poisonous oath is not just words. It invokes supernatural retribution on the swearer and their family if broken. Here, Han Li uses it as a tool to bypass Li’s suspicion—but the fact that Li accepts it as binding shows just how seriously such oaths are taken in this world. It’s a transaction, not a prayer.

**The Medicine-Saver’s Dilemma:** Han Li’s internal vow to “never save anyone without benefit” is a staple of the Mortal Stream sub-genre. In Western fantasy, saving a stranger is heroic; here, it’s treated as a liability analysis gone wrong. The narrative doesn’t punish Han Li for this cold logic—it rewards him. That’s the core tonal difference: the world is hostile, and only those who treat charity as a risk survive.

**Pill Dependency as Power Structure:** Li Feiyu’s Marrow-Extracting Pill isn’t just a plot device—it’s a metaphor for how the cultivation world hooks its underdogs. You get power, but you pay with agony and a shortened lifespan. Han Li’s ability to produce a painkiller doesn’t break the cycle; it just makes the cycle bearable. That’s the best deal anyone can hope for here.

## Reading Guide
This is a **character-establishing chapter**, not an action one. Watch how Han Li manages the confrontation: he doesn’t panic, doesn’t beg, doesn’t posture heroically. He offers a solution (the oath) and then immediately pivots to a tangible offer (the painkiller). This is the blueprint for how he will handle almost every future negotiation—identify leverage, offer a trade, never show vulnerability.

Also, note the narrator’s direct commentary. This is rare in xianxia, but here the author flatly tells us that this chapter is why Han Li becomes the way he is. That’s your cue: this isn’t just a scene, it’s an origin story for his core trait.

For newcomers, the “poisonous oath” might feel archaic. But in this world, it’s a socially enforced contract—break it and you lose face, incur karma, or worse. Han Li uses it as a bargaining chip, not a spiritual act. That’s the pragmatic tone you should expect going forward.
