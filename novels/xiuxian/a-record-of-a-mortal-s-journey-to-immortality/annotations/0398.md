## Chapter Overview

In this chapter, Han Li faces one of the most brutally pragmatic logistical puzzles the cultivation world can offer: you have an S-tier war asset, but you can’t feed it, and you don’t have the time or resources to raise it to fighting strength before you *need* it to fight. Our favorite mortal-stream protagonist runs the numbers on the Gold Devourer Beetles, finds them wanting for his immediate timeline, and does what he always does when a plan fails the cost-benefit analysis: he shelves it, pivots, and doubles down on what actually works. The result is a masterclass in resource management, capped by a healthy dose of obsessive puppet manufacturing and a clear-eyed view of his next expedition.

## Key Plot Points

- Han Li systematically tests the Gold Devourer Beetles' dietary preferences: insect feed, raw ores, spirit stones, blood-meat—all fail to cause growth. Only devouring other strange insects (like the dead Golden-Back Demon Mantis eggs) produces a visible change.
- The Spirit Nourishing Pill works on the beetles, but the effect is diluted across hundreds of individuals; the Bloodjade Spiders show far more dramatic results from the same dose.
- Han Li does the math: even with the Heaven Vial's accelerated herb production, he cannot raise a functionally powerful swarm (tens of thousands) within a century. He *immediately* loses enthusiasm and shifts resources to the Bloodjade Spiders.
- Over two years of seclusion, Han Li manufactures over two hundred second-tier beast-shaped puppets, greatly improves his talisman-crafting skills, and pushes the Bloodjade Spiders to top-grade first-tier with concentrated Spirit Nourishing Pills.
- Qu Hun completes the refinement of the Chaos Yuan Bowl (about 70% power), but Han Li realizes that forging Qu Hun a personal magic treasure would be too expensive and time-consuming.
- Han Li concludes his preparations are sufficient for hunting fifth-grade demon beasts and heads to the Heaven Star City market to buy maps and intelligence on the outer sea.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**The Economics of "Raising Insects" (育虫) in Xianxia**

In Chinese web novels, "raising insects" is a distinct sub-specialty of cultivation that straddles alchemy and beast taming. Unlike pets or spirit beasts, insect swarms are typically treated as consumable military assets: you don't bond with them emotionally, you breed them, feed them, and throw them at the enemy. The "Strange Insect List" (奇虫榜) is a recurring trope, a Daoist version of a bestiary ranking that obsessive insect-fixated cultivators compile from lost ancient texts.

What makes this chapter's treatment so different from most xianxia is Han Li's immediate, cold-eyed cost-benefit analysis. A standard protagonist might see the Gold Devourer Beetles and think "this is my ultimate trump card; I will nurture it for a thousand chapters." Han Li sees the feeding requirements, calculates the time to effective strength, and decides the ROI isn't there. This is the mortal stream's signature move: it treats legendary insects not as plot coupons, but as capital assets with a maturity timeline. If they're not ready in time for the next expedition, they get cut from the budget.

**The "Soul-Suppressing Bowl" (聚魂钵): A Recycled Horror**

Han Li uses the souls stored in his Soul Gathering Bowl for puppet refinement. The narrative coldly notes that these souls "have lost their consciousness and the qualification for reincarnation, so they can only be used as materials." This is not a moment of moral reflection; it's a logistics note. In a dark-fantasy western setting, this would be a villain's origin story. In the mortal stream, it's Tuesday. The bowl itself is a macabre fantasy of efficiency: a premade, portable inventory of disposable animating force, harvested from the lingering dead, stripped of personhood, and ready for industrial use.

## Reading Guide

This is a "quiet victory" chapter—the kind that mortal-stream readers devour and that genre tourists find boring. There are no explosions, no boss fights, no dramatic breakthroughs. Instead, you get two hundred carefully manufactured puppets, a canceled insect-breeding project, and a trip to the shopping district. If that sounds tedious, you're missing the point. The mortal stream's tension is not in the *action* of the fight, but in the *preparation* for the fight. Every puppet Han Li builds is a die he's casting for his next battle. Every failed beetle experiment is a mistake he won't repeat six hundred miles offshore. Watch how he cancels a legendary asset without a single regret—that's the core character note. This is a man who treats his own ambitions like a budget spreadsheet, and he's not afraid to delete the luxury line items.
