## Chapter Overview

Who knew pharmaceutical research could be this thrilling in a cultivation novel? Chapter 25 takes us deep into Han Li’s lab phase, following a pattern that will become his signature: wild exhilaration, followed by methodical, almost paranoid testing. The ripening effect of the mysterious green liquid is confirmed, and the result is spectacular. But Han Li—never one to trust a gift horse without examining its teeth—immediately pivots to risk management. He tests the herbs on rabbits, agonizes over whether the vial will produce more liquid, and is then stonewalled by a half-month of cloudy weather. The emotional rollercoaster is real, and it gives us one of the first cracks in Han Li’s perpetually cool facade. Get ready, because this chapter is less about fighting and more about the quiet, obsessive grind that separates a survivor from a reckless fool.

## Key Plot Points

- Han Li discovers the overnight transformation: the herbs have aged from one-to-two years to over a decade, displaying textbook signs of maturity.
- He experiences a rare, full-throated emotional release—somersaults, laughter, wild daydreaming about infinite silver—before reining himself in.
- Han Li immediately identifies three critical unknowns: the herbs’ actual toxicity, whether the vial will produce more green liquid, and the exact method of controlling the ripening process.
- He buys two new rabbits to test the herbs’ safety, this time tethering them outside his room for continuous observation.
- He feeds them medicated food and confirms no poisoning; the rabbits actually grow healthier, hinting that the ripened herbs are safe or even beneficial in small doses.
- Han Li tests the vial that night under overcast skies—and nothing happens. His hope sinks as dawn approaches with no result.
- He deduces the key condition: the vial’s green liquid only manifests on clear nights when stars and moonlight are unobstructed.
- The chapter ends on a frustrated note: a half-month of continuous drizzle prevents him from confirming his theory, deepening his anxiety.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

One of the most underappreciated aspects of *Mortal Stream* fiction is the rigorous, almost scientific methodology Han Li applies to his supernatural discoveries. This chapter is a textbook example. He doesn’t just accept a miracle; he cross-checks it against herbal manuals, runs animal trials, and deduces environmental variables. The emphasis on medicinal taxonomy—leaf color, petal count, fruit skin—reflects the deep cultural weight placed on herbalism in Chinese medicine, where subtle visual cues determine potency and toxicity. For Western readers, think of it as a cultivation-era lab protocol: Han Li’s anxiety isn’t paranoia; it’s due diligence. Also note the importance of the sky: in Daoist cosmology, the interplay of heavenly bodies with earthly energy is fundamental. The green liquid’s dependence on moonlight and stars isn’t arbitrary—it suggests an alchemical connection to celestial essence, a recurring motif in xianxia. The half-month rain delay isn’t just a plot contrivance; it reinforces that even with a miraculous tool, the cultivation path is defined by waiting, uncertainty, and limited windows of opportunity.

### Qi Deviation

A life-threatening condition where a cultivator's internal energy spirals out of control; often triggered by emotional shock, attachment, or karma.

## Reading Guide

This chapter is a masterclass in showing how Han Li’s mind works. Pay attention to the sequence: euphoria, calculation, experimentation, frustration, deduction. It’s a cycle he’ll repeat for hundreds of chapters. The rabbit test is also a quiet callback to Chapter 24, where undiluted green liquid was lethally toxic. Now, the ripened herbs are harmless or even beneficial—which suggests the liquid’s interaction with plants is fundamentally different from direct consumption. Also, note the emotional beat: Han Li does somersaults. That’s huge. For a character who usually registers emotion as granular data points, this is a volcanic eruption. It makes his subsequent calm deduction even more impressive—and the rain-induced despair more relatable. If you’re a new reader, understand that this patience-grind is the emotional core of orthodox xianxia. No one hands you power; you wrestle it from an indifferent universe, one frustrating meteorological forecast at a time.
