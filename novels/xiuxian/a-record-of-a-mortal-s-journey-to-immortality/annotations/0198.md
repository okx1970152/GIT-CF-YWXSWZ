## Chapter Overview
In this chapter, we finally see Han Li put those *Gold Cicada Mother-and-Child Blades* to their most wicked use yet—laying an invisible trap that would make any survivalist proud. After dispatching the giant centipede with a gruesomely clever ambush (who knew muddy blades could be so effective?), he collects the precious Purple Ape Fruit and even loots a piece of the beast’s absurdly tough shell for future armor. But the real fireworks happen in a nearby valley, where three cultivators face off over two ripe spirit herbs. The interplay of strength, alliance, and cold calculation shows exactly why the mortal stream world is no place for lone hotheads.

## Key Plot Points
- Han Li discovers that his top-grade tools barely scratch the giant centipede’s shell, forcing him to rely on a pre-set trap of overturned blades.
- He reveals his ambush method: eight child blades of the Gold Cicada set in a line along the passage, their edges blackened with mud, so the pursuing centipede slices itself open.
- After the centipede dies, Han Li cuts several pieces of its extremely hard back shell to take away for potential armor crafting.
- He successfully collects the unripe Purple Ape Fruit and prepares to head to his next target—likely some unripe Celestial Spirit Fruit.
- In a valley to the southeast, a Tianque Fortress youth kills a Blazing Horn Deer solo and claims two ripe Purple Ape Fruit, but an old man (with a weird flying staff) and a Qingxu Valley Daoist arrive.
- The old man and Daoist decide to ally and split the herbs, ignoring the youth’s protests.
- The youth tries a desperate snatch-and-flee tactic but is blocked by the old man’s flying staff and then menaced by the Daoist’s incredibly fast Spirit Fox Step.
- Realizing he is outclassed, the youth flees. The Daoist lets him go, surprising the old man, but the alliance holds—for now.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Ambush Tactics in Xianxia:** Han Li’s trap is a textbook example of “using the environment to overcome a stronger opponent.” In many martial arts stories, laying traps (especially with blades or spikes) is considered lowly or dishonorable—which is exactly why it works in the Mortal Stream. The giant centipede, with its armored body and deadly venom, never got to use its main strengths because Han Li bypassed direct combat entirely. This reinforces the core principle that *preparation beats raw power*.

**The Gold Cicada Mother-and-Child Blades:** This name comes from a common xianxia trope: a “mother” blade that controls several smaller “child” blades. In this case, Han Li uses the child blades as disposable (or at least, detachable) assets for a field trap. The children can be sacrificed or lost, while the mother remains safe in his hand. This is a classic risk-management design.

**The “Two vs. One” Politics:** The old man and Daoist’s immediate alliance—without even discussing terms—shows how quickly cultivators in this world form temporary pacts. The youth, despite being wealthy and powerful enough to kill an upper-grade beast alone, is instantly neutralized because he has no social backup. This is the dark truth of cultivation: *unilateral strength is fragile; alliances are what truly decide territory.*

## Reading Guide
Fellow Daoists, how many of you yelled “YES!” when Han Li’s muddy-blade trap clicked into place? That’s the stuff. This chapter is a love letter to cunning preparation—no flashy magic, just pure strategy. Also, can we appreciate the absolute *nerve* of that old man and Daoist? They basically say, “We’re the adults here, kid, run along,” and the youth *runs*. No dramatic last stand, no honor death—just cold, pragmatic survival. The Mortal Stream doesn’t do heroics. And honestly? It’s refreshing. Han Li’s approach to harvesting the centipede’s shell is equally perfect: take what has maximum utility, leave the rest. That’s how you survive against a system that chews up the greedy. Next stop: Celestial Spirit Fruit—and probably more conflict.
