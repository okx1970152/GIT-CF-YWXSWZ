## Chapter Overview

Negotiation time. Han Li and Zi Ling finally sit down for the talk we’ve been waiting for, and it’s a masterclass in cutthroat diplomacy wrapped in tea-and-smiles etiquette. The Divine Thunder Bamboo is real—but it comes with a string attached that Han Li didn’t see coming: an invitation to become an invited elder of the splintered Wonderful Sound Sect. What starts as a straightforward haggle evolves into a cleverly crafted compromise that benefits both sides, and it’s a beautiful showcase of how deals get done in the cultivation world—where everyone’s holding a knife behind their back while sipping fragrant tea.

## Key Plot Points

- Zi Ling drops the first bombshell: she sees through Qu Hun as Han Li’s External Incarnation, demonstrating her sharp insight and the inability to keep this secret forever.
- The three surviving women have no intention of returning to the Wonderful Sound Sect, anticipating betrayal or absorption by other powers. They plan to relocate the sect to Heaven Star City.
- The Divine Thunder Bamboo reveal: the sect originally acquired two sections, not one. The root-bearing half was kept by Zi Ling, making her the sole holder of the intact treasure.
- Zi Ling proposes an invited elder arrangement: Han Li enjoys the perks of a sect elder without the obligations, while the sect uses his name as a deterrent.
- Han Li accepts after careful deliberation, securing the Divine Thunder Bamboo while limiting his exposure and obligations to zero unless he explicitly chooses to act.
- Zi Ling produces the Preservation Box made of Cream Jade, an other-element material that can store the Divine Thunder Bamboo indefinitely without leakage.
- The chapter closes with a rare author’s note (请假条) explaining a headache-related delay, a charming glimpse into the serialization treadmill.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Invited vs. Honorary Elder: A Structural Distinction**

The idea of an “invited elder” (客卿长老) is a staple of sect politics in xianxia fiction, but it’s not just a fancy title. Unlike a true elder, who is bound by sect rules and can be ordered into battle, a invited elder is a contractor—they provide a service (usually reputation or occasional violence) in exchange for benefits, but their allegiance is strictly transactional. Zi Ling’s proposal to make Han Li a “nominal elder” (名义长老) takes this one step further: he gets everything, gives nothing unless he chooses, and the sect absorbs all the risk of his name being used. It’s a brilliant piece of institutional arbitrage by a desperate but clever leader.

**Cream Jade and the Five Elements Exemption**

The Preservation Box (膏玉盒) is made of Cream Jade, a material “outside the five elements.” This is a subtle but powerful concept in xianxia cosmology. Most materials are bound to one of the Five Elements (Metal, Wood, Water, Fire, Earth), and those elemental properties can interact with the stored item—a wood-element container might nourish wood-element herbs, but also extract energy from them over time. A neutral container avoids this entirely, preserving the item in stasis. Cream Jade is essentially the Tupperware of immortal treasures, and its rarity tells you how few cultivators have the luxury of proper long-term storage.

## Reading Guide

Welcome to the kind of chapter that makes Mortal Stream fans pur with satisfaction: no bloodshed, no explosions, just a perfectly balanced power play over tea. Han Li enters as the strong silent type with a bargaining chip (his reputation), and Zi Ling enters as a cunning survivor who knows that a Core Formation cultivator’s name is worth more than a dozen magic tools. The negotiation is a chess match, not a shouting match, and it’s a pleasure to see Han Li get exactly what he wants while walking away with zero commitment.

Pay close attention to Zi Ling’s phrasing—every clause she adds to the “nominal elder” proposal is designed to seem generous, but it’s also a cage. Han Li spots it, counters, and the final deal is a clean compromise. That’s classic Mortal Stream politicking: the real battle happens in the spacing between words.

Also, yes, the author’s note at the end about getting sick while staying up late is part of the web novel experience. These personal asides (请假条) are a beloved part of serialized Chinese fiction culture—the author is basically writing to their readers as friends, apologizing for life getting in the way. Don’t skip it; it’s part of the charm.
