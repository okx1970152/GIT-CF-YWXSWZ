## Chapter Overview

This chapter is a masterclass in the Mortal Stream’s *expedition logic*. Han Li and his temporary party finally reach their objective’s core—an underground chamber—only to find a locked-down Sealing Spirit Pillar and a tense debate about pushing it over. The drama here is not about *adventure* or *discovery* but a cold, hard risk assessment: what’s on the other side could be a fortune in rare spirit herbs (the legendary Seven Iris Flower) or a reality-ending demon. Every character speaks their *price* for proceeding. The pacing is tight, the tension is buried in unspoken calculations, and the final reveal—a soul-crushing lucky punch of a discovery—lands with that classic *RMJI* punch: here’s your treasure, but somebody died holding it, and maybe that’s not a good omen.

## Key Plot Points

1. **The Sealing Spirit Pillar Debate**: The party identifies the Sealing Spirit Pillar and discusses its two possible uses—sealing a legendary herb or suppressing a terrifying demon. This triggers a careful social dance where each cultivator states their position. Jian is all-in for profit; Hu Yue admits to being a broke rogue cultivator who needs the gamble; Han Li is noncommittal but internally calculates his own absurdly high confidence (72 Divine Thunder Bamboo flying swords). Shi Die flexes her family connections. This whole section operates on unspoken alliances and risk calculators, not trust.
2. **Qu Hun’s Strongman Feat**: The pillar can’t be budged with ordinary strength. Han Li silently orders Qu Hun to handle it, and the soulless corpse-servant does exactly that—a seamless demonstration of Han Li’s hidden trump card inventory being deployed without a word. It’s a quiet flex that reminds the party (and the reader) that Han Li is always ready.
3. **The Underground Hall**: After descending the hidden staircase, the party discovers a beautiful, well-lit chamber with two side passages and a central pool. The scene is layered with beauty (night pearls, sparkling walls) and danger (a skeleton with an arrow through its skull). The skeleton’s presence acts as an immediate reality check: someone powerful was here before, and it didn’t end well for them.
4. **The Seven Iris Flower**: The big reveal. A three-colored lotus bud floating in a pool of what Hu Yue identifies as Thousand-Year Stone Milk. This is a legendary spirit herb, practically priceless, and it’s *right there*. But the presence of the mysterious skeleton and the Sealing Spirit Pillar above it all scream that this location is a trap, a sealed crime scene, or a failed final stand. The chapter ends on a perfect note of high-value-loot tension combined with an unspoken threat.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes

**Sealing Spirit Pillar (封灵柱, Fēng Líng Zhù)**: This is a classic xianxia architecture trope: a massive stone or jade pillar that functions as a reality-sealing valve. In this chapter, it does exactly what it says—it isolates an area’s spiritual energy, preventing leaks that would either degrade a rare herb or let a demon escape. The trick is that you need brute strength or a specialized technique to rotate and lay it flat. No fancy incantations here—just grunt work. That’s *RMJI*: even ancient magical artifacts require you to get your hands dirty. Also note the materialistic reading: a formation that’s overkill in cost usually signals an *understated* value underneath, not a cheap trick. In xianxia economy, if someone spent this much to lock something up, it’s either a priceless treasure or a world-ending threat—and the party is betting it’s both.

**Seven Iris Flower (七霞莲, Qī Xiá Lián)**: A legendary spirit herb whose name literally means “Seven Rosy Clouds Lotus.” The petals correspond to different colored light, and its signature trait is manifesting a miniature rainbow above it. In standard xianxia alchemy, such lotus-type herbs are often the core ingredient for high-grade pill formulas (e.g., for breaking through major bottlenecks). The fact that this one is only three-colored suggests it’s not fully mature—but in the brutal resource economy of the Mortal Stream, even an unripe lotus of this caliber is worth more than most cultivators will ever see in their lifetimes. The Thousand-Year Stone Milk pool it grows in is itself a legendary medium, used for accelerating plant growth and preserving spiritual essence.

**Why the Skeleton Matters**: In xianxia, a sealed treasure site often comes with a corpse or a remnant. It’s not a souvenir—it’s a *warning*. That foot-long emerald arrow is not decorative; it’s a killing stroke, probably from a self-targeting trap or a bitter duel. The corpse’s presence automatically re-classifies the entire location from “adventure” to “high-risk forensic investigation.” Han Li, of course, pretends not to care but is already mentally mapping the death, the arrow, and the exit routes.

## Reading Guide

This is one of those *RMJI* chapters that’s almost pure procedural craft—every phrase is a weighted stone in a risk-reward scale. The party isn’t bonding or celebrating; they are consciously inventorying their odds. Jian is a brash gambler betting on his niche demon-repelling technique. Hu Yue is the broke pragmatist who *has* to take the shot. Shi Die is the wealthy brat whose arrogance is propped up by Daddy’s gear. And Han Li? He’s already decided he can handle whatever’s inside, but he’s going to let everyone else test the waters first. The skeleton by the pool is the real scene-stealer—it’s a perfect *RMJI* image: someone else found this delicious treasure, and now they’re a broken skull with an arrow through it. The lotus is beautiful, but it’s growing on a grave. The reader should feel the same mix of lust and dread that the characters feel: *I want it... but maybe I shouldn’t want it that much*.
