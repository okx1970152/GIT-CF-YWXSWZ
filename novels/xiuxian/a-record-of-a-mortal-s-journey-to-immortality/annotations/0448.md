## Chapter Overview
Get ready, fellow Daoists—the Void Heaven Palace just turned up the heat. In this chapter, the black-robed man (yes, Extreme Yin himself) nearly gets ghost-drained but survives thanks to a mysterious white light that shatters his ancient mirror. Meanwhile, the ghost army finally decides to stop playing: a dozen Wéiwú ghosts encircle our three cultivators, with two tougher ghost shadows and two green-haired yakshas thrown in. Han Li, ever the cold accountant of risk, assigns the minions to his reluctant allies and takes the bosses himself. The real highlight? A green ghost orb reveals its trump card—a solid tiger-shaped artifact spirit, something Han Li has been hunting for years. But his sword arts are no ordinary tools; they resist the yin qi as if they were born for ghost-slaying. This chapter is a masterclass in Mortal Stream combat logic: no flashy heroics, just precise resource allocation and tactical testing of the enemy’s toys.

## Key Plot Points
- Extreme Yin’s near-death escape: The gray shadow would have killed him, but a white light (resembling a giant bird) repels it and shatters his bronze ancient mirror, saving his life at the cost of the artifact.
- Han Li notices the mirror’s spiritual energy vanish, confirming it’s now useless. Extreme Yin, offended by Han Li’s earlier non-intervention, snubs him but can’t deny the situation.
- The Crying Soul Beast returns to Extreme Yin’s shoulder after sucking in one Wéiwú ghost, showing its specialized ghost-eating ability but getting tied up by two yakshas.
- The ghost army emerges: seven or eight Wéiwú ghosts (late Foundation-tier), two ghost shadows, and the green-haired yakshas form a complete encirclement.
- Han Li instantly delegates: “You two take the Wéiwú; I’ll take the ghost shadows. Buy me time.” No debate, no pep talk—pure tactical command.
- The black ghost shadow uses a green ghost orb that spews yin qi to entrap Han Li’s flying swords. Han Li’s Green Bamboo Cloudswarm Swords transform into flood dragons and tear through the yin qi, surprising the ghost.
- The ghost shadow unleashes its ace: the orb transforms into a solid demon tiger—an artifact spirit. Han Li is genuinely startled but quickly recovers, recognizing the rarity and the mechanism behind artifact spirits.
- A flashback infodump on artifact spirits: one-time seals, low success rates with powerful spirits, and why most treasures lack them. Han Li’s frustration at never finding a flood dragon for his own swords is noted.
- Han Li decides to test the tiger’s power head-on, sending his sword-dragons forward without fear.

## Cultural / Xianxia Notes
**Artifact Spirits (器灵, Qì Líng)** – In xianxia metaphysics, an artifact spirit is the soul of a slain beast or ghost sealed into a magical treasure. This concept draws loosely from Daoist ideas of “bound spirits” and talismanic crafting, but in *RMJI* it’s purely mechanical: a one-time gamble that can make or break a weapon. The seal must happen during the treasure’s lifecycle and only once; if it fails, the treasure remains spiritless forever. Most cultivators hoard powerful souls (like Nangong Wan with her Ink Flood Dragon) and wait for perfect conditions, but many fail because the stronger the spirit, the lower the success rate. This creates a brutal scarcity: in the entire Chaotic Star Sea, Han Li has never captured a true flood dragon spirit, so his own swords remain spiritless—a quiet disadvantage that he offsets with raw sword refinement.

**Wéiwú Ghosts (炜吾鬼)** – A ferocious type of ghost that resembles a horned, clawed humanoid. In Chinese folklore, such ghosts are often associated with violent death and stagnant yin energy. In the cultivation world, a single Wéiwú can terrorize a region; here, a dozen appear as cannon fodder, underscoring the insane difficulty of the Void Heaven Palace expedition.

**Green-haired Yakshas (绿毛夜叉)** – Yakshas are originally nature spirits in Buddhist and Hindu mythology, but xianxia reimagines them as burly, green-furred zombies wielding bone forks that shoot ghost-fire. They are rare daylight-capable variants, adding to the pressure on the allies.

## Reading Guide
This chapter is a textbook demonstration of how *RMJI* stages group battles: the protagonist never takes unnecessary risks. Han Li immediately assigns the safe (ish) job to his companions while he tackles the unknowns. His internal reaction to the artifact spirit—surprise, then cold interest, then immediate tactical decision to test it—perfectly captures his engineering mindset. The infodump on artifact spirits is dropped at just the right moment: instead of pausing the action, it deepens the tension by showing the stakes (that tiger is a serious threat). Also note the subtle worldbuilding: Extreme Yin’s broken mirror reacted to the gray shadow’s weakness to light—a hint that these ghost shadows have a light-element vulnerability. Keep your eyes peeled for how Han Li will exploit that. And remember: in the Mortal Stream, the first exchange is always a probe, not a commitment. Han Li hasn’t even started fighting seriously yet.

**Deep Dive: The Economics of Tool Spirits** – Why does almost no one have artifact spirits in *RMJI*? Because the system is designed to favor the lucky or the ultra-prepared. A mid-grade cultivator can spend decades hunting a suitable beast soul, only to fail the seal in seconds. This scarcity reinforces the novel’s core theme: resources are never enough, and shortcuts are always gambles. Han Li’s decision to test the tiger rather than retreat shows his curiosity as a martial scholar—but also his cold confidence that even if the tiger is strong, his trump cards (still hidden) can handle it.
