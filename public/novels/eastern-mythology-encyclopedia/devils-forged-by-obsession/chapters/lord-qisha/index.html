Lord Qisha

七杀魔君

Entry0047 Type魔种包 VolumeDevils Forged by Obsession Updated2026-05-19T19:00:55+08:00

Qisha Mojun (Lord Qisha, the Butcher of the Nine Peaks) was not a primordial monster or a chaotic remnant—he was a man who watched his entire sect, three thousand brothers and sisters, burn alive in a single night. In the ashes of that day, he made a choice that sealed his fate: he refused to let go of the rage, and let the rage become the only thing he was. He became a walking execution ground, a being who could never stop killing because stopping meant remembering what he had lost.

七杀魔君 (Lord Qisha, the Butcher of the Nine Peaks)
堕落之源: 被正道联手诛杀宗门兄弟的复仇执念 (Obsessive vengeance for the sect slaughtered by the combined forces of orthodoxy)
Epoch of Transformation: Post-Honghuang Era, during the mortal age of sect warfare.
Current Demon Rank: Obsession-Bound (执念缠身者).
Sphere of Influence: The territories of the seven destroyed orthodox sects and the southern barbarian regions.

None. The Skull Palace was reportedly razed by the orthodox coalition after his death. No confirmed ruins or sealed sites associated with Lord Qisha survive.

Lord Qisha's narrative is intimately connected to the destruction of the Xuánmíng School, the blood oath that created the Qisha Sword, and the collective memory of the Seven Orthodox Sects in the southern regions. His rise and fall illustrate the obsession-based path of Mo transformation, where a fixation on vengeance rewrites the cultivator's entire being. The figure of his backstabbing strategist remains unnamed in most accounts, serving only as a narrative device that underscores the tragic inevitability of the Mo's end. His laughter at the moment of death, echoing the camaraderie of his former sect, is the most cited image in later tellings—a final glimpse of the man who was, before the night that broke him.

Lord Qisha is classified as an Obsession-Bound (执念缠身者), the first and most common stage of Mo transformation. In this stage, the original self has not yet been fully consumed by the fixation; the being retains memories, emotions, and even moments of clarity, but every action is now filtered through the lens of the obsession. For Qisha, the obsession is total vengeance against the orthodox factions that annihilated the Xuánmíng School. His identity as a grieving disciple and his identity as a butcher coexist in a single consciousness, though the butcher has long taken command. This stage can persist for centuries, but it is inherently unstable—any further intensification of the fixation may birth a separate obsession-entity, pushing the being into the Yan Mo stage. Qisha never crossed that threshold. His descent was cut short by death.

Lord Qisha was born into a southern barbarian tribe, a child of raw strength and a natural diet of raw flesh. His天赋 caught the attention of the Xuánmíng School (玄冥宗), the largest Mo sect of its time, which took him in as an inner disciple. His cultivation progressed at an astonishing rate. The turning point came when the seven major orthodox sects laid a coordinated ambush on the Xuánmíng School. Three thousand disciples were slaughtered over the course of a night. His master, using his own body as a shield, carved a path for Qisha to escape—the last gift of a dying teacher. In that moment, watching the sect burn behind him, Qisha's cultivation inverted. The spiritual energy that had once flowed toward enlightenment now reversed course, feeding his rage. He spent the next one hundred years in hiding, forging a sword not with metal but with a blood oath, using the bones of the seven sect leaders he had secretly slain one by one. The Qisha Sword (七杀剑) was the crystallization of his descent: every inch of its blade soaked in the obsession that had rewired his meridians. His former identity as a promising disciple was consumed; only the Butcher remained.

The obsession that drives Lord Qisha is a vengeance knot (执念死结) anchored to the mass execution of the Xuánmíng School. Every orthodox cultivator, regardless of individual guilt, is perceived as a living piece of that night. His vision is said to tint red when a righteous aura approaches; the sound of orthodox incantations triggers auditory flashbacks of his brothers' dying screams. His olfactory sense became hypersensitive to the scent of certain spell residues, which he associates with the ambush. These sensory distortions are not voluntary—they operate as a permanent filter, turning the entire world into a crime scene that demands punishment. The fixation cannot be reasoned with because it is no longer a thought; it is the architecture of his perception.

In the state of Blazing Skandhas (五蕴炽盛), Lord Qisha's hunger is directed specifically at the life force and fear of orthodox cultivators. He finds no satisfaction in ordinary food; only the hot spray of a righteous cultivator's blood or the crack of a spell breaking under his sword provides a brief, ecstatic relief. This relief lasts only a few heartbeats before a deeper, colder emptiness settles in, driving him to find the next victim. During these intervals, his mind sometimes clears enough for him to recall his master's face or a laugh shared with a fellow disciple—but the clarity is agonizing, and he silences it with more killing. He is aware that he is trapped in a cycle he cannot break, but that awareness only fuels his rage.

As an Obsession-Bound, Lord Qisha has not yet spawned an independent obsession-entity (执念意识体). His original consciousness and his killer instinct remain integrated in one vessel, though the balance is heavily tilted toward the Butcher. In battle, the obsession speaks through him; he may mutter grievances from the night of the massacre while delivering a killing blow. But in rare moments of stillness—when no enemy remains—the voice of the grieving disciple surfaces. He has been known to weep silently in his throne room, surrounded by the skulls of his enemies, before the bloodlust returns and scours the tears away. This internal contradiction is the hallmark of the Obsession-Bound stage: the self is not yet dissolved, but it is a prisoner in its own home.

Lord Qisha's most infamous act was the forging of the Qisha Sword (七杀剑), a blade crafted from the bones of the seven orthodox sect leaders he had murdered in secret over a century. With this weapon, he raised an army of rogue cultivators and systematically destroyed the seven sects that had annihilated the Xuánmíng School. At each conquered sect, he offered no quarter: every disciple, elder, and servant was killed. On the ruins of the strongest sect, he constructed the Skull Palace (骷髅殿), a fortress whose throne was built from the heads of orthodox masters. The orthodox world launched multiple campaigns against him, but his military cunning and personal ferocity repelled them for years. The end came through betrayal: his trusted strategist, bribed or coerced by the orthodox coalition, poisoned his spiritual meridians during a decisive battle. Weakened, Qisha was pierced by a hundred swords from a thousand attacking cultivators. In his final moment, he let out a laugh—the same laugh he had shared with his brothers in better days—and his spirit shattered into nothing.

Lord Qisha's relationship with the immortal dao (仙道) is one of absolute enmity. The orthodox sects represent everything he hates; he never sought reconciliation and never expected it. His relationship with the divine path (神道) is undocumented; the Celestial Court appears to have taken no direct action against him, perhaps considering him a mortal affair. The Buddhist path (佛门) has no recorded interaction with him; no monk attempted to convert him, likely due to his reputation. Among the mortal world, especially the southern barbarian tribes, he is remembered as both a tragic hero and a monster. Some outcast cultivators and rogue Yaoguai joined his army, attracted by his promise of plunder and the destruction of established powers. Fear of his name persists in the region, with parents using "the Butcher will get you" to discipline unruly children.

Lord Qisha's current state is total annihilation. His death in battle resulted in the complete dissolution of his spirit, leaving no residue for reincarnation. There is no record of Heavenly Tribulation (天劫) or Cosmic Obliteration (天谴) descending upon him; instead, the universe's law of cause and effect was carried out through the hands of orthodox cultivators. His soul was not granted a second chance. Within the cosmic order, he is remembered as a cautionary instance: a being who chose revenge over release, and in doing so ensured his own permanent erasure. No path remained for him—no reincarnation, no redemption, no lingering trace.

Lore Notes

Xuánmíng School (玄冥宗)

The largest Mo sect of its time in the mortal realm, annihilated by a coalition of seven orthodox sects. Lord Qisha was an inner disciple.

Qisha Sword (七杀剑)

A blade forged from the bones of seven orthodox sect leaders, bound by a blood oath. It personifies Lord Qisha's obsessed vengeance.

Skull Palace (骷髅殿)

A fortress built on the ruins of the strongest orthodox sect, featuring a throne constructed from the heads of killed cultivators.

Seven Orthodox Sects (正道七派)

The collective name of the seven righteous factions that destroyed the Xuánmíng School and later fell to Qisha's revenge.

Southern Barbarian Tribes (南疆蛮族)

The native culture of Lord Qisha, known for physical strength and raw rituals.

FAQ

Why is he called "Seven Kills" (七杀)?

The name refers to the seven orthodox sects he eradicated in revenge for his own sect's destruction. "Qi" (seven) and "Sha" (kill) directly reflect his deed.

How was the Qisha Sword made?

Over a century, Qisha secretly assassinated the leaders of the seven orthodox sects, then forged a sword using their skull bones as the core material, bound by a blood oath of vengeance.

Did Lord Qisha ever achieve a higher Mo stage?

No. He remained an Obsession-Bound until his death. His obsession never spawned a separate entity; he was always both the butcher and the grieving disciple.

What happened to his spirit after death?

His spirit was completely shattered by the combined sword strike and the betrayal poison. No trace remained for reincarnation—total cosmic erasure.

Is there any connection to the Celestial Court?

No direct interaction is recorded. The orthodox cultivators acted as agents of cosmic law in ending him, not the Heavenly Bureaucracy.

Entry Guide

Imagine you are in a tavern, and an old scholar leans across the table. He says: "Forget everything you think you know about 'demon kings.' The Butcher of the Nine Peaks was not born a monster. He was a boy from the southern wilderness who loved his sect so fiercely that when it burned, he could not let the fire go. So he carried it inside him. For a hundred years, he built a sword out of the bones of the men who killed his brothers—one bone per murdered sect leader, each one a silent shriek. And when the sword was ready, he didn't just kill them. He killed everything they had ever built. He built a palace from their skulls and sat on a throne of heads, listening for echoes of voices that would never speak again." The scholar pauses, takes a long drink, and says: "That is the tragedy of a Mo. He didn't become a monster because he was evil. He became a monster because he refused to be healed."

You might have heard the name "Qi Sha" in passing—a generic demon lord from certain xianxia novels, a cardboard cutout evil overlord. But the version that circulates in the deeper mythic tradition is more specific. He's not just any dark lord. He is the perfect example of what happens when a person's need to avenge becomes the entire operating system of their soul. The common stories skip over the most important part: how he got there. They just show him being a blood-soaked tyrant. But the transformation logic is everything. In Eastern cosmology, a Mo is not a species—it's a condition. Qisha was once a human with friends, a master, and a future. The same sun that warmed his sect rose over the bodies of his brothers. And in that dawn, he chose to become a being that could never stop killing. Understanding that choice—the moment between love and ruin—is understanding what makes the Mo category so unsettling.

Let's look at how Qisha fell. There are two roads into Mo: the path of obsession and the path of chaotic contamination. Qisha took the first road. His obsession was simple: he could not accept that his sect was gone. The Daoist ideal of Supreme Detachment (太上忘情) demands that you release attachments when the cosmic order asks you to. Your master dies? Let him go. Your sect burns? Let it burn. That is the path of cultivation. But Qisha refused. In the moment he escaped the slaughter, his spiritual energy reversed—instead of rising toward purity, it began feeding his grief, his rage, his need for retaliation. This is not a moral failing. It is a violation of the physics of the spirit. Think of it like a river that suddenly starts flowing uphill, eroding its own banks. The obsession rewired his meridians. From then on, every breath he took was poison to his old self, and fuel to his new one. The blade he forged was not made of steel; it was made of that reversed energy, given shape by the bones of the men he had already begun to kill. It's a brutally efficient transformation: the more he killed, the stronger the obsession; the stronger the obsession, the more he needed to kill.

Once a cultivator descends into Mo, the sensory system recalibrates entirely. This is the Blazing Skandhas (五蕴炽盛). For Qisha, normal food became ash. The only thing that gave him a moment of relief was the death of an orthodox cultivator—the hot splash of blood across his face, the crack of a righteous spell breaking under his sword, the scent of fear rising from a fleeing elder. But that relief never lasted. Maybe five seconds of quiet, then a deeper emptiness would swallow him, and he would need to kill again. It is like an addiction that cannot be satisfied, only temporarily numbed. And the worst part: he was aware of it. He knew that every kill pushed him further from the man he used to be, but stopping meant facing the full weight of what he had lost. So he kept feeding the hunger, knowing it would never end, until the only end was death.

One concept that keeps coming up in Mo lore is the obsession-entity (执念意识体)—the point where the fixation becomes so strong that it births a separate consciousness inside the same body. Qisha never reached that stage. He remained an Obsession-Bound all the way to his death. That means there was no second voice in his head, no monster wearing his face while he watched from behind glass. It was always him. The Butcher and the grieving disciple were the same person. When he wept alone in his throne of skulls, those were his tears. When he butchered a monastery full of disciples, those were his hands. That integration makes him in some ways more tragic, not less. He didn't lose himself to a separate entity; he became the monster with his eyes wide open.

Qisha's power peaked after he had destroyed all seven sects and built his Skull Palace. He was a force that could crush any single orthodox army. But his strength was also his weakness: he was the center of his own gravity, and his entire army depended on his presence. The coalition that finally defeated him used the oldest trick in the book: betrayal from within. His strategist, for reasons that vary by account—bribes, coercion, or genuine fear—poisoned his meridians at the moment of the final battle. Weakened, Qisha was struck by a hundred swords simultaneously, a technique designed to shatter a Mo's spirit beyond repair. In his last breath, he laughed. It was not the laugh of a demon; it was the laugh of the boy who once wrestled with his brothers in the sect courtyard, before the world fell apart. Then his spirit dissolved, leaving nothing for the cycle of reincarnation to catch.

So why does the Eastern cosmos need a category like "Mo"? Because every system produces waste. In a universe governed by Dao, where everything has a proper flow and return, some beings break the flow so hard they cannot be folded back in. Qisha is a perfect example: he didn't just break the rules—he rewired himself to be a walking rule-breaker. The Dao has a provision for such cases: not judgment, not punishment in a moral sense, but pure erasure. No reincarnation, no second chance, no lingering ghost. He is gone. All those years of pain, all those hundreds of thousands of deaths, all that love twisted into hatred—gone, as if he never existed. And what remains? A story. A name whispered by southern tribes to frighten children. A caution carved into the memory of the cultivation world. The Mo's fate is to become a lesson, not a legacy. Every time you clench your jaw and refuse to let go of something you should release, you are touching the same edge Qisha touched. The difference is that he stepped over—and there was no coming back.
