Xi Feng

溪风

Entry0044 Type魔种包 VolumeDevils Forged by Obsession Updated2026-05-19T18:54:40+08:00

Xi Feng (a Mo forged from the shame of a monstrous face and the desperate longing for a beautiful voice) was not a conqueror or a destroyer. He was a creature who traded his one perfect gift for a lover's smile—a singer who sold his voice for a face worth seeing, and spent the rest of his existence in a silence that consumed him from within. His is a story not of cosmic rebellion, but of a heart that broke itself against the mirror of its own self-loathing.

溪风 / Xi Feng, The Singer of the Lost Voice
堕落之源：对丑陋容貌的自卑与对美好声音的渴望 / The shame of a monstrous visage and the yearning for a beautiful voice
Transformation Era: The period following the end of the Honghuang Ji Yuan (洪荒纪元), during the early establishment of the Three Realms' current order
Current Mo Realm Level: Obsession-Bound (执念缠身者)
Realm of Influence: The southern riverbanks of the mortal realm, and the depths of the sea near the border of the Mo Realm

None. No permanent forbidden zones or sealed sites are associated with Xi Feng.

This entry is closely connected to the figure of Chonglou (重楼), the Mo Sovereign who initiated the contract that transformed Xi Feng. The relationship is that of tempter and victim, with Xi Feng's path into Mo being directly engineered by Chonglou's intervention. The entry also relates to Shui Bi (水碧), a water deity whose love briefly masked the cost of Xi Feng's bargain. Her discovery of the truth and subsequent death are the emotional climax of his story. Further context on the nature of the obsession-based path into Mo can be found in related entries on the "Zhi Nian Si Jie" (Obsession Knot) and the figure of the "Ji Du" (Ketu), which also explores obsession-driven corruption, though on a vastly different scale. See the relation entries below for a structured reference.

Xi Feng is classified as an Obsession-Bound (执念缠身者), a being whose original self has been deeply warped by a terminal fixation but has not yet been fully consumed by an independent obsession-entity. His transformation dates back over a millennium, possibly longer. At this level, the original consciousness still exists and retains memories of its past life, but it is perpetually bound to the pain of its own unfulfilled desire. The obsession—the shame of his appearance and the longing to be loved—does not speak with a separate voice, but it governs every choice he makes. His self-perception is the cage in which he lives. He can still recognize the tragedy of his own state, but that recognition provides no path back to wholeness.

Xi Feng's descent into Mo did not originate from a rejection of the path of transcendent cultivation. He was not a dedicated Daoist or Buddhist practitioner who chose to defy the law of Tai Shang Wang Qing (太上忘情). He was a minor Yao (妖怪) spirit living by a river in the southern wilds—a creature born with a voice so beautiful it could still the wind, but a face so grotesque that any living thing that saw it fled in terror. There is no record of a single catastrophic moment of cultivation reversal. His transformation was slower, built from years of accumulated agony. The critical turning point came when he encountered the Mo Sovereign Chonglou (重楼), who offered a simple and cruel contract: Xi Feng would receive a perfect human face and a mortal identity in exchange for his voice. The moment he spoke his acceptance, his spiritual energy began to reorient itself around the loss. He did not reverse his cultivation—he hollowed his own center out. The self he had been—the singer, the hidden lover, the creature who poured his soul into songs he could never sing face-to-face—was traded for a mask. From that moment, the silence began to fester within him like a slow spiritual rot.

The specific form of Xi Feng's Zhi Nian Si Jie (执念死结) is a dual obsession: a profound disgust for his own physical form, intertwined with an all-consuming desire to be seen as worthy of love. The former is the wound; the latter is the imagined balm that can never truly heal it. After the contract with Chonglou, his perception of the world became filtered through the constant absence of his own voice. He could see the beauty of the world—the morning light on the water, the face of the woman he loved, Shui Bi (水碧)—but he could no longer participate in it through the one medium that had given his existence meaning. Every time he wanted to speak, to sing, to laugh aloud, the silence caught in his throat like a bone. The voice was not destroyed—it was taken, stored as a tangible object in Chonglou's possession. Xi Feng could feel its potential, its existence elsewhere, but he could not reach it. The obsession became a constant, grinding pressure: the awareness that he had paid the one thing that made his ugliness bearable, and now he was nothing but a beautiful mask over an empty core.

Xi Feng did not experience the classic Wu Yun Chi Sheng (五蕴炽盛) hunger for blood or terror. His form of corrupted sensory craving was more subtle and more specific: a craving for the act of being heard. In his hundred years with Shui Bi under the mortal name Feng Xi (风溪), he could not sing, could not speak of his past, could not tell her who he really was. The satisfaction he derived from her love was real—but it was incomplete, haunted by the perpetual awareness that it was obtained through a deception. Each moment of tenderness only deepened the hollow feeling. He did not feel physical hunger for living essence. Instead, he felt a spiritual starvation: the absence of his own voice echoing back to him in the silence between their conversations. The moments of relative peace were rare and fragile. In the deeper stages of his curse, he would retreat into darkness, where the silence became less oppressive, and where he could pretend that the loss had not happened. In those moments, his residual self-awareness flickered—he knew he had traded himself away, but he could not see a way to undo it without losing the love he had bought.

Xi Feng's obsession has not coalesced into a fully independent Yan Mo (魇魔) consciousness with its own voice and face. However, his self-loathing has created a persistent internal adversary: the voice of his own shame. This voice does not command his hands to kill or his feet to wander. It simply whispers, continuously, that he is not worthy of the love he has received, that the beauty of his borrowed face is a lie, and that Shui Bi would leave him the moment the truth was uncovered. This inner saboteur does not seize control of his body, but it corrupts his choices from within. When Shui Bi eventually discovered the truth, the voice was vindicated. Xi Feng's subsequent fury and despair were not the actions of a separate entity taking control. They were the original self, pushed past its limits, reacting with the only weapons left: rage and self-destruction. He did not lose his body to a stranger. He lost his hope, and the body that remained was merely a vessel for the collapse of everything he had built.

Xi Feng's most significant act was not a violent rampage but a self-surrender. After Shui Bi learned of his true nature and the terms of his contract with Chonglou, Xi Feng attempted to force a confrontation with the Mo Sovereign to reclaim his voice. The conflict was brief and one-sided. Chonglou, unimpressed by the challenge, transformed Xi Feng into a half-human, half-Mo creature and imprisoned him within the Mo Realm. No records exist of major battles with the Celestial Court, Daoist sects, or Buddhist orders. Xi Feng did not threaten the cosmic order. His legacy is one of personal tragedy rather than widespread catastrophe. The only law pollution attributed to him is the lingering resonance of grief in the waters where he once sang—a subtle, melancholy distortion in the spiritual atmosphere of that region.

**Relation with the Path of Celestial Beings and Daoism (仙道):** Xi Feng was never a formal cultivator of the Daoist path. His existence as a Yao spirit placed him outside the orthodox frameworks of transcendent cultivation. No conflict or alliance is recorded.

**Relation with the Path of the Gods (神道):** His love object, Shui Bi (水碧), was a water deity—a minor god of the rivers and seas. Their relationship brought him into a close and emotionally charged proximity with the path of the gods, but not as an adversary. He was a lover, not a challenger.

**Relation with the Buddhist Path (佛门):** No direct interaction with Buddhist institutions or figures is recorded.

**Relation with the Yaoguai and Mortal Society (妖族及凡俗政权):** As a natural-born Yao spirit, Xi Feng belonged to the lower reaches of the non-human world. His transformation was solitary, with no evidence of followers or a cult. In mortal folklore, he is remembered in fragmented songs about a voice that once calmed the waters, heard only in the darkest nights.

Xi Feng's current state is one of voluntary stillness. After Shui Bi's death, he chose to remain imprisoned at the bottom of the sea, his body slowly fusing with the coral around Shui Bi's preserved form. This is not a formal cosmic judgment—no Tian Ji (天罚) has been recorded as descending upon him. He was never powerful enough to trigger the full obliteration mechanism that awaits a Tian Mo (天魔). Instead, his end is a quiet, self-imposed extinction: a being who has chosen to stop moving, stop fighting, and simply dissolve into the ocean's silence. In the cosmic ledger, he leaves behind a small, sharp mark—not a scar on the fabric of reality, but a story preserved in the folk memory of southern fishing villages, of a monster who loved a goddess and sang his heart out in the dark, where no one could see him.

Lore Notes

Shui Bi (水碧)

A secondary water deity in the southern rivers, beloved by Xi Feng. She lived a century with him under his mortal identity Feng Xi before discovering the truth of his contract.

Chonglou (重楼)

The Sovereign of the Mo Realm, a being of immense power and authority. He personally offered Xi Feng the contract—a beautiful face in exchange for a voice.

Feng Xi (风溪)

The mortal identity Xi Feng adopted after receiving his new face. He lived under this name for a century with Shui Bi, unable to speak of his past.

FAQ

Why did Xi Feng stop singing?

He traded his voice to the Mo Sovereign Chonglou in exchange for a beautiful human face, so that he could approach the water goddess Shui Bi without being seen as a monster.

Did Shui Bi ever discover the truth?

Yes. After a century of life together under the name Feng Xi, she learned the nature of his contract. This revelation led to her death and Xi Feng's self-imprisonment at the bottom of the sea.

Is Xi Feng considered a dangerous Mo?

No. He never threatened the cosmic order or committed large-scale acts of violence. His tragedy is personal, not apocalyptic.

Where is Xi Feng now?

He remains at the bottom of the sea, fused with the coral surrounding Shui Bi's preserved form. He has chosen this stillness rather than seeking revenge or freedom.

Entry Guide

Picture a creature with a voice so beautiful that when he sang, the river would pause in its flow. Now picture that creature with a face so grotesque that every living thing that heard his song would flee before it finished. That was Xi Feng. He was not a conqueror or a rebel. He was a shy monster who fell in love with a goddess, and who spent years doing the only thing he knew how to do: sing to her from the shadows, where he could not be seen. Imagine her standing by the water, listening to a voice she could never find, and smiling at nothing. Now imagine the day when a dark king offered him a way out. A new face. A mortal name. In exchange for the one thing that made his existence bearable. That was the day Xi Feng stopped being a singer and began his long, slow silence.

If you have ever played or watched Chinese fantasy media, you might have heard the name Xi Feng as a footnote—a tragic love story in a larger epic about Mo Sovereigns and celestial conflicts. The simplified version is this: an ugly monster loved a water goddess, traded his voice for a beautiful face, and ended up imprisoned at the bottom of the sea. But that version misses the point. The real question is not what Xi Feng did. The question is what his story reveals about the logic of Mo in this universe. He is not a case of power-hungry corruption or spilled blood. His Mo is entirely rooted in self-perception. He did not become a monster to destroy; he became a monster because he could not bear the monster he already was. That distinction is everything. Let me walk you through it.

Xi Feng's transformation follows the obsession path, not the Primordial Chaotic Residue path. He was not corrupted by fragments of pre-creation disorder. His fall was a choice—a desperate, self-loathing choice that he made in full knowledge. The mechanism here is not a sudden contamination of the soul. It is a slow hollowing. He took a contract: his voice for a face. On the surface, it is a fair trade. But what he did not understand—or perhaps understood perfectly well and accepted anyway—was that his voice was the only thing that connected him to his own worth. Without it, he was not a creature with a beautiful face. He was a creature with an empty heart and a borrowed skin. The obsession that drives him is not the desire to be loved—it is the shame of being unworthy of love. That shame became the center of his existence, and everything else—the hundred years of happiness with Shui Bi, the slow agony of silence, the fury when the truth came out—was just the echo of that original wound.

Xi Feng's version of Wu Yun Chi Sheng (五蕴炽盛) is quiet. He does not hunger for blood. He does not crave fear. His corrupted senses are tuned to something more specific: the absence of sound. Imagine sitting at a table with someone you love, laughing and talking, but you cannot join the conversation. You can only nod and smile, while inside you feel a scream that has nowhere to go. Every song you ever sang is still in your throat, but the door is locked. That was his daily existence during the hundred years he lived as Feng Xi. He could taste Shui Bi's cooking, feel the warmth of her hand, see the light in her eyes. But he could not hear himself speak his own love. The satisfaction he got from her presence was real, but it came with a perpetual price tag: the awareness that she loved a lie. And that awareness grew, over time, into a deeper emptiness than the silence itself.

Xi Feng does not quite reach the stage of Yan Mo (魇魔). His obsession has not split off into a second voice that takes control of his body and does unspeakable things while he watches from behind a glass wall. But he has something almost as cruel: a persistent inner voice of self-loathing that never stops whispering. It tells him, in the quiet moments, that he is a fraud. That his love is a lie wrapped in borrowed skin. That Shui Bi would leave him the moment she knew. This voice is not a separate entity—it is the obsession itself, speaking in his own mind, using his own memories. When Shui Bi finally did discover the truth, the voice did not seize the wheel. It merely leaned back and said, "I told you so." And Xi Feng, already broken by the weight of his own deception, could not find a counter-argument. He did not need a separate monster to replace him. He was already the monster who had sold his best self for a dream.

Xi Feng never becomes a Tian Mo (天魔). He never fuses with Primordial Chaotic Residue, never becomes a walking violation of physical law. His power, such as it was, remained modest. The most devastating thing about his existence is not what he did in battle—it is the silence he left behind. When he was imprisoned at the bottom of the sea, he did not fight. He did not rage. He simply stopped. He chose to remain there, letting the ocean slowly claim him, fusing his form with the coral that grew around Shui Bi's preserved body. This is the gentlest fate a Mo can have in this universe: a quiet sinking into stillness, rather than the total annihilation of Tian Ji (天谴). But do not mistake gentleness for mercy. The cost was the same: a life that could have been beautiful, reduced to a slow, silent dissolution. He did not need to shake the foundations of the world to be a tragedy. He only needed to fade.

Xi Feng's story sits at the gentler edge of what this universe calls Mo. He did not break the celestial order. He did not drown a kingdom. But his existence asks a question that lingers long after his song fades: what does it mean to hate yourself so much that you would trade the one thing that makes you real for a mask? In this cosmos, the Daoist path demands you let go of all attachments. The Buddhist path demands you see through the illusion of the self. The Mo path is what happens when you refuse both—when you cling so hard to a desire, or a wound, or a shame, that you let it hollow you out from the inside. Xi Feng's downfall was not ambition or rage. It was a simple, aching belief: that he was not worth loving as himself. And that belief, like a slow poison, turned one of the most beautiful voices in the world into a ghost that haunts the shores of southern rivers, still singing in the dark, where no one can see.
